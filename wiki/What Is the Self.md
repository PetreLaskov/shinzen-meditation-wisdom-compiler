This 3-minute `@ShinzenVideos` Q&A gives Shinzen's simplest public
definition of ordinary self. Its durable value is not a new no-self doctrine
but a concise, quality-A source anchor: what people call self can be examined
as ongoing identification with visual thought, auditory thought, physical
body sensation, and emotional body sensation.

## Teaching Register
Short public-facing Q&A and guided definition. Shinzen does not begin with a
philosophical proof; he walks the questioner through what is already
available to notice: thoughts, body sensations, emotional body changes, and
the reflex to identify with them.

## Why This Talk Matters
The wiki already had stronger and deeper no-self sources. This talk matters

not first treated as a mysterious entity, but as a lived pattern of

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen asks the questioner to notice that thought is ongoing and
  can appear visually as memory, plan, and fantasy scenes or auditorily as
  inner dialogue and monologue.
- **S2**: He distinguishes body sensation into physical sensations and
  emotion-related changes in body sensation.
- **S3**: He says that when a thought, body state, or especially an emotional
  body state arises, people tend to identify with it as "me" or "mine."
- **S4**: His simplest working definition is that self is a moment-by-moment
  experience consisting of mind and body: mental image, mental talk,
  physicality, and emotionality.
- **S5**: He acknowledges that different legitimate perspectives on the word
  "self" exist, then offers this as his simplest practice-facing version.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S2**: The teaching move starts with direct ordinary noticing before
  any doctrine. Self-analysis begins from continuous thought and body
  experience, not metaphysical assertion.
- **S3**: Identification is made momentary and observable. The important
  event is the reflexive ownership of the current mind/body state.
- **S4**: The talk bridges broad mind/body language into Shinzen's sensory
  primitives without requiring the full no-self map.
- **S5**: The definition is framed as a perspective on "self," not a total
  theory of personhood.

## Practice Handles
- When "self" feels active, ask which components are present now: mental
  image, mental talk, physical body sensation, and emotional body sensation.
- Notice the ownership move as it happens: "this thought is me," "this is my
  body," or "this emotional sensation is especially me."
- Treat emotional body sensation as often more identity-loaded than neutral
  physical sensation, while still checking both.
- Use this as an entry to [[Inner Sensory System]] and [[No-Self And
  Personality]], not as a complete safety or clinical interpretation of
  self-loss.

## Summary
Shinzen answers the question "what is the self?" by first naming ordinary
sensory facts. The questioner has frequent thoughts, some visual and some
auditory; the body has physical sensation and emotion-related body changes.
He then points to the identification reflex: when a thought or body state
arises, a person tends to experience it as me or mine, with emotional body
states feeling especially self-relevant.

The talk's definition is deliberately simple. Self is a moment-by-moment
experience made of mind and body. Mind means mental image and mental talk;
body means physicality and emotionality. The ordinary experience of self is
identification with those streams as they arise.

## Weakest Claims
- "Continuous" or "incessant" thought and body sensation are useful public
  simplifications; actual experience may include quiet, blank, restful, or
  inaccessible periods.
- The tendency to identify with every arising mind/body state varies by
  person, context, and training.
- The definition is a practice-facing perspective, not a complete theory of
  selfhood, development, social identity, ethics, or psychology.

## Important Omissions
- No detailed instruction for deconstructing self into components or working
  with self-arising over time.
- No distinction between no-self insight and depersonalization,
  derealization, dissociation, depression, trauma shutdown, psychosis risk,
  or other clinical concerns.
- No account of personality re-arising, behavior change, service, ordinary
  agency, or relational accountability.
- No Source, Zero, Flow, Gone, or impermanence-side explanation of how
  self-experience changes under deeper practice.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk grants many legitimate perspectives on self while selecting a
  simple sensory-identification definition. Future use should preserve that
  local scope.
- The body side includes both physicality and emotionality, while the
  technical inner-system model usually narrows the self-practice target to
  emotional body sensation plus Image/Talk. The broader public definition and
  the narrower practice target should not be collapsed.
- Emotional sensation is presented as especially self-like, but the source
  does not explain when strong emotional identification should be turned
  toward, turned away from, supported clinically, or left alone.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "self is moment-by-moment experience" to invalidate personal
  history, relationships, trauma, agency, ethical responsibility, or social
  identity.
- Do not tell someone with frightening self-loss, blankness, derealization,
  or dissociation that they have merely understood Shinzen's definition of
  self.
- Do not treat every emotional body sensation as ego pathology. In this
  source, emotionality is a component of ordinary self-experience and a
  potential practice object.
- Do not use the broad body/mind definition to erase the more precise
  distinctions among physical sensation, emotional Feel, Image, and Talk.
