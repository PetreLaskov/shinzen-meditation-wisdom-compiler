## Teaching Register
This is retreat-ending advanced Dharma teaching with a service exhortation. Shinzen uses the cloth-bag monk image to translate late-stage realization into ordinary availability, gifts, and teaching without specialness.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk prevents the ox-herding map from ending at emptiness, repose, or ordinary appearance. Shinzen says the final cause of practice is the automatic call to serve others, expressed through ordinary presence, practice effects, coherent explanation, and sometimes explicit teaching.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: The final picture, entering the marketplace with hand outstretched, presents the end of practice as ordinary, approachable service rather than visible specialness.
- **S2**: Shinzen identifies the final cause of practice with *daoxin* or bodhicitta, an intense call to serve fellow beings that follows from tangibly knowing that one's Source is everyone's Source.
- **S3**: Marketplace service can be subtle: the effects of practice may teach through presence, even without explicit Dharma talk.
- **S4**: Coherent, logical, reasonable description of one's practice is itself a gift when someone asks.
- **S5**: Some practitioners may eventually teach one or more people; Shinzen reverses the cliche by saying that when the teacher is ready, students appear.

A practitioner or guide can use this final picture as a life-integration check: does practice make a person more available, ordinary, approachable, and capable of giving something useful without needing to appear special?

The talk also gives three service channels: practice effects communicated through presence, coherent description when asked, and eventual teaching for some people when students appear. ## Summary
Shinzen asks why people practice. The beginning may involve curiosity, desperation, wanting relief, wanting a high, or wanting to find out who one really is. The final cause, in his reading of the last ox-herding picture, is entering the marketplace with hand outstretched.

He illustrates this with the cloth-bag monk, an ordinary and approachable figure who gave gifts, played with children, and did not appear special unless someone knew what to look for. Shinzen contrasts this Chinese image with more archetypal or godlike formulations of enlightenment: here the enlightened person is ordinary, available, and carrying gifts.

The final claim is service. Shinzen says practice ends in bodhicitta, the intense call to serve fellow beings, because Source is known as shared in direct perception. Retreatants leave for the marketplace with gifts: the effects of practice, coherent explanation if asked, and for some, actual teaching when students appear.
