## Teaching Register
This is comparative instruction. Shinzen is not selling one method as best; he names attention-coverage strategies, gives body-based examples, and compares their practical advantages and difficulties.

## Why This Talk Matters
Gate 1 already has entry aims, CCE assumptions, reaction recycling, and path-fit posture. This talk adds a local method-choice variable: when a practitioner picks a focus method, they are also picking a way attention covers the field. That can be natural and responsive, exhaustive and detail-building, or broad and integrating.

## Key Claims / Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: Shinzen classifies focus methods by attention-coverage strategy: free-floating, systematic inventory, or even coverage.
- **S2**: Noting body locations is his body example of free-floating attention: attention goes where sensation pulls, with some possible control but no fixed route.
- **S3**: Body scanning, part-by-part sampling, and continuous sweeping are examples of systematic inventory, including subtle surface, through-body, directional, or spiral inventories.
- **S4**: Holding all body locations simultaneously is his body example of even coverage.
- **S5**: Systematic inventory helps ensure coverage and develops sensitization and detail, but practitioners may get lost when little is detected unless absence or rest can be detected as a presence.
- **S6**: Even coverage integrates, gives the big picture, trains expansive concentration, and prepares large-field practice, but it is challenging because contact with pieces can be lost; Shinzen's practical instruction is to hold the field as broadly as possible without straining.
- **S7**: Free-floating attention is natural, easier, immediately workable for many people, and often more enjoyable; Shinzen does not name a specific disadvantage in the talk.
- **S8**: Focus In, Focus Out, and Focus on Rest are generally free-floating among categories, though zooming out can create brief moments of even coverage.
- **S9**: Focus on Flow can become maximal even coverage when flow is present across somatic, visual, and auditory spaces and the practitioner zooms out to all sensory spaces at once.
- **S10**: Focus on Positive may function as free-floating or even coverage across one, two, or all three inner spaces.
- **S11**: The fallback rule is divide-and-conquer: if a large-field or even-coverage approach does not work, try smaller fields through free-floating or systematic inventory, then build toward coverage.

## Practice Handles
- Use free-floating when naturalness, ease, immediacy, and responsiveness matter more than exhaustive coverage.
- Use systematic inventory when the practice needs all areas sampled, detail sharpened, or subtle sensation sensitized.
- When inventory finds little sensation, include rest or absence if that is the chosen category and it is actually detectable.
- Use even coverage when the aim is broad integration, big-picture contact, or expansive concentration.
- Keep even coverage broad without strain; loss of contact with parts of the field is a signal to simplify.
- Treat zooming out as a temporary even-coverage move inside otherwise free-floating Focus In, Focus Out, or Focus on Rest.
- If large-field practice is too much, divide-and-conquer by narrowing to smaller fields or a systematic inventory, then gradually rebuild coverage.

## Summary
Shinzen says focus methods can be compared by how attention covers a field. Free-floating methods let attention go where it naturally goes while staying aware of that movement. Systematic inventory scrolls through the field and samples what is present. Even coverage attempts to hold all relevant areas at once. He first explains the taxonomy through body-location practice: free-floating attention moves to whatever body area calls; inventory scans or sweeps the body; even coverage holds all body locations together.

The tradeoffs are practical. Inventory ensures coverage and builds detail, but it can frustrate practitioners when sensation is not obvious. Even coverage gives the big picture, trains broad or expansive concentration, and supports integration, but it can strain or lose contact with parts of the field. Free-floating is easier, more natural, and often more enjoyable, and Shinzen does not name a clear disadvantage in this brief comparison.

He then maps the strategies onto Shinzen methods. Focus In, Focus Out, and Focus on Rest are generally free-floating among their categories, though zooming out can briefly create even coverage. Focus on Flow can become extremely broad when all sensory spaces are covered at once. Focus on Positive can either float among positive inner systems or hold one, two, or three inner spaces together. The closing rule is divide-and-conquer: if broad coverage fails, work smaller, often through free-floating or systematic inventory, then build back toward coverage.

## Weakest Claims

- The integration-to-annihilation-to-Source language is asserted, not explained or operationalized in this talk.
- The broad concentration discussion invokes Sasaki Roshi's contractive/expansive distinction but gives no independent source or detailed lineage context.
- The method mappings are brief and should not replace full technique instructions.

## Important Omissions
- No safety criteria are given for strain, dissociation, dullness, panic, trauma activation, medical pain, or when body scanning should stop or soften.
- No dosage or sequencing guidance is supplied for moving from inventory to even coverage.
- The talk does not define how much contact must be maintained for even coverage to be workable.
- It does not explain when free-floating is wise responsiveness versus unexamined drifting.
- It does not specify how guides should adapt coverage strategy for beginners, high-intensity states, fatigue, or neurodivergent attention patterns.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen says free-floating is natural and names no disadvantage, while the broader wiki already treats method choice as needing outcome, safety, and behavior criteria.
- Even coverage is praised for integration and expansive concentration but simultaneously described as difficult and prone to losing contact with pieces.
- Systematic inventory is praised for detail and coverage, yet it may become frustrating or confusing when sensation is subtle or absent.
- The talk makes large-field integration sound developmentally powerful, but its divide-and-conquer fallback prevents broad practice from becoming a hierarchy.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not turn even coverage into a superiority claim. In this talk, broad coverage is useful only when it can be held without strain and without losing too much contact.
- Do not use "no disadvantage" for free-floating as permission for vague drifting or avoidance.
- Do not use systematic inventory to force sensation where none is detectable; absence or rest must be part of the actual focus category.
- Do not treat Source or annihilation language here as evidence that a practitioner should push toward dissolution.
