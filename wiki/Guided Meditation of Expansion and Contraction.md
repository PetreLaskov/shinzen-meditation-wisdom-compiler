## Summary
Shinzen opens with posture as a living example of being between expansion and
contraction, then leads a series of surface-detection exercises. The first is
the simplest: notice any increase or decrease in intensity, speed, frequency,
or spatial size. The second turns pressure into inward, outward, bidirectional,
or absent force. The third reframes scattering, flooding, and gripping as
impersonal space-patterns rather than personal failure.

The fourth exercise is subtler. Shinzen acknowledges that getting centered can
be comfortable and ordering, but points toward freedom from the need to occupy
a fixed center. Being pulled off center is interpreted as expansion, while the
center's disappearance or collapse is interpreted as contraction. He admits
that this language may feel opaque and invites long-horizon exploration rather
than immediate mastery.

The final section links surface practice to possible deep Expansion-
Contraction: an effortless spreading, collapsing, bothness, or stillness
around and within sensory-motor events. The talk becomes most safety-relevant
here, because Shinzen repeatedly says this is a possibility, not a certainty,
and that it may remain merely an idea. If deep polarity is not available, the
practitioner is told to continue with surface patterns.
