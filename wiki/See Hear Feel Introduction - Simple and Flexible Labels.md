This unit covers pages 24-35 of Shinzen Young's *See Hear Feel: An Introduction*, using a mechanical Markdown extraction from the source PDF. It teaches SHF's compact modality labels, how to handle simultaneous modalities, why label meanings depend on the active focus range, and when labels or noting itself may be dropped.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: SHF classifies sensory events into three modalities in the standard order See, Hear, Feel: visual experience, auditory experience, and body or somatic experience.
- **S2**: With an unrestricted focus range, See can include physical sight, mental image, visual rest, visual flow, and visual spaciousness; Hear can include physical sound, mental talk, auditory rest, auditory flow, and auditory spaciousness; Feel can include physical body sensation, emotional body sensation, smell and taste, body rest, body flow, and body spaciousness.
- **S3**: Smell and taste are placed under Feel for symmetry and simplicity because they are experienced somatically through the mouth, nose, tongue, and related body sites.
- **S4**: When two or three modalities arise simultaneously or nearly simultaneously, practitioners may use exclusive emphasis by choosing one target, or inclusive emphasis by focusing on the co-occurring components with labels such as See Hear, See Feel, Hear Feel, or All.
- **S5**: In Unified Mindfulness, emphasis is a technical term for the exclusive-versus-inclusive choice, not a generic name for range options, label options, or re-noting options.
- **S6**: Shinzen reframes intense emotional distress as a possible All experience when mental image, mental talk, and emotional body sensation arise together, and he links this to traditional models in which stream entry involves total self-arising in total mindfulness.
- **S7**: Flexible labeling has one central rule: the meaning of the label depends on the focus range. See always means something visual, Hear something auditory, and Feel something somatic, but each may refer broadly or narrowly depending on the selected range.
- **S8**: Within restricted ranges, the same simple labels can stand for more specific categories: in Focus on Rest, See/Hear/Feel mean visual, auditory, and body rest; in Focus In, they mean mental image, mental talk, and emotional body sensation; in Focus Out, they mean physical sight, sound, and body sensation; the same principle applies to Flow and Spaciousness.
- **S9**: Labels are not necessary when direct awareness can maintain the acknowledge-focus rhythm, but dropping labels is not the same as dropping noting.
- **S10**: Common objections to labeling include agitation, reification, mental associations, performance anxiety, effort, distance between observer and observed, and interference with the observed event.
- **S11**: The quick fix for label-related problems is to drop labels; the deep fix is persistence until labeling becomes automatic and less agitating.
- **S12**: Inclination to drop intentional noting may indicate a useful transition from SHF to Do Nothing or Nurture Positive, or it may indicate sloppiness; the source leaves that diagnostic distinction under-specified.

## Summary
The unit first makes SHF simple. Instead of requiring the older bisyllabic grid labels at all times, it trains three root modality labels: See, Hear, and Feel. These cover visual, auditory, and somatic experience broadly, including physical, mental, rest, flow, and spaciousness examples in each modality. Smell and taste are placed under Feel as a simplicity convention, continuing the Basic Mindfulness preference for a small usable taxonomy over a perfect natural-language ontology.

The unit then handles co-occurrence. When modalities arise together, a practitioner can keep practice simple by choosing one target, called exclusive emphasis, or can note the co-occurring components, called inclusive emphasis. Inclusive labels use the triangle edges and interior: See Hear, See Feel, Hear Feel, and All. The source sharply narrows the technical term emphasis to this exclusive/inclusive choice rather than using it for every kind of practice option.

The largest model delta is flexible labeling. The meaning of See, Hear, and Feel depends on the chosen focus range. If the practitioner is working only with rest, See means visual rest; if working with inner activity, Hear means mental talk; if working with breath or body scan, Feel may be enough because the range is already somatic. This preserves the old grid's precision while letting modern SHF use a small rhythmic label set.

The final pages keep labels subordinate to noting. If labels agitate, reify, create performance pressure, or interfere with contact, the quick fix is to drop them, provided direct awareness still maintains the acknowledge-focus sequence. The deep fix is to keep practicing until labeling becomes automatic. The unit also allows that a wish to drop intentional noting may signal a transition to Do Nothing or Nurture Positive, while warning that it may also be ordinary sloppiness.

## Source Frame
This is a technical vocabulary and interface unit. It wants the reader to see SHF as simpler than the older grid without losing precision: simple labels work because the focus range silently supplies the missing specifier. It also wants to lower the stakes around labeling by treating labels as optional supports, not as the essence of mindful awareness.

The strongest claims are procedural: the label sets, the exclusive/inclusive distinction, the technical use of emphasis, and the range-dependent meaning rule. The weaker claims are attainment and habituation claims: the stream-entry framing for All experiences and the expectation that label objections tend to dissolve with time are presented instructionally rather than supported with external evidence.

The unit preserves the old/new-system tension. The old grid terms remain semantically available, but the modern interface often drops words like In, Out, Rest, Flow, or Spaciousness when the focus range already makes them clear.

## Weakest Claims
- The stream-entry comparison around total self-arising in total mindfulness should remain source-attributed; this unit does not establish an attainment criterion.
- The claim that labeling objections tend to resolve with time may be true for many practitioners, but the unit does not specify when persistence is unhelpful, dysregulating, or unsafe.
- The diagnostic distinction between a useful transition out of SHF and simple sloppiness is named but not operationalized.
- Flexible labeling is elegant, but it can become ambiguous if the practitioner or coach has not made the focus range explicit.

## Important Omissions
- The unit does not define Spaciousness in enough detail to warrant a standalone page or to distinguish it fully from Rest, Flow, absence, openness, or thinness.
- It does not give safety criteria for intense All experiences, severe distress, performance anxiety, dissociation, or compulsive labeling.
- It does not specify how a coach should decide between exclusive emphasis, inclusive emphasis, dropping labels, persistence, or switching techniques.
- It does not give detailed instructions for Do Nothing or Nurture Positive, which are only named as possible alternatives to SHF.
- It does not explain the beginner starting ranges; Unit 04 carries that next procedural layer.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The simplified label set reduces cognitive load, but it also makes correct interpretation depend on a background focus range that may be implicit or forgotten.
- Labels are optional, yet the unit also praises persistence through label-related agitation; the practical boundary between helpful training friction and counterproductive strain remains open.
- Inclusive emphasis makes co-occurrence explicit, while exclusive emphasis deliberately ignores some simultaneous material; both are framed as productive, so the source prioritizes workable practice over exhaustive registration.
- The All reframing turns distress into a possible self-arising practice opportunity, but without support criteria this can be overused in situations that need grounding, relational help, or clinical care.
