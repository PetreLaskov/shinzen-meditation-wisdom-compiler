## Teaching Register
Short pragmatic instruction with autobiographical framing. Shinzen starts
from his own experience doing intellectual work and gives a portable handle
for householder practice, so the talk is strongest as practice routing and
weakest as a general equivalence claim about practice duration.

## Why This Talk Matters
The wiki already tracked micro-hits and practice in life. This talk adds the
missing high-demand case: when a task needs the whole mind and technique
cannot be held during it, stop afterward, start instantly, and treat the
activation generated by the task as complete sensory experience.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen says that, for him, intellectual work, writing, and figuring things out do not support a formal technique while the work is happening, but they can be peppered with brief practice contacts.
- **S2**: The central instruction is "stop on a dime": interrupt the activity even when the momentum to continue is strong.
- **S3**: When the practitioner stops, peaceful formlessness may occur, but more commonly past/future material erupts in See/Hear/Feel; that eruption is the practice object.
- **S4**: Even thirty seconds or a few minutes can function as retroactive meditation if the practitioner brings full intentionality and complete experience to whatever was stimulated by the prior activity.
- **S5**: "Start on a dime" means beginning a technique immediately, without a preliminary period of settling or hesitation; the transcript gives examples involving See, Feel, Rest, and Gone, but the exact label sequence is caption-fragile.
- **S6**: Shinzen uses the stimulus-response gap to argue for instant technique implementation in daily life: if mindfulness is not present in the first two or three seconds, reactive habit can take over.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S3**: The talk protects task integrity. It does not demand continuous explicit technique during work that actually needs cognitive resources.
- **S2-S5**: The practice handle is procedural: stop the action, let the stirred-up material appear, start a technique immediately, and complete the available sensory experience.
- **S3-S4**: The target is not the past activity itself but the present sensory activation it left behind, especially the backward pull into what happened and the forward pull into what needs to happen next.
- **S5-S6**: The "start on a dime" move turns micro-hits into reaction-time training rather than vague good intentions to practice later.
- **S4**: The claim that a short, high-quality practice period can have the same overall growth effect as meditating during the prior activity is important but should stay Shinzen-attributed and capacity-bounded.

## Practice Handles
- During demanding intellectual or practical work, do not force a formal technique if doing so would degrade the task.
- Use an external or remembered cue to stop the activity periodically, only when stopping is ordinary-life safe.
- At the stop, let the pull of what just happened and what comes next show itself as current See/Hear/Feel activation.
- Start the chosen technique immediately. Do not wait for a long settling period before noting, feeling, tracking Rest/Gone, or otherwise contacting what is present.
- Treat even thirty seconds or two minutes as worthwhile only if it has real concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity.
- Do not use retroactive meditation to justify unsafe task interruption, divided attention during safety-critical work, procrastination, or a fantasy that low-quality micro-practice replaces formal practice.

## Summary
Shinzen opens with the problem of intellectual work. In his own experience,
writing and figuring things out do not allow him to implement a formal
technique while the work is underway. The alternative is to pepper the
activity with brief practice-in-life moments.

He calls the first move "stop on a dime": stop the activity even when the
momentum to continue is strong. When the activity stops, the practitioner
may not find peace. More often, the energies created by the last hour or two
of ordinary activity erupt as past/future material in See, Hear, and Feel.
Retroactive meditation means giving those few moments full practice quality
so the activation stirred by the prior activity is completed now.

The second move is "start on a dime": begin a technique without hesitation.
Shinzen links this to the gap between stimulus and response. In ordinary
life, a reaction may begin within a few seconds; if mindfulness is not
available there, habitual reactivity can take over. The practical training
therefore is not just to practice eventually, but to be ready to implement a
method immediately when the gap opens.

## Source Frame
This is primary evidence for Shinzen's practice-in-life idiolect and his
householder method for cognitively demanding work. It is not evidence that
every practitioner can replace long formal practice with brief stops, or
that all tasks can safely be interrupted or practiced through. The talk's
aim is to keep practice available when continuous technique is unrealistic:
preserve task competence during the activity, then practice intensely with
the present activation the activity leaves behind.

## Weakest Claims
- The suggestion that a few high-quality minutes can have the same overall growth effect as meditating during the whole prior activity is plausible inside Shinzen's practice model but not empirically demonstrated here.
- The handle depends on a practitioner being able to bring real CCE quickly; the talk does not specify readiness, minimum skill, or failure modes.
- The stimulus-response gap framing is practice-useful but does not by itself establish a general psychological mechanism for all reactions.
- The transcript is auto-captioned and garbles several references and label examples, so phrase-level wording should not be quoted without audiovisual spot check.

## Important Omissions
- No criteria for how often to interrupt work, how long to practice, or how to choose the technique.
- No safety protocol for driving, machinery, caregiving, surgery, professional duties, conflict, or other tasks where stopping or divided attention may be unsafe.
- No guidance for practitioners whose past/future activation becomes panic, trauma flooding, dissociation, obsession, mania-like acceleration, or functional impairment.
- No discussion of how retroactive meditation relates to formal daily sitting, sleep, rest, deadlines, or ordinary productivity boundaries.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Mindfulness is present-time practice, yet the talk deliberately calls the method "retroactive"; the resolution is that the practitioner works with present activation left by past activity, not with the past itself.
- The instruction respects tasks that cannot carry technique, but also asks for instant practice when the stop occurs. Forcing either side into all situations would distort the teaching.
- The equivalence claim can motivate high-quality micro-practice, but it can also be misused to avoid sustained formal practice or competent support.
- Stopping on a dime trains freedom from momentum, but interruption itself can become avoidance if the task or responsibility is being escaped rather than completed.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "retroactive meditation" to rationalize multitasking during work that needs undivided attention.
- Do not interrupt safety-critical activity to practice unless ordinary safety and responsibility are already handled.
- Do not treat two minutes of vague practice as equal to hours of formal practice.
- Do not turn past/future eruption into rumination, productivity analysis, or self-criticism; the object is current sensory activation.
- Do not use the "lizard brain" or cockpit metaphor as a shame frame for normal reaction speed or trauma-linked response.
