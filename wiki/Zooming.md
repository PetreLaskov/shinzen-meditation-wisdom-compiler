Zooming is a spatial-scope option inside [[Noting]]. After a sensory event has been acknowledged and persists into the focus phase, attention may be left uncontrolled, narrowed, widened, or held both locally and globally.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: The default is not to control spatial attention; zooming is an optional support when changing spatial scope helps CCE.
- **Why this matters**: Zooming gives practitioners and guides a concrete way to regulate intense sensations without immediately changing methods.
- **Key tensions**: Zooming can reduce pressure, clarify subtle spread, or support purification; it can also become strain, overwhelm, disappearance-chasing, or unsafe somatic intensity if used as a mandate.
- **Source posture**: Probable as Shinzen's practice option from SHF and the Part 2 oral instruction; speculative where the talk explains suffering through below-threshold spread and irreversible purification.

## Model
The SHF Noting mini-manual defines zooming compactly: attention can shrink into one area, spread across the whole event, extend beyond the event to find openness around it, or combine local intensity with broad scope ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S7).

The Part 2 labeling talk supplies the oral mechanics. Acknowledgement identifies that some sensory event is present. If the event vanishes immediately, the branch is [[Gone]]. If it persists, there is a focus phase of several seconds, and the event has some spatial extent even if its borders are vague ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S1).

Shinzen's default is important: within the spatial scope of the acknowledged event, the fallback is not to control attention ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S2). Zooming is therefore not the standard form of Noting. It is an option when spatial control seems appropriate or useful.

The option has three main forms:

- **Zoom in**: intentionally focus on a small part of the phenomenon, often a weaker perimeter region around pain or emotional intensity, so the practitioner can open to that small volume more completely ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S3).
- **Zoom out**: spread attention broadly across the whole sensation or as much of it as is detectable; Shinzen says this can reduce intensity by giving the event more volume ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S4).
- **Zoom in and out simultaneously**: focus on the most intense local point while spreading awareness as broadly as possible through the body, especially with body sensation ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S5).

This is related to, but not identical with, [[Focus Coverage Strategies]]. Coverage strategy asks whether attention floats, inventories, or holds a field broadly. Zooming is a local option used inside an acknowledged event or selected field. Zooming out may create even-coverage moments, but the practice question is more specific: what spatial scope should attention use during this focus phase?

## Purification Frame
The talk's most consequential claim is that suffering may be less about the obvious local intensity and more about resistance to subtle spread through the body, including spread below conscious detection ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S6).

Simultaneous local-global zooming is said to "grease the rails" for that spread to occur without resistance, even when the practitioner cannot consciously detect the spread ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S7). With time, Shinzen says the process can take on a splash-ripple-radiate-release rhythm and the taste of purification, as though a quantum of potential suffering has been released ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S8).

This belongs under [[Insight and Purification]] as a practice image, not as a measured mechanism. The durable handle is that zooming can change the relationship between local intensity, whole-body spread, and resistance.

## Routing
Use zooming questions after a focus range and technique are already clear:

- Has the event vanished immediately, or does it persist long enough for a focus phase?
- Is simple non-controlled focus already working?
- Is the whole event too intense, while a small local region is workable?
- Would broadening attention over the whole event reduce pressure or improve integration?
- Is simultaneous local-global zooming appropriate, or would it overpressure the practitioner?
- Is the report one of increased clarity and release, or of overwhelm, loss of control, strain, or dissociation-like drift?
- Does the practice need a different Noting option, a narrower range, grounding, rest, Do Nothing, stopping, guidance, or outside support?

## Boundaries
Zooming is not required. Shinzen explicitly says never using the simultaneous option is fine and reiterates that the default is non-control ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S10).

Zooming is not a clinical protocol. The talk uses intense body sensations, pain, emotional intensity, shaking, overwhelm, and loss-of-control impressions, but it does not give criteria for trauma activation, panic, medical pain, dissociation, mania, psychosis risk, neurological symptoms, or when to stop and seek support.

Zooming in and out at the same time is not simply "more advanced." It deliberately contacts a local intensity while widening through the whole body. That can support release in Shinzen's frame, but it can also magnify somatic intensity. A guide should not infer that overwhelm is automatically purification.

The purification explanation should remain source-attributed. The raindrop-ripple analogy helps convey irreversible release, but it is not evidence that psychological suffering follows the physics of energy dissipation.

## Implications
Zooming makes Noting more precise without changing its core. The act remains acknowledge and focus; zooming only adjusts how spatial attention behaves during the focus phase.

It also creates a safety-relevant distinction between smaller contact and wider contact. For intense experience, narrowing can make contact possible; widening can distribute pressure; local-global practice can test whether resistance to spread is the active suffering mechanism. Those are options to try, not proof that intensity should be pushed.
