## Practice Handles
For an interpreter, this talk adds a "no simpler, but not colder than needed" rule: preserve Shinzen's distinctions long enough to see what they protect, then still ask whether their delivery creates avoidable burden.

For a guide, the talk adds teacher-fit questions:

- Is the complexity serving real phenomenological discrimination or becoming terminology load?
- Does a sharp style help this student clarify experience, or does it weaken rapport and trust?
- Is irreverence demystifying inflated tradition claims, or is it alienating the student from useful forms and lineage context?
- Is the practitioner being invited to test the teaching directly, or being asked to accept the teacher's self-defense?

For a practitioner, the talk supports evaluating Shinzen's material by use-value rather than by personality fit alone. Disliking the style is not a practice failure; liking the precision is not proof that the method is complete or safe for every context.

## Summary
Shinzen lists three reasons people say he is a poor teacher. First, they find his teaching complex and subtle, full of definitions, categories, and relationships. He defends this by saying he is trying to build a broad framework for contemplative and mystical traditions, and that wide applicability can require a certain amount of unavoidable complexity.

Second, he says people experience him as cold and intellectual. He frames this as a personality trait and draws on the image of sharp compassion: a scalpel-like mode of help rather than warm emotional expressiveness.

Third, he says some people dislike his irreverence toward Buddhism. He is comfortable criticizing his own roots and sometimes makes jokes that others find excessive. The closing posture is self-deprecating but not self-condemning: he accepts that he can seem complex, cold, and irreverent, while inviting listeners to see whether the teaching still has value.
