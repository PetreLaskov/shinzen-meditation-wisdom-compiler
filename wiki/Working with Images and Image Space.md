## Teaching Register
Q&A-style phenomenological instruction with a comparative Vajrayana bridge. Shinzen responds to a report about subtle body imagery, then widens the answer into a practice map for how images help construct body-self, location, memory, fantasy, myth, and visualization practice.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk makes image space more precise than the generic label "See In." It gives future agents a way to distinguish at least three visual locations that can be separately clarified, equanimized, or integrated into Flow: the image of the body/self, the image of the surrounding ambience, and the central memory-plan-fantasy screen.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: The perception "I am body" is not produced only by body sensation; it often includes a subtle mental picture of the body's outline, facial expression, clothing, or self-image that body sensations seem to fill.
- **S2**: Hating the presence of subtle self-image interferes with no-self practice; loving or affirming the image "to death" through concentration, clarity, equanimity, and sometimes direct focus lets image and sensation break into integrated Flow.
- **S3**: Ambient imagery gives the felt sense of where one is; if eyes are closed, sound, source imagery, and memory images of the place help locate the practitioner.
- **S4**: Central image space carries memory, planning, fantasy, and potentially deeper mythic or archetypal imagery, functioning as a portal into stored images of the world.
- **S5**: Vajrayana visualization can be read as insight practice because it deliberately replaces ordinary self-image, central imagery, and ambient imagery with archetypal or mandala forms to reveal how who-and-where perception is constructed.
- **S6**: In flashback-like traumatic memory, Shinzen says present ambient imagery may disappear and be replaced by memory imagery; training people to detect that replacement can be insightful and liberating.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: The body-self is made partly by visual image, not only Feel or Touch, so no-self practice must sometimes include body/self imagery.
- **S2**: The no-self instruction is not to attack self-image but to complete it through CCE until image and sensation become fluid.
- **S3-S4**: Image space has different functional locations; "image" is not one undifferentiated inner screen.
- **S5**: The Vajrayana bridge preserves visualization as a way to study construction of perception, not as possession, fantasy inflation, or a break with reality.
- **S6**: The trauma-adjacent model is practice-relevant but clinically under-specified, so it should sharpen sensory discrimination without becoming a treatment claim.

## Practice Handles
- When the sense "I am the body" is active, check both body sensations and the subtle body/self image they seem to fill.
- Do not resent the subtle image as an obstacle to no-self. Work with the image, the sensations, or both together until they become more fluid.
- When eyes are closed, notice whether ambient place imagery and source imagery help create the sense of location.
- Distinguish central memory-plan-fantasy images from ambient imagery; watch how one can displace the other.
- Treat visualization practices as controlled construction and deconstruction of body/self, central object, and ambience imagery, not as proof of external mythic reality.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by saying that body-self perception is not only body sensation, even though body sensation is primary. It often includes a subtle image of the body's outline, face, clothing, or self-presentation. Body sensations seem to fill that visual container. The sense that the body is a thing comes from both bodily nebulosity and viscosity and image-based nebulosity and viscosity.

The practice move is counterintuitive. If a practitioner hates the body/self image because it seems to block no-self, that aversion becomes a problem. Shinzen instead recommends focusing on the image, the sensations, or both, bringing clarity and equanimity until image and sensation break into one integrated Flow and the subtle "I am the body" sense softens.

He then maps two more image-space locations. Ambient imagery supports the felt sense of being somewhere, especially with eyes closed when sound, source images, and remembered place images help locate the person. Central image space carries memory, planning, fantasy, and deeper archetypal or mythic material. Shinzen reads Vajrayana visualization as deliberately replacing ordinary body/self image, central image, and ambient image with mythic forms in order to understand how who-and-where perception is built. He closes with a trauma-adjacent example: in flashback-like memory, present ambient imagery can disappear and memory imagery can occupy its place.

## Weakest Claims
- The flashback explanation is a Shinzen practice interpretation and should not be treated as a complete trauma, memory, or dissociation model.
- The archetypal and mythic depth of central image space is asserted briefly and not developed enough to ground a broader comparative or metaphysical page.
- The Vajrayana account is strong as Shinzen's retrospective teaching interpretation, not as a full history of Vajrayana practice.

## Important Omissions
- The talk does not say when image-space practice is destabilizing, obsessive, dissociative, or better routed to grounding or support.
- It gives no clinical criteria for traumatic flashbacks, PTSD, dissociation, psychosis risk, or when professional trauma care is needed.
- It does not specify dosage, sequencing, or how beginners should distinguish faint body/self imagery from imagination or conceptual inference.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The instruction to focus on subtle body/self imagery can help no-self practice, but it can also increase fixation if done with aversion, status-seeking, or overcontrol.
- Vajrayana visualization is presented as insight into construction, while its mythic imagery could be misread as metaphysical proof or identity inflation.
- The flashback model makes a useful sensory distinction, but it risks overconfidence if generalized into trauma treatment without safeguards.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "love the image to death" to force exposure to overwhelming trauma imagery or dissociative body-image material.
- Do not treat mythic or archetypal images as attainment, literal entities, or license for grandiose identity.
- Do not treat ambient-image replacement as a clinical explanation sufficient to handle flashbacks without trauma-informed support when needed.
