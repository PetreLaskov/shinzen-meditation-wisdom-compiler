## Key Claims
- **S1**: Unified Mindfulness defines mindful awareness as concentration power, sensory clarity, and equanimity working together, while acknowledging the system's strength as conceptual clarity and comprehensiveness and its weak point as complexity.
- **S2**: Concentration power is the ability to focus on what is relevant, sensory clarity is the ability to track what is being experienced in the moment, and equanimity is the ability to let sensory experience come and go without push and pull.
- **S3**: Shinzen treats these as trainable internal skills for processing outer physical experience and inner thought-emotion experience.
- **S4**: The unit links lack of concentration to failed performance, lack of clarity to flooding and overwhelm, and lack of equanimity to reduced satisfaction from pleasure or added suffering.
- **S5**: A label is a word naming what is being experienced; mental labels are thought internally, spoken labels are said aloud, and both should use a low, gentle, matter-of-fact equanimity voice.
- **S6**: The unit maps label supports onto CCE: label pacing primarily aids concentration, label wording primarily aids sensory clarity, and label tone primarily aids equanimity.
- **S7**: Typical label pacing ranges from about one label per second to one label every four or five seconds, may repeat the same object, need not be metronomic, may synchronize with breath, and should avoid being too racy or too spacey.
- **S8**: Spoken labels are useful when concentration is difficult and in coaching because tone, pacing, and wording reveal how guidance may help.
- **S9**: Strongly spoken labels are a recovery option: attention is divided between the labeled experience and the label stream, so losing the stream exposes lost mindfulness and restoring it reestablishes a basic CCE level.
- **S10**: Labels themselves are auditory events but should not be included in the tracked field; the source frames them as scaffolding for observing other sensory events.
- **S11**: To note a sensory event means to acknowledge its presence and briefly focus on it; labels aid Noting, labels can be dropped when direct awareness maintains the rhythm, and Noting is one way to develop mindful awareness.
- **S12**: When acknowledged events vanish too quickly for a focus phase, this is not a practice failure but an opportunity to detect impermanence or Gone.
- **S13**: If observing alters experience, such as making mental talk vanish or become restful, the alteration should be observed like any other phenomenon rather than dismissed as an artifact.

## Summary
The unit restates Unified Mindfulness's central definition of mindful awareness as concentration power, sensory clarity, and equanimity working together. It adds a practical formulation for each member of the triad: relevance-based focus, moment-by-moment tracking, and allowing sensory events to come and go without push and pull. It also names the system's teaching tradeoff: conceptual clarity and comprehensiveness are strengths, while complexity and many focus options can overwhelm beginners.

The main procedural contribution is labeling. Labels are words that name present experience and can be mental, spoken, whispered, or mouthed. Their mechanics are intentionally tied to CCE: pacing supports continuity of concentration, wording supports clarity about what is present, and a gentle matter-of-fact voice supports equanimity. Strongly spoken labels are not presented as a higher form of practice; they are a recovery tool that creates a feedback loop when attention is getting spaced out or caught up.

The unit then clarifies the hierarchy between mindful awareness, Noting, and labels. Noting typically alternates acknowledgement and brief focused contact. Labeling is optional scaffolding for that sequence, not the essence of practice. If events vanish too quickly for the focus phase, or if the act of observing changes the event, the source treats that as valid sensory material: vanishing can become Gone practice and observation-induced rest can become Rest practice.

## Source Frame
This is a technical instruction unit. It wants the reader to understand CCE not only as a definition but as a practice diagnostic embodied in label rhythm, label choice, and voice tone. It also wants to reduce common confusions: labeling is not identical with Noting, labels should not be relabeled, and disappearance under observation is not a problem.

The strongest claims are definitional and procedural. The weaker claims are causal or global: the source says systematic practice can dramatically increase satisfaction and reduce suffering, but this unit does not specify dosage, population differences, coaching competence, or adverse-condition boundaries.

The teaching frame is pragmatic. Complexity is admitted, but handled by saying the practitioner only needs one or two effective focus options. The label instructions are similarly adjustable: use mental labels, spoken labels, strongly spoken labels, or no labels according to what preserves enough CCE.

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that systematic practice can dramatically increase satisfaction and decrease suffering is asserted instructionally rather than evidenced in this unit.
- The claim that strongly spoken labels by definition restore an acceptable level of mindful awareness works as a practice heuristic, but it should not be overread as proof that the whole triad is balanced or safe in all conditions.
- The causal claim that an equanimity voice tends to create equanimity is plausible within the training frame but not independently demonstrated here.
- The unit treats flooding and regret as consequences of low sensory clarity, which may be useful for practice routing but is too narrow as a full account of overwhelm, trauma, executive function, or behavior.

## Important Omissions
- The unit does not specify when difficulty concentrating calls for coaching, rest, medical or mental-health support, or stopping practice rather than stronger labels.
- It does not describe how to assess whether a practitioner is becoming mechanical, tense, ashamed, performative, or dissociated while labeling.
- It names coaching use of spoken labels but does not define facilitator competence, consent, privacy, or how a facilitator distinguishes useful self-consciousness practice from harmful exposure.
- It references Gone and Rest but leaves their detailed instructions to other sources.
- It does not yet explain how See, Hear, and Feel labels change meaning by focus range; that is Unit 03's task.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Labels are optional scaffolding, yet correct labeling is treated as enough CCE for effective practice; this is best held as a training criterion rather than an absolute measure.
- Strongly spoken labels are recommended when difficulty arises, but difficulty can have causes outside meditation technique, including fatigue, trauma, illness, medication, or unsafe context.
- Labeling uses auditory events while instructing practitioners not to track the labels as auditory events; this is coherent as scaffolding but complicates simple sensory-totality claims.
- Observation-induced disappearance is treated as valid impermanence data, but this leaves open when observation is productively clarifying versus subtly suppressing or controlling experience.
