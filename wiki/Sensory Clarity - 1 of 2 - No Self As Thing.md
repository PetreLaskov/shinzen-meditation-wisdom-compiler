dialogue as a foil for a more practice-operational no-self argument. Its
durable contribution is to make "no self as thing" depend on direct
Feel/Image/Talk sensory clarity rather than on an abstract proof from parts
and wholes.

## Practice Handles
- When self feels like a thing, ask which direct sensory strands are present:
  emotional body sensation, mental image, internal talk, or some combination.
- Prefer direct sensory investigation over trying to prove no-self through
  abstract parts-and-wholes reasoning.
- Use the CRT-style resolution move: what appears as one self-image may become
  multiple component streams when sensory resolution increases.
- Preserve conventional self-language while investigating whether any
  directly experienced component is itself a self-thing.
- Recognize constructive identity practices, such as deity visualization,
  mantra, gesture, and emotional tone, as evidence that the same inner
  building blocks can be assembled as well as deconstructed.

## Summary
Shinzen opens with the *Questions of King Milinda*, presenting the famous
exchange in which Nagasena says he is conventionally called Nagasena but
there is no thing called Nagasena. The chariot analogy then shows that a
chariot is not found in its wheels, hub, axle, yoke, or other parts. Shinzen
accepts the conclusion that self is no thing, but says he does not find the
argument philosophically decisive enough to end all objections.

His alternative is to narrow the investigation to what meditators can
directly experience. Instead of beginning with conceptual aggregates such as
body, formations, or consciousness considered as an entity, he asks
practitioners to look at the sensory strands that most directly build
ordinary identity: Feel, Image, and Talk. Emotional pleasure and pain,
visual thinking, and internal speech are all directly available enough to be
trained as sensory objects.

He then points to Shingon deity practice as an implicit precedent. Deity
identification uses physical posture or gesture, mental image, mantra or
internal talk, and emotional tone, suggesting that identity can be built from
the same components that sensory clarity later untangles. When practitioners
tease those strands apart, Shinzen says they can see feeling, thought, and
image without finding a thing called self.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "There is no thing called the self" should not be used to deny conventional personhood, responsibility, trauma history, relationship needs, or clinical symptoms.
- The critique of the chariot argument should not become dismissal of Buddhist tradition; Shinzen uses the tradition while tightening the practice route.
- Shingon identity-construction language should not be used to claim that any imagined identity is spiritually or psychologically beneficial.
- Brain-imageability should not be treated as validation that the Feel/Image/Talk model is a complete neuroscience of self.
