Kriyas are Shinzen's borrowed Indic term for spontaneous movements that can
arise in meditation. They may be treated as purification-adjacent sensory
events, but they are not required for progress and should not be made into a
badge, fear object, or substitute for ordinary safety assessment.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Kriyas range from small rocking or shaking to dramatic movement or sounds, and the useful practice question is whether the practitioner can complete the subtle urge sensations or, failing that, the surface movement.
- **Why this matters**: This page routes a conspicuous altered practice phenomenon without treating it as pathology by default, proof of progress, or something to chase.
- **Key tensions**: Shinzen reassures practitioners strongly, but the available source does not define clinical, neurological, trauma, injury, retreat, or teacher-response criteria.
- **Source posture**: Strong as Shinzen's own teaching on how to relate to kriyas; speculative as a mechanism for why kriyas occur or as safety guidance.

## Model
[[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] defines kriyas in the meditation context as
spontaneous movements that "just sort of happen" to the practitioner. They
can be mild rocking or shaking, but may sometimes include dramatic movement,
grimacing, or sounds ([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S1).

Shinzen's first move is normalization. The phenomenon is not uncommon in
meditation traditions with Indic background, and having a technical term can
reassure practitioners that they are not uniquely unstable or failing
([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S2). His second move is anti-status:
kriyas are not a barrier to progress and not a requirement for progress.
Some practitioners experience them and some do not ([[Kriyas & Complete
Experiences]] S3-S4).

[[Kriyas & the Cloud of Unknowing]] adds a cross-traditional anti-status
witness. Shinzen says the medieval Christian Cloud of Unknowing describes
kriya-like phenomena and reports practitioners mistaking strange movements
and gestures for spiritual progress or divine indwelling. He reads the
Cloud's correction as the same mature rule: neither desire kriyas nor
suppress them; let them do their thing ([[Kriyas & the Cloud of Unknowing]]
S5-S7).

The purification explanation is source-attributed. Shinzen uses a U Ba Khin
lineage model in which sensations experienced without mindfulness lay down
sankhara grooves; meditation breaks those grooves up; as they surface in
reverse, they may appear as present sensations before they are fully
observable, and that semiconscious surfacing can move the body
([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S6-S8).

## Practice Routing
The main practice rule is two-level complete experience. If the subtle
sensations underneath the urge to move can be detected, those are the best
object for [[Complete Experience]]. Shinzen says that when they are completed,
the body is no longer driven to move ([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S9).

If the underlying sensations cannot be detected, the practitioner completes
the surface kriya itself: the rocking, shaking, movement, sound, or other
available body sensation. That is not failure; it is the best available
object at that time ([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S9).

Suppressing versus allowing should be an experiment rather than a doctrine.
Shinzen recommends trying both because not moving may reveal the subtle
driver, while allowing the movement may make the surface event workable
([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S5, S9).

The two kriya talks create a useful distinction. Experimental restraint can
be tried to clarify the subtle driver, but suppression as fear, aversion,
control, shame, or status management contradicts the mature stance of neither
desiring nor suppressing the phenomenon ([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]]
S5, S9; [[Kriyas & the Cloud of Unknowing]] S7).

## Boundaries
Kriyas are not proof of purification or attainment. Shinzen explicitly warns
that talking about them can create craving in practitioners who think nothing
interesting has happened yet ([[Kriyas & Complete Experiences]] S3-S4).
The Cloud talk strengthens that warning because Shinzen presents status
inflation around kriya-like movement as an old cross-cultural error, not a
modern oddity ([[Kriyas & the Cloud of Unknowing]] S6-S8).

They are also not automatically a pathology. Shinzen reassures practitioners
that kriyas do not by themselves mean they are going crazy, but that
reassurance is not a clinical differential. The current source does not
distinguish kriyas from neurological symptoms, trauma activation, panic,
medication or substance effects, sleep deprivation, injury risk, group
contagion, teacher pressure, or destabilization.

Kriyas differ from [[Strong Determination]]. Strong Determination is chosen
voluntary immobility that intensifies touch/Feel/Image/Talk practice; kriyas
are spontaneous movements that may either be restrained experimentally or
allowed experimentally. They also differ from [[Dissolution]]: kriyas may be
purification-adjacent and altered, but the source does not identify them with
bhanga or Source-side destabilization.

## Implications
For practice guidance, kriya reports should trigger four checks:

- Is the practitioner relating with fear, craving, shame, or status?
- Can the subtle urge sensations be detected, or is the surface movement the available object?
- Does suppressing or allowing movement increase concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity?
- Are ordinary safety factors active: injury risk, neurological symptoms, trauma activation, sleep/substance/medication issues, destabilization, or teacher/group pressure?

These checks preserve Shinzen's reassurance while keeping kriyas inside the
larger [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].
