## Teaching Register
Explanatory classification talk. Shinzen is translating between early Buddhist sensory categories and his practice interface, with special emphasis on why his subdivisions make thinking and emotional body sensation observable in real time.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk gives Gate 5 a clean opening: sensory clarity depends on making experience qualitatively and spatially tangible. It also supplies a direct oral source for several conventions already present in the manual, especially mental image/talk, emotional Feel versus physical Touch, and smell/taste as body sensation.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen relates his sensory classification to the traditional Buddhist six consciousnesses: hearing, seeing, smelling, tasting, body sensation, and thinking.
- **S2**: He splits thinking into auditory thinking or Talk and visual thinking or Image because this gives thought qualitative tangibility and spatial location.
- **S3**: He splits body experience into emotional body sensation, called Feel, and other body sensation, called Touch, because subtle emotional body sensation can strongly distort perception and behavior.
- **S4**: Smell and taste are treated as forms of physical body sensation for simplicity and symmetry.
- **S5**: By learning talk space and image space, practitioners may detect subtle, fleeting, massively parallel undercurrents out of which conscious talk and image arise; Shinzen identifies this as direct, real-time monitoring of the subconscious.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S2**: "Observe your thinking" becomes practical only after thought is given sensory whats and wheres: auditory/visual qualities plus locations such as head, ears, front/behind eyes, surrounding ambience, or body image.
- **S3**: Feel is not a vague synonym for emotion; it is the body component of emotion, including subtle wispy signals that may shape action before they are obvious.
- **S5**: The surface/depth distinction keeps sensory clarity from stopping at explicit words and pictures, while leaving the subconscious claim inside Shinzen's practice frame rather than turning it into a clinical model.

## Practice Handles
- When observing thought is too intangible, divide it into Talk and Image, then ask both qualitative and spatial questions: what sensory quality is present, and where is it located?
- When working with emotion, look for the somatic component rather than assuming the emotion is only mental content.
- Treat subtle emotional Feel as significant practice material, especially when perception or behavior seems disproportionate to the overt content.
- If explicit inner talk or image quiets, gently check for subtle vibratory undercurrents in talk space or image space without forcing them.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by linking his system to the traditional six consciousnesses, then explains his revisions. Instead of leaving thinking as one intangible category, he divides it into Talk and Image. Talk has an auditory quality and tends to localize around the head or ears; Image has a visual quality and can localize in front of or behind the eyes, around the environment when eyes are closed, or on the body when one imagines the body.

He likewise divides body sensation into Feel and Touch. Feel is emotional body sensation, including subtle somatic components of emotion, while Touch covers the rest of body sensation. This split matters because emotional experience often includes body sensation even when people think of emotion as only mental, and subtle emotional body sensations can distort perception and behavior. Smell and taste are grouped with physical body sensation for simplicity and symmetry.

The talk then extends the surface map toward depth. As practitioners become intimate with talk space and image space, explicit talk and image may give way to subtle undercurrents of processing that Shinzen describes as massively parallel, fleeting, and vibratory. He interprets this as a way to monitor the general activation level of the subconscious mind in real time.

## Source Frame

The claim that subtle mental undercurrents are "the subconscious" is a Shinzen teaching interpretation, not independent clinical or neuroscientific evidence.
