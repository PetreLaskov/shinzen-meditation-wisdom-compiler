## Teaching Register
The register is informal Q&A-style guidance about teacher discernment. Shinzen uses a provocative body-language anecdote to keep the listener from equating apparent no-self presentation with realization, goodness, or teaching competence.

## Why This Talk Matters
This talk adds a missing Gate 6A bridge between realization and teacher qualification. It says liberation signs are uncertain and component-specific, while qualification rests more strongly on ordinary behavior and the results a teacher gets with students.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Having a liberation experience does not necessarily qualify someone to teach, and lacking liberation does not necessarily exclude someone from being a good teacher.
- **S2**: Shinzen informally guesses whether someone has had liberation experiences by looking at body language and personal behavior, but he says these are guesses and personal behavior is hard to know without long close contact.
- **S3**: Body language can express a flow of emptiness and impermanence through voice, gaze, and movement, but body language alone can mislead because mental illness or harsh life can produce a pseudo-no-self presentation without clear enlightened consciousness.
- **S4**: Teacher qualification should be judged by student results: "the virtue of a teacher" is in the effects the teacher gets with students, not in teacher status as such.
- **S5**: A good teacher may not have the deepest liberation experiences, while a deeply enlightened person may not be a good teacher; the practical test is whether the teacher can help students with what they bring.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: This is the central anti-guru move: realization and teaching competence are related but separable components.
- **S2**: Shinzen preserves discernment while calibrating uncertainty; he can make a "pretty good guess" but not know for sure.
- **S3**: The homeless-person anecdote blocks charisma/body-language inference by showing that no-self-like presentation can be disconnected from integrated liberation and functional behavior.
- **S4**: The result criterion shifts evaluation from teacher aura to student outcomes.

## Practice Handles
For a practitioner or community evaluating a teacher, this talk suggests three checks:

- Do not treat liberation claims, aura, gaze, voice, or bodily ease as sufficient qualification.
- Look for ordinary human goodness and behavior over time, especially from people close enough to observe daily conduct.
- Look at student results: whether students become clearer, more skillful, better supported, and able to work with what arises.

## Summary
Shinzen opens by denying a simple equivalence between liberation and teaching qualification. A person may have liberation experiences and still not be a good teacher; another person may lack major liberation and still teach effectively. His informal signs for liberation are body language and personal behavior, but both are limited. Body language is immediately visible, while personal behavior requires long close observation.

He then destabilizes the body-language criterion. He recalls seeing a homeless person whose body language, if cleaned up and placed before an audience without speech, could resemble a famous enlightened-looking figure. Shinzen says this could happen because mental illness and harsh life may have driven ego expression out of the body, producing a pseudo-enlightened presentation without classical clear enlightened consciousness. The point is not to diagnose the person but to show why body language is not enough.

The qualification criterion is more pragmatic: look at students. Shinzen says teachers do not exist as special entities; there is only the activity of teaching and its effects. Some deeply enlightened people may be poor teachers, while less deeply realized teachers may get strong results and handle what students bring.

## Source Frame
The source is a discernment teaching, not an institutional standard. Shinzen wants to preserve sensitivity to liberation signs while preventing overclaim: body language can be meaningful, but behavior and student outcomes are stronger checks. The transcript does not define how to measure results, how to audit student outcomes, or what safeguards are needed when a teacher's results are mixed with power, dependency, abuse, or institutional incentives.

## Weakest Claims
- Inferring liberation from body language, gaze, voice, or "flow of empty energy" is explicitly uncertain and not independently verifiable from the transcript.
- The homeless-person anecdote is rhetorically powerful but speculative; it should not become a general claim about homelessness, mental illness, or enlightenment.
- "Results with students" is a useful criterion but underspecified: the talk does not define good results, timeframe, selection effects, student dependency, misconduct masking, or adverse outcomes.

## Important Omissions
- No concrete teacher-ethics standards, credentialing criteria, scope-of-practice limits, consent rules, grievance processes, or student-protection mechanisms.
- No distinction between student progress caused by the teacher, by the student's prior practice, by community support, or by survivorship bias.
- No clinical framework for distinguishing mental illness, trauma, dissociation, DPDR, no-self, and liberation; the anecdote only warns that appearance can mislead.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen uses body language as one informal criterion while also showing that body language can be dangerously misleading.
- The talk values student results but does not say how to weigh results against misconduct, dependency, power imbalance, or harmed students.
- Saying there are no teachers, only teaching activity, reduces status projection but could be misused to evade role responsibility unless paired with accountability structures.

## Easily Misused Claims
- No-self-like body language could be overread as proof of attainment.
- Student results could be used to excuse teacher misconduct or ignore harmed students.
- "There are no teachers" could be misused as non-doership rhetoric unless ordinary role accountability remains intact.
- The mental-illness anecdote should not be generalized into stigmatizing claims or used to diagnose people from appearance.
