- **S3**: Shinzen equates "in the zone" with extraordinary concentration during performance, using sport as the example, but says zone states do not automatically retrain ordinary perception.
- **S4**: To count as meditation, an activity must consistently elevate baseline concentration when the person is not deliberately trying to be concentrated.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S2**: The clip distinguishes activity-as-container from activity-as-meditation. Art and sport are eligible only when their effects leave the activity and inform daily life.
- **S3-S4**: "In the zone" becomes a concentration taste, not a sufficient path criterion. The decisive test is trait-like elevation of ordinary focus.
- **S5-S6**: Shinzen avoids both extremes: formal sitting is not the only theoretical route, but most ordinary activities need coaching or formal practice support to become reliable meditation.

## Practice Handles
For a practitioner or guide:

- Ask whether the activity produces measurable-seeming daily-life carryover, not only absorption during performance.
- Treat art, sport, running, singing, or work as possible practice containers when they are intentionally trained toward baseline focus.
- If a practitioner wants to substitute an art for formal meditation, check whether the activity is retraining ordinary perception month after month.
- Use formal meditation or coaching when the activity creates peak focus but no stable carryover.
- Keep "in the zone" distinct from Shinzen's technical [[Flow]] and from baseline concentration growth.

## Summary
The questioner asks whether ordinary arts such as piano, martial arts, dancing, ballet, or sport can count as meditation. Shinzen says yes in principle: an art can become de facto meditation when the way it is practiced produces carryover into daily life and changes the person's ordinary base level of focus.

He then repeats the limiting condition. Many people can become deeply concentrated while performing an art or sport. Athletes call that being "in the zone." But Shinzen says the uncommon and decisive feature is that the concentration carries over so every moment of life is increasingly informed by elevated focus. Without that carryover, the activity may produce strong temporary concentration but not count as meditation in his technical sense.

The clip closes by distinguishing theory from typical reality. In theory, systematic sitting exercises are not the only way to develop baseline concentration. In practice, Shinzen says formal meditation usually trains the carryover more directly, and someone trying to use running, singing, or another art as meditation will probably still need coaching in how to make the state transfer into daily life.
