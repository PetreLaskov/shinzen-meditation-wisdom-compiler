## Teaching Register
Epistemic calibration and public teaching boundary. Shinzen is not guiding a sit or answering a practice problem; he is telling listeners where he claims expertise and where he does not.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk prevents a common overread of Shinzen's Source, Zero, Flow, no-self, and science language. His confidence belongs to trainable sensory experience and its liberating effects, while what is ultimately behind sensory experience remains outside his confident teaching claim.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen usually avoids talking about reality and prefers to talk about sensory experience because years of work with his own and others' sensory experience give him confidence there.
- **S2**: He does not claim to know what underlying objective reality is, whether there is a reality behind sensory experience, or what people really see and hear, because scientists and philosophers still disagree.
- **S3**: He says he can teach sensory experience in ways that are true, deep, useful, and broad, and he frames that as a kind of good science for sensory experience.
- **S4**: His confident examples include the perceiving self arising through Feel/Image/Talk reactions to Touch/Sight/Sound, suffering as pain multiplied by resistance, and ordinary sensory experience becoming extraordinary or spiritual under enough CCE.
- **S5**: His offered metaphysical conjecture is explicitly marked as wild conjecture: every Touch/Sight/Sound/Feel/Image/Talk experience may be an experience of everything together, the whole causal nexus or suchness, in a way the human brain is not evolved to process.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S3**: The main move is epistemic humility. Shinzen narrows his authority to the domain where practice observation, instruction, and usefulness have accumulated.
- **S4**: The confident teaching domain is not small; it includes self-construction, suffering reduction, and ordinary experience becoming spiritually significant through concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity.
- **S5**: The talk preserves speculative imagination without converting it into doctrine. The conjecture is valuable only because Shinzen labels it as non-authoritative.

## Practice Handles
- When asked what is really out there, route back to what can be known in experience: Touch, Sight, Sound, Feel, Image, Talk, CCE, resistance, selfing, and ordinary sensory transformation.
- Use Source or everything-at-once language only after naming its evidence tier. It may function as a contemplative conjecture or poetic pointer, not as a practice prerequisite or proof.
- Treat sensory maps as interfaces for liberation, not as claims that the six sensory categories reveal the final ontology of the universe.

## Summary
Shinzen says he normally does not talk about reality because he lacks confidence in answers about what, if anything, lies behind sensory experience. Scientists and philosophers have debated objective external reality for a long time without agreement, so he does not want to make amateur claims in that domain.

He contrasts that restraint with strong confidence about sensory experience. He says he can teach sensory experience in ways that are true, deep, useful, and broad, including how the perceiving self arises through Feel/Image/Talk reactions to Touch/Sight/Sound, how suffering depends on resistance to pain, and how ordinary sensory events can become extraordinary through enough concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity.

Only at the end does he offer a conjecture about reality: perhaps each sensory event is an experience of everything all at once, the whole causal nexus or suchness, beyond the human brain's normal processing. The important source posture is that he explicitly frames this as a guess rather than something to convince others of.

## Source Frame
The talk wants listeners to distinguish Shinzen's sensory-practice confidence from ontology. It supports the wiki's reading of Shinzen as a phenomenological and pedagogical system-builder, not as someone claiming settled metaphysics whenever he uses Source, suchness, science, or everything-language.

The source is reliable for Shinzen's self-positioning and the practice claims he regards as high-confidence. It is weak as evidence for the metaphysical conjecture itself, for scientific validation of his sensory maps, or for a complete epistemology.

## Weakest Claims
- The "true, deep, useful, and broad" science formulation is Shinzen's teaching frame, not independent validation that his sensory categories meet scientific standards.
- The everything-at-once conjecture is intentionally under-supported and should not become the wiki's ontology.
- The transcript does not define how to test the confident examples, such as suffering as pain multiplied by resistance, beyond Shinzen's teaching experience.

## Important Omissions
- No detailed practice instructions, dosage, safety criteria, or troubleshooting are given.
- No philosophy-of-perception argument is supplied; the talk relies on the fact of continuing expert disagreement.
- The talk does not specify how Shinzen distinguishes high-confidence sensory claims from conjecture in borderline cases such as Source, science analogies, or comparative mysticism.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen says he does not speak about reality, then gives a reality conjecture; the tension is resolved only if the conjecture remains explicitly non-authoritative.
- The talk calls sensory-experience teaching good science while not providing empirical methods or measurements; this should be read as a pragmatic science analogy unless supported elsewhere.
- "Everything all together" language is close to Source/suchness language elsewhere, but this talk pressures the wiki to keep that language epistemically weaker than practice phenomenology.

## Easily Misused Claims
- The everything-at-once conjecture could be inflated into a metaphysical doctrine, nondual proof, or claim that all experiences literally contain all reality.
- "Good science for sensory experience" could be overread as completed empirical validation.
- "Absolute confidence" could be generalized beyond Shinzen's examples into certainty about external reality, clinical outcomes, or cosmic structure.
