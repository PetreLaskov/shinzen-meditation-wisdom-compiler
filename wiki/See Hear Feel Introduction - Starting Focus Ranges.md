This unit covers pages 36-41 of Shinzen Young's *See Hear Feel: An Introduction*, using a mechanical Markdown extraction from the source PDF. It teaches SHF by focus range: where attention is allowed to go during a practice period, how beginners can start with four ranges, and how range choice can simplify or widen practice when experience is easy, difficult, sparse, or intense.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: A focus range is the set of experiences a practitioner has decided to focus on for a period of time; ranges can be narrow, medium-sized, or unrestricted.
- **S2**: SHF can be applied across many focus ranges, and the standard starting point is four options: Focus on See, Focus on Hear, Focus on Feel, and Focus on Everything.
- **S3**: The four starter options can be used singly, selected in the moment, or organized into a five-step broad-narrow-broad sequence: Everything, See, Hear, Feel, Everything.
- **S4**: Range choice can regulate practice load: if unrestricted noting is too busy, narrow to one modality; if single-modality practice is taxing, broaden to Everything.
- **S5**: Uncomfortable experiences can be met either by Turn Towards, choosing a range that includes the challenge, or by Turn Away, choosing a different range; Shinzen says both train concentration, clarity, and equanimity if done as mindful awareness practice.
- **S6**: In restricted Focus on See, Hear, or Feel, the practitioner actively returns when pulled outside the modality while passively allowing inner activity, outer activity, or rest states within that modality.
- **S7**: In Focus on Everything, the starter contact stance is passive and the starter co-occurrence option is exclusive emphasis: choose one modality to note when multiple modalities come or call.
- **S8**: If nothing much comes or calls during Focus on Everything, the practitioner is likely experiencing visual, auditory, or body rest, which should be noted as See, Hear, or Feel respectively.
- **S9**: Energy phenomena are labeled by their sensory field; body energy is usually labeled Feel, and the Unified Mindfulness technical name for such energy is Flow.
- **S10**: Later SHF options include specialized ranges, Gone, inclusive emphasis, re-noting, and zooming; the unit frames options as a way to optimize training with walls and windows.

## Summary
The unit defines focus range as the scope of experience selected for practice. SHF can work with narrow ranges such as breath sensation, medium ranges such as body scan, and broad ranges such as Open Presence or Choiceless Awareness. Its standard beginner interface is four ranges: just visual experience, just auditory experience, just somatic experience, or unrestricted sensory experience.

Shinzen presents those four ranges as both standardized and flexible. A practitioner may use one, several, or all four; may choose by interest, opportunity, or necessity; or may run a broad-narrow-broad sequence of Everything, See, Hear, Feel, and Everything. The sequence can be paced from a few minutes to an hour by changing the time devoted to each step.

The unit then turns focus range into a challenge-handling variable. If unrestricted range is overwhelming, narrow to one modality. If a single modality is constricting or effortful, broaden to Everything. With discomfort, distress, confusion, fatigue, urges, or similar challenges, the practitioner may Turn Towards by choosing the range that includes the challenge, or Turn Away by choosing another range; the source says both are valid when they train CCE rather than avoidance or collapse.

The final instruction layer clarifies contact stance. Restricted row practice uses active return to the chosen modality and passive reception within that modality. Focus on Everything uses a passive "come or call" stance, with exclusive emphasis as the starter rule for co-occurrence. If nothing seems available, rest states count as sensory experience and should be noted as See, Hear, or Feel. If experience breaks into energy, the practitioner labels it by modality and understands the technical category as Flow.

## Source Frame
This is a beginner-routing and practice-options unit. It wants the reader to understand SHF as easy to start without being one-size-fits-all: four starter ranges are enough for many practitioners, yet the same system can later expand into specialized ranges, Gone, inclusive emphasis, re-noting, zooming, and other options.

The strongest material is procedural: the focus-range definition, the four starter ranges, the optional sequence, restricted versus unrestricted contact stance, rest-state labeling, and Flow labeling. The weaker material is pedagogical and outcome-oriented: the claims that children from around four can usually implement the ranges, that turn-toward and turn-away are a "win-win," and that options optimize training are not backed here by developmental, clinical, or comparative evidence.

The source's governing assumption is that mindful awareness practice is defined by concentration, clarity, and equanimity rather than by whether attention turns toward or away from a difficult object. That is useful for practice routing, but it also creates a safety boundary: the source does not specify when turning away is adaptive regulation versus avoidance, or when turning toward is useful courage versus unnecessary flooding.

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that children from about age four can usually understand and implement the four ranges should stay source-attributed; the unit gives no developmental or educational evidence.
- The "win-win" framing for Turn Towards and Turn Away is instructionally useful but can overstate reliability without criteria for avoidance, dissociation, overwhelm, clinical distress, or needed ordinary action.
- The unit's examples of emotional distress, confusion, discomfort, fatigue, and negative urges are broad enough that practice advice could be misapplied without support thresholds.
- The Flow labeling instruction is clear for SHF taxonomy but does not distinguish meditative energy phenomena from medical, neurological, trauma-related, or affective processes.

## Important Omissions
- The unit does not define how to choose among the four ranges beyond interest, opportunity, necessity, or whether the current range feels too busy or too taxing.
- It does not specify when to Turn Towards, when to Turn Away, when to stop, when to rest, when to seek guidance, or when to address a practical or clinical problem directly.
- It names specialized ranges, Gone, inclusive emphasis, re-noting, zooming, Spaciousness, walls, and windows without giving the fuller instructions carried by later units or other sources.
- It does not explain how coaches should teach the starter sequence across temperament, age, trauma history, medical pain, sleepiness, or high-intensity retreat conditions.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- SHF is standardized around four starter ranges, but the same unit emphasizes customization, optional sequencing, and eventual specialization.
- Turn Towards and Turn Away are both framed as valid CCE training, but they can have opposite psychological functions depending on context; the source does not operationalize that difference.
- Focus on Everything is described as relaxing and delicious, yet also potentially overwhelming because many events may happen at once.
- Rest states are treated as sensory experience and "absence as presence," which sharpens detection skill but could be confused with dullness, shutdown, or dissociation without additional criteria.
