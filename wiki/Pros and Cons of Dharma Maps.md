This 61-minute `@ShinzenVideos` talk answers a question about Buddhist
stage maps by turning the issue into practice calibration. Its durable value
is not a new path map, but a set of tests: use maps for orientation and
motivation, watch for map fixation, judge progress by practice taste and
life effects, and evaluate teachers by transformational track record rather
than map agreement.

## Teaching Register
Advanced-path Q&A, expectation calibration, and teacher-accountability

## Why This Talk Matters
The existing wiki already had map humility, trap, and teacher-accountability
sources. This talk adds a selected-source synthesis of how to use maps
without being used by them: maps can inform practice, motivate effort, and
name likely terrain, but they become harmful when they feed rating, status,
internet contention, questing for the perfect map, or literal linearity. The
talk also gives one of Shinzen's most compact progress-test ladders.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen frames Buddhist path maps as numerous, historically varied,
  and only one subset of the wider field of world contemplative practice.
- **S2**: His own early scholar-practitioner period made map comparison
  painful and distracting; he describes about fifteen years of torturing
  himself with where he stood on inherited maps.
- **S3**: Major cons of maps include rating/status concern and spending
  practice energy on map debate, map selection, and the quest for the right
  map.
- **S4**: Shinzen says he takes comfort in the view that none of the current
  maps work very well by his standards, while also saying they are vastly
  better than not practicing or not having maps.
- **S5**: Major pros of maps are that they can tell practitioners where to
  go, what to look for, what may be optimal when certain things occur, and
  why to stay motivated.
- **S6**: Maps deserve respect because they are partly based on real
  experience from deep practitioners, but textual transmission, scribal
  error, student interpretation, cultural authority, and forced agreement
  with scripture make literal fundamentalism unsafe.
- **S7**: Shinzen prefers broad trends over a personal stage map: over time
  concentration, clarity, and equanimity tend to rise and have major life
  effects.
- **S8**: He offers four progress criteria in ascending maturity: unusual or
  entertaining experiences during practice; tasting concentration, clarity,
  and equanimity during practice; long-term daily-life effects such as less
  suffering, more fulfillment, insight, behavior improvement, and love or
  service; and finally practicing without much fixation on goals.
- **S9**: A complete experience of the belief "I am not enlightened" can
  collapse the apparent distance to enlightenment; this is a practice handle,
  not a denial of ordinary maturation.
- **S10**: Shinzen rejects the common expectation that enlightenment means a
  before/after perfection switch. In this talk, his program target is
  stream-entry-like seeing no-self, which begins rather than completes the
  larger path.
- **S11**: He says realization need not be sudden and dramatic; it may come
  on gradually enough that the practitioner acclimatizes before recognizing
  it.
- **S12**: He rejects the claim that everyone must pass through long
  paralytic dark-night experiences before enlightenment, while allowing that
  everyone meets some fear, depression, or disorientation.
- **S13**: He rejects literal linear maps. In practice, he says many maps
  operate more like looping-and-branching algorithms, with walls,
  opportunities, optimal responses, revisiting, and cycles.
- **S14**: Teacher evaluation should look less at whether the teacher's map
  agrees with Shinzen's and more at track record in leading people to
  significant transformation.
- **S15**: Shinzen distinguishes arhatship from Buddhahood by describing a
  Buddha-like figure as a creative arhat who discovers something new and
  powerful enough to change human history; he speculates that a future
  Buddha-like discovery might come from a team.
- **S16**: Shinzen speculates that future open dialogue, evidence, and
  science may produce a more universal and complex model of enlightenment;
  this remains conjecture.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S2-S4**: Shinzen uses his own map-related suffering to make map humility
  practical. The danger is not abstract wrong theory but practice energy
  captured by rating, status, and map quest.
- **S5-S6**: He refuses both cynicism and fundamentalism. Maps can carry deep
  practitioner knowledge, but source transmission and cultural pressure make
  literalism unsafe.
- **S8**: The four progress criteria give future agents a compact diagnostic
  ladder, moving from experience-chasing to CCE taste, daily-life effects,
  and non-fixated practice.
- **S10-S12**: The talk corrects three enlightenment myths: perfection after
  awakening, suddenness as required, and long dark night as universal.
- **S13**: The linear-map correction links map talk back to Shinzen's own
  branch-and-loop guidance style: walls and windows require adaptive
  response, not only stage identification.
- **S14**: Teacher track record becomes a map-independent evaluation rule:
  helping students transform matters more than sharing the same model.
- **S15**: The creative-arhat frame keeps path discovery tied to historical
  service impact rather than private novelty or self-certified status.

## Practice Handles
- Use a map when it helps orient attention, normalize likely terrain,
  identify what to look for, or choose an optimal response to a practice
  event.
- Drop or downgrade map inquiry when it consumes energy that should be going
  into actual practice, behavior change, or support.
- If map comparison creates status anxiety, note the rating and
  self-location process itself as Image/Talk/Feel practice material, then
  return to the live method.
- Judge progress first by whether practice builds CCE and then by whether
  daily life shows less suffering, more fulfillment, clearer understanding,
  better behavior, love, service, or reduced inside/outside alienation.
- Do not use unusual experiences as the only progress criterion; they can
  motivate, but dry or difficult periods are inevitable.
- When "I am not enlightened" becomes the dominant obstacle, treat that
  belief as a complete-experience object rather than as proof of distance.
- Do not assume suddenness, perfection, no dark-night bypass, or linear
  sequence. Ask what loop, branch, wall, window, or broad trend is actually
  happening.
- Evaluate teachers through student transformation, ordinary behavior,
  feedback, and scope competence rather than agreement with a favored map.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by naming the breadth of the question. Buddhist map systems
are numerous: ox-herding pictures, four classes of noble individuals,
stages of insight, jhana maps, bodhisattva stages, and other scholastic or
lineage models. He presents them as part of a much larger world
contemplative field, then uses his own history as a caution. His academic
training eventually helped him teach, but early in practice it fed a
painful obsession with where he was on maps and which stage remained
unreached.

The middle of the talk distinguishes map value from map fixation. Maps can
tell practitioners where to go, what to look for, and what may be optimal
when experiences arise. They are often based on deep practitioners'
experience and should not be dismissed. But they also travel through oral
memory, student writing, hand copying, cultural authority, and forced
agreement with inherited scripture. This makes fundamentalist literalism and
internet-style map contention poor uses of limited practice energy.

Shinzen then replaces a stage map with progress criteria and myth
corrections. The least mature criterion is unusual or entertaining
experience during practice. More mature is tasting concentration, sensory
clarity, and equanimity during practice. More mature still is long-term
daily-life change: less suffering, more fulfillment, insight, behavior
improvement, love, service, and reduced inside/outside alienation. The most
mature criterion is simply doing the practice without much goal fixation. He
then rejects perfection-after-enlightenment, required suddenness, universal
long dark night, and literal linearity. In practice, maps are better treated
as looping-and-branching algorithms with walls, windows, and revisited
territory. He closes by saying teacher evaluation should depend on track
record in helping people transform rather than on agreement with his map,
while conjecturing that future science and open dialogue may eventually
produce a better shared model.

## Source Frame
The source is a map-calibration teaching. Shinzen respects inherited maps
while demoting them from final authority to useful, fallible practice tools.
His strongest claims are Shinzen-system claims about practice orientation,
progress criteria, teacher evaluation, and map-misuse risks.

The talk's weakest frame is future-facing scale and scholarship detail.
Auto captions distort many names and technical terms, and Shinzen explicitly
declines precise Mahasi-map comparison because his memory is not strong
enough in the moment. Future agents should cite this page for his map-use
and progress-calibration moves, not for exact taxonomy, philology, Buddhist
history, or future-science prediction.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen respects inherited maps as deep practitioner evidence while saying
  none works very well by his standards.
- He says he does not have a map, but he does give broad trends, four
  progress criteria, stream-entry framing, and a looping-and-branching
  algorithm.
- The talk values practice without goal fixation while still giving progress
  criteria and transformation aims.
- Teacher track record is privileged over map agreement, but the source does
  not define how to count results, harms, selection effects, or hidden
  dependency.
- The talk rejects universal long dark night while still saying everyone
  meets some fear, depression, or disorientation.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "None of the maps work very well" can become cynicism, anti-teacher
  reflex, or refusal to use helpful tools.
- "Maps are based on deep experience" can become fundamentalism if textual,
  cultural, and transmission caveats are ignored.
- The first progress criterion can fuel unusual-experience chasing if it is
  not ranked below CCE taste and daily-life effects.
- "I am not enlightened" as complete-experience object can bypass ordinary
  maturation, behavior work, clinical care, or teacher feedback if isolated.
- Teacher track record can be misused to excuse misconduct unless paired
  with harm, dependency, feedback, consent, and accountability checks.
- The future-science hope can become premature technology or research hype
  if separated from informed consent, safety, and behavior verification.
