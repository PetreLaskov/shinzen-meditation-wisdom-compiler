Practice Cycles translate Shinzen's techniques from a menu of options into maintained life practice. Chapter 11 names daily, yearly, and fun cycles; the See Hear Feel introduction adds focus-range application, explicit stillness-motion-life minimums, yearly support rhythm, and method-choice principles. [[Organizing Your Practice]] gives the selected oral package that ties those cycles to trigger practice, motion challenge sequences, and formal-session setup.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Practice is maintained by daily, yearly, accelerator, fun, and nonlinear reward rhythms, with retreat momentum converted into stable plateaus by day-to-day continuity, then applied through focus ranges, focus methods, micro-hits, retroactive meditation, trigger practice, motion challenges, formal-session setup modes, and one-deep-hole or backhoe-like strategies chosen for particular situations.
- **Why this matters**: The manual expects formal practice, movement practice, ordinary-life practice, retreat, teacher or coach feedback, intrinsic reward, and situational SHF application to reinforce one another.
- **Key tensions**: The sources give minimums and examples but do not specify retreat safety, coach competence, clinical boundaries, accelerator dosage, or when enjoyment and flexible method choice become avoidance.
- **Source posture**: Strong as manual application guidance; incomplete as a safety or scheduling protocol.

## Daily Cycle
The daily cycle has three venues: practice in stillness, practice in motion, and practice in life ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S5). Chapter 12 repeats this as one of the Ten Elements of Guidance: each focus option should be taught or practiced in all three venues ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S13).

[[The Reptilian Brain, Skinnerian Training & the Experience of God]] adds a
mechanism for why stillness practice matters: a daily sit is a "micro
retreat" that simplifies conditions enough for the body-level mind to learn
the immediate feedback between fighting sensation and suffering, or
noninterference and fulfillment ([[The Reptilian Brain, Skinnerian Training
& the Experience of God]] S2-S4).

SHF Unit 08 gives the most concrete version of the daily rhythm: formal stillness most days with a recommended minimum of 10 minutes, formal motion most days with the same recommended minimum, and life practice most days through at least 3-6 micro-hits plus background practice with no specified minimum ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S1).

[[Organizing Your Practice]] gives the selected-source version of the daily
cycle. Formal practice means a set technique or sequence that receives most
resources for a period of time; formal motion is still formal practice, while
practice in life is intermittent technique use inside ordinary activity
([[Organizing Your Practice]] S1-S3).

The micro-hits FAQ turns the life venue into a concrete daily procedure.
Shinzen says the practitioner can "stop on a dime" when nothing too
demanding is happening and begin practice for 30 seconds, three minutes, or
even a few seconds, using a simple on-the-fly See-Hear-Feel noting sequence
as one example. He recommends peppering the day with at least a half-dozen
such brief practice contacts ([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]] S3-S5).

[[Retroactive Meditation]] adds the high-demand version. When intellectual
work, writing, or figuring things out needs enough resources that a formal
technique cannot be maintained, Shinzen says the practitioner can periodically
stop on a dime, start a technique immediately, and bring full practice quality
to the See/Hear/Feel activation stirred up by the prior work. The work is not
converted into meditation by divided attention; the present activation after
stopping becomes the practice object ([[Retroactive Meditation]] S1-S5).

[[Organizing Your Practice]] adds the resource-allocation rule behind those
micro-hits: practice in life is appropriate when ordinary demands do not need
all available resources; when life needs those resources, the practitioner
deals with life. Its "whole day is a monastery" attitude makes ordinary
events meaningful as training without erasing task responsibility
([[Organizing Your Practice]] S2-S4).

The venues imply different tests:

- **Stillness**: reduced external demand makes sensory clarity and concentration easier to stabilize.
- **Motion**: the body moves while the technique continues, testing portability.
- **Life**: ordinary situations, behavior, and relationships become the practice field.

This is the manual's antidote to a seated-only interpretation. A focus option is not fully integrated until it has some expression in ordinary activity.

[[Can Ordinary Experience Count as Meditation]] adds a tighter criterion for
ordinary activity as practice. Shinzen allows arts, sports, running, singing,
and similar activities to function as meditation in principle, but only when
the way the activity is practiced retrains ordinary perception and raises
baseline focus outside the activity. Peak absorption during performance is
not enough; without carryover into daily life, the activity becomes a dead
end for meditation purposes ([[Can Ordinary Experience Count as Meditation]]
S1-S6).

[[There's No Need to Leave Samadhi]] adds a state-transition version of
practice in life. When a deep state is available and action is needed,
Shinzen says the practitioner does not need to return to the normal way of
being before functioning; needed doing can arise from the state itself. If
fear, disorientation, or inner-system reactivation appears, it becomes the
practice object and a chance to see self forming from no-self ([[There's No
Need to Leave Samadhi]] S3-S6).

[[Shinzen April Daylong Retreat - Four Quadrant Training]] adds a daylong
implementation example. Shinzen frames the six hours as unbroken practice
across seated or standing stillness, walking or other motion, presentation,
and discussion. During discussion, practitioners may continue formal
practice, keep background practice, use micro-hits, or deliberately suspend
formal practice; during stillness and motion, they are asked to have a
well-defined strategy ([[Shinzen April Daylong Retreat - Four Quadrant
Training]] S1). This sharpens the cycle rule: breaks and discussion are not
automatically outside practice, but the mode needs to be chosen.

[[Appreciate the Senses Transcend the Self Express the Source]] confirms the
same discussion-mode rule and adds the individual structure behind it: at
least one workable technique, optional sequencing or free movement among
techniques, daily formal stillness, formal motion, informal micro-hits or
background practice, periodic retreat or self-retreat, and occasional
competent guide check-ins ([[Appreciate the Senses Transcend the Self
Express the Source]] S3, S9). This makes cycle design part of the Note
Everything teaching itself rather than a separate administrative afterthought.

[[Four Ways Forward - June Shinzen Retreat]] adds a full method-workout
example. Stillness periods train Note Everything, Feel Good, and Space/
Expansion-Contraction; motion periods can continue Note Everything, train
Auto Move, or use pleasant outdoor senses to trigger Feel Good; discussion
periods again require a deliberate choice of formal practice, background
practice, micro-hits, or suspension ([[Four Ways Forward - June Shinzen
Retreat]] S4, S11, S13).

[[Shinzen Young Retreat at the Monastic Academy 05.27.2017]] adds a
Source-facing version of the same daylong continuity. The stream alternates
stillness and formal motion while keeping a chosen method active, then closes
with Auto Chant, explicit driving caution, micro-hits, and background
practice as the bridge from retreat to life ([[Shinzen Young Retreat at the
Monastic Academy 05.27.2017]] S11-S15).

[[Shinzen Young Day-Long Retreat at the Monastic Academy - July 22, 2017]]
adds the spaciousness version, under quality-C transcript caution. The day
cycles through Feel Space in stillness, formal motion that continues rather
than breaks practice, discussion options, and a closing intention to use
micro-hits and background practice after retreat. The source again restricts
driving to sensory driving practice rather than spaciousness, no-self, or
Source exploration ([[Shinzen Young Day-Long Retreat at the Monastic Academy
- July 22, 2017]] S5-S8, S15).

[[Organizing Your Practice]] adds trigger practice as the stillness-side
accelerator. Instead of waiting for life to present full-strength reactions,
the practitioner can sit in formal practice while controlled sights, sounds,
or sensations arrive, adjusting type, intensity, duration, timing, and
frequency like graded load. Shinzen distinguishes this from evoking, where
one intentionally brings material up from inside ([[Organizing Your Practice]]
S5-S6).

The same FAQ adds a graded version of that test. A challenge sequence takes
one technique from seated eyes-closed practice into eyes-open sitting,
standing, slow movement, more impactful movement environments, simple tasks,
and eventually demanding tasks. Shinzen compares this to gradually adding
weight or repetitions in training, so life practice develops by progressive
load rather than by an immediate demand for continuous mindfulness
([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]]
S6-S7).

[[Organizing Your Practice]] gives the motion-side version: a technique may
be trained from eyes-closed sitting through eyes-open sitting, standing,
walking, dishes, meal preparation, and conversation, with challenge
increased gradually rather than abruptly ([[Organizing Your Practice]] S7).

[[Mindfulness Strategies when Interacting with Others]] gives the
conversation-specific gate for that final step. Shinzen says some
substantive conversations require all available attention, while lighter
interactions leave enough spare capacity to practice. The usable life-cycle
rule is to pick battles: use Focus Out, body-based lovingkindness, or
reaction-noting only when appropriate response can stay intact
([[Mindfulness Strategies when Interacting with Others]] S1-S7).

## Focus-Range Application
The See Hear Feel introduction adds a compact implementation distinction. SHF affects happiness generically through regular continued practice that raises baseline CCE, and specifically through versions of the technique chosen for particular situations ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S8).

The source calls the intentionally selected set of categories a "focus range." Beginners are expected to explore four ranges: visual experience, auditory experience, body experience, and any/all experience ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S9). The examples are practical: Focus Out can turn a boring task into sensory engagement, Focus on Rest can support a good night's rest even when sleep is imperfect, and Focus In can deconstruct severe emotional distress into image, talk, and emotional body sensation ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S8).

This links practice cycles to situational routing. A cycle is not only repeated sitting; it is the repeated capacity to select a workable range when ordinary life presents boredom, sleeplessness, distress, pleasure, pain, confusion, or behavior pressure.

## Formal Method Choice
Unit 08 adds three ways to organize formal practice. A practitioner may mostly use one focus method, run a set sequence, or freely shift among methods ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S4).

The shorter YouTube session-setup talk gives the same triad in Basic Mindfulness language. The simplest mode is to choose one technique and reduce choice load. The middle mode is to make a psycho-spiritual workout sequence out of the Five Ways. The most complex mode is to choose a theme, stay with it, or switch when interest, opportunity, or necessity points elsewhere ([[Three Ways to Set Up Your Basic Mindfulness Session]] S1-S7). [[Organizing Your Practice]] embeds this triad inside the whole daily/yearly cycle and adds the caution that loop-and-branch choices should not be driven by craving, aversion, unconsciousness, or making the decision into a big deal (S10-S11).

Shinzen's sample workout sequence is Focus In, Focus Out, note restful states, Focus on Change, Do Nothing, then Focus on Positive. He presents Focus on Positive as a reconstructive close after deconstructive practice, like a jacuzzi after exercise; that metaphor is useful for sequencing but does not supply dosage or safety criteria ([[Three Ways to Set Up Your Basic Mindfulness Session]] S3-S5).

[[A Psycho-Spiritual Workout Routine]] gives a shorter version of the same
workout handle. The sequence begins with ordinary sensory experience, shifts
to Rest as a thinned-out version of ordinary experience, lets dynamic Flow
and vanishing become available, and then returns from Source or Zero language
into intentionally created positive Feel/Image/Talk, joy, compassion, and
service ([[A Psycho-Spiritual Workout Routine]] S1-S5). This confirms the
workout as balance across major practice modes rather than a single
technique recommendation.

This choice should be light but not random. Shinzen says method choice can be motivated by interest, opportunity, and necessity; should not be driven by craving, aversion, or unconsciousness; and need not be made into a big deal because every option trains concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S5).

The example sequence is diagnostically rich: a session can move from Focus on Everything to Rest, then to a customized Focus In with strong labels, inclusive emphasis, zooming, and explicit Gone during anger, and finally to Nurture Positive using Flow to radiate lovingkindness ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S6). The source presents this as flexible routing, not a proof that every shift is wise or safe.

## Yearly Cycle
The yearly cycle includes maintaining the daily cycle, periodic retreat, and periodic check-in with a teacher ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S5). Chapter 12's detailed formulation names at least one retreat and at least one check-in with a competent coach as part of the year-to-year cycle ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S4).

The same feedback model scales up to retreat. Shinzen names weekends, weeks,
10-day retreats, and longer retreat as macro versions of the simplified
environment, but the talk gives no readiness, dosage, teacher-competence, or
destabilization criteria; those stay under the safety boundary ([[The
Reptilian Brain, Skinnerian Training & the Experience of God]] S3-S4).

Unit 08 sharpens that into a season-to-season rhythm: at least one week-long residential retreat or equivalent each year, plus at least one yearly competent-coach check-in. It also says monthly participation in the Home Practice Program for a year counts as a week-long residential equivalent for many people ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S2). [[Organizing Your Practice]] gives the oral basis for that equivalence: the four-hour Home Practice Program is presented as the "trivial case" of retreat because it still includes preparation, teaching, Q&A, guided practice, self-work, and limited teacher contact ([[Organizing Your Practice]] S8-S9).

[[Retreat Welcome, April 2009]] gives the retreat-entry version of the same
cycle. Shinzen tells participants that they have come to learn the practice,
the needed concepts, and the techniques that develop core skills; he frames
retreat as one more level of human learning aimed at Total Happiness,
including happiness dependent and independent of conditions for oneself and
others ([[Retreat Welcome, April 2009]] S1-S3). This confirms retreat as a
learning container before it is a momentum container.

The retreat-aftercare talk gives the same rhythm as a five-part continuity
rule after intensive practice: a little real formal practice most days, a lot
of practice every once in a while, practice-in-life micro-hits, regular
contact with someone competent enough to monitor the big picture, and keeping
the whole pattern up for the long haul ([[What to Expect and Do After a
Mindfulness Retreat]] S8). Shinzen's post-retreat minimum is "ludicrously
small" but quality-protected: if the formal sit is only 10 minutes, use
spoken labels so the short session is actual practice rather than vague
relaxation ([[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness Retreat]] S8).

[[Retreat Farewell - May 2009]] gives the retreat-closing version of the
same ecology. Shinzen says retreat builds all-day practice momentum and may
open new levels, but without day-to-day practice those openings remain peak
experiences; when backed by daily practice, they can become plateaus from
which further growth is possible ([[Retreat Farewell - May 2009]] S1-S2).
The same talk names retreat's support functions: easier practice conditions,
peer support, teacher contact, private interviews, and possible between-
retreat contact ([[Retreat Farewell - May 2009]] S3). This confirms retreat
as a support ecology rather than merely an intensity container.

The same unit names optional accelerators: duration training, trigger
practice, and motion challenge sequences ([[See Hear Feel Introduction -
Practice Organization and System Transition]] S3). The micro-hits FAQ gives
challenge sequences their first oral definition in this wiki: gradually add
life complexity while maintaining the technique, with formal-practice
momentum, micro-hit "surgical strikes," and progressive challenge training
working together toward the figure-ground reversal where life happens inside
meditation ([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]] S8-S9). Because neither source gives stop rules, challenge
sequences remain an accelerator pointer rather than a full safety protocol.

This strengthens the support ecology. Basic Mindfulness is modular, but the final manual does not present self-guided practice as the whole support system. Retreat and teacher or coach feedback are normal parts of maintaining the practice.

The Windows/Walls talk adds an unplanned intensive counterpart. Shinzen says most people will not create monastic intensity through traditional retreat, but life may bring injury, illness, betrayal, violence, grief, or moral failure. When the practitioner has internalized the method vocabulary and a trained facilitator can guide the branching, such events may become intensive practice conditions rather than merely disruptions ([[Maximizing Psycho-Spiritual Growth with an Algorithmic Approach (Windows & Walls)]] S4-S6). This is not a substitute for retreat or support; it is a high-risk life-practice claim that depends on guidance and ordinary safety response.

## Fun Cycle
The fun cycle is the manual's motivation model. Chapter 11 says the practitioner should have as much fun as possible by finding something interesting, enjoyable, or productive to focus on and by detecting the distinct reward flavors of concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S6).

Chapter 12 makes the cycle recursive: enjoyment supports skill, skill supports more enjoyment, and more enjoyment supports yet more skill ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S4). This keeps motivation from depending only on discipline, belief, or suffering relief.

The reward flavors belong to [[Mindfulness Skill Triad]]. Concentration can be intrinsically rewarding; sensory clarity can have the taste of crispness or deeper processing; equanimity can feel like stored material breaking up and future suffering decreasing ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S5-S8).

[[The Hockey Stick Metaphor and Exponential Growth on the Spiritual Path]]
adds the long-haul version of the same reward model. Shinzen says early
practice may look merely linear and discouraging, but when the taste of
purification becomes tangible, CCE, especially equanimity with present
experience, can become a positive feedback loop: the sensed reduction of
future suffering and clearing of past pain motivates more practice, which
supports more growth ([[The Hockey Stick Metaphor and Exponential Growth on
the Spiritual Path]] S1-S6).

[[Depth and Breadth of Concentration - Part 1 of 3]] adds an oral example of the concentration side of this cycle. Shinzen says boredom can lose its force when a practitioner can taste samadhi while waiting or doing a repetitive task, and he treats simple temple tasks as a bridge from seated concentration into broader life activity ([[Depth and Breadth of Concentration - Part 1 of 3]] S3-S6).

[[When Sensory Experience Loses Its Something-ness]] adds the householder
version of the same reward loop. Shinzen describes waiting, sickness,
banality, and ordinary daily tasks as "secret times" when concentration can
be cultivated, then frames ego death as something householders amortize over
long periods rather than force through severe monastic conditions ([[When
Sensory Experience Loses Its Something-ness]] S1, S7).

[[Digging Holes]] adds the strategy-choice version of the fun cycle. Shinzen
closes the one-deep-hole versus broad-backhoe comparison by saying to use the
strategy that is fun, interesting, and productive ([[Digging Holes]] S7).
Inside Practice Cycles, that is a motivation and fit signal, not a full
safety test: fun can keep practice alive, but it still needs continuity,
clarity, behavior, and support checks.

## Boundaries
The cycles are not complete dosage rules. Unit 08 gives basic stillness and
motion minimums, and the micro-hits FAQ gives the practical target of at
least a half-dozen brief daily contacts, but the sources do not say how
intense retreat should be, what counts as safe equivalence, how much motion
or life practice is enough for different risk profiles, or how to dose
accelerators.

Teacher or coach check-in raises competence questions. Chapter 12 names a competent coach, but does not define competence, ethics, supervision, trauma literacy, or scope of practice.

Fun and interest are not the only criteria. Enjoyment can support practice, but it can also bias a practitioner toward easy, pleasant, or identity-confirming options. Unit 08's "not a big deal" advice reduces choice anxiety, and the session-setup talk validates staying with one method, but choice still needs safety, support, and outcome feedback when distress, avoidance, intensification, or behavior is involved.

Activity practice needs the same outcome feedback. A practitioner who is in
the zone during ballet, piano, sport, running, or singing has not thereby
shown that the activity is functioning as meditation; the source's test is
whether baseline concentration in ordinary daily life rises consistently
([[Can Ordinary Experience Count as Meditation]] S2-S5).

Nonlinear growth is also not a promise. The hockey-stick talk explicitly says
the pattern is common rather than universal, gives no measurement criteria,
and should not be used to turn purification taste into an attainment label or
timeline expectation ([[The Hockey Stick Metaphor and Exponential Growth on
the Spiritual Path]] S1-S6).

Looping and branching can optimize effort, but it can also feed choice-confusion or constant self-adjustment. A fixed workout can bring balance, but it can also overexpose a practitioner to deconstructive methods, turn Source/Gone into a required station, or use Focus on Positive to bypass unfinished material. Those risks route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

Retreat is also not automatically appropriate. Later safety work should specify when retreat, silence, intensive noting, Do Nothing, Gone, or Flow might be destabilizing and require modification or support.

Post-retreat effects also are not success or failure markers by themselves.
Afterglow, aftershock, both, or neither may follow a retreat; Shinzen's own
evaluation standard is whether daily life shows more fulfillment, less
suffering, and better behavior ([[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness
Retreat]] S1-S7).

The April daylong adds a narrow task-safety limit. Shinzen treats the
transition from formal practice to life as safety-sensitive and restricts
driving practice to sensory attention to driving, not altered, inward, or
Source-facing practice ([[Shinzen April Daylong Retreat - Four Quadrant
Training]] S12).

The June daylong repeats and sharpens that limit. After a retreat full of
Auto Move, Space, and Source-facing language, Shinzen still restricts driving
or heavy machinery to sensory driving practice ([[Four Ways Forward - June
Shinzen Retreat]] S14).

The July daylong repeats the same transition boundary after spaciousness and
no-self material. Retreat aftereffects may be carried into movement, talk,
micro-hits, and background practice, but driving is narrowed to seeing,
hearing, and feeling the drive ([[Shinzen Young Day-Long Retreat at the
Monastic Academy - July 22, 2017]] S15).

Trigger practice also needs stronger load criteria. [[Organizing Your
Practice]] says the practitioner can control stimulus type, intensity,
duration, timing, and frequency and should work near an edge rather than
overburdening the system, but it gives no trauma, panic, dissociation,
medical, clinical, or interpersonal-safety contraindications
([[Organizing Your Practice]] S5-S6).

Challenge sequences also need task-safety criteria. The source says to work
up toward complicated circumstances, but it does not define which activities
should not be combined with explicit technique, when divided attention is
unsafe, or when life practice becomes compulsive self-monitoring rather than
ordinary-life integration.

Retroactive meditation adds a different task-safety edge. The method assumes
some activities should not carry explicit technique while they are underway;
the practitioner preserves the task, stops only when stopping is safe, then
practices with the activation the task leaves behind. Its strong claim that a
short high-quality contact can process prior activity should not be used to
replace formal practice or justify low-quality micro-hits ([[Retroactive
Meditation]] S1-S6).

Samadhi-to-function practice has the complementary edge. The short source
supports action arising from a clear state, but it gives no criteria for
driving, tools, care duties, conflict, professional roles, or clinical
instability. Its forceful oral phrase about acting through fear should not
override task safety, grounding, ordinary responsiveness, or external support
when disorientation is present ([[There's No Need to Leave Samadhi]] S3-S6).

Conversation practice narrows that same task-safety rule. The interaction
must leave enough room for practice; if the other person, role, conflict, or
situation needs full attention, Shinzen's own CPU rule routes away from
explicit technique and back to ordinary conversation
([[Mindfulness Strategies when Interacting with Others]] S1-S3).

Unplanned crisis-as-practice needs even sharper boundaries. The Windows/Walls
talk treats life intensity as potential practice, but it does not define when
illness, violence, grief, betrayal, or harmful conduct should route first to
medical, legal, therapeutic, relational, or accountability support instead of
meditation optimization.

## Implications
Practice advice should ask where a method currently lives. A technique may be clear in stillness but absent in motion; useful in motion but unavailable during conflict; fun in formal practice but untested in behavior.

The cycles also give a default implementation check:

- Is there daily contact with practice?
- Does practice include stillness, motion, and life?
- Are micro-hits actually occurring during low-demand moments, or is life
  practice only an aspiration?
- When work is too demanding for continuous technique, is the practitioner
  using safe stop-on-a-dime intervals, or trying to split attention inside
  the task?
- Is the technique being trained through a gradual challenge sequence before
  being expected to hold in complicated circumstances?
- If carryover is weak, have trigger practice and motion challenges actually
  been tested at a manageable load?
- If an art, sport, or ordinary activity is being used as meditation, is
  there daily-life baseline-focus carryover, or only peak absorption during
  performance?
- Is formal practice best organized now as one technique, a workout sequence, or flexible branching?
- Is the longer strategy one deep hole, broad backhoe-like clearing, or
  unfocused method hopping?
- Are micro-hits or background practice present in ordinary life?
- Is there periodic intensive practice or retreat when appropriate?
- If a retreat just ended, is the practitioner interpreting afterglow,
  aftershock, both, or neither through daily-life behavior rather than state
  comparison?
- Is there competent feedback?
- If life has become intensely monastery-like without being chosen, is there
  competent facilitation plus ordinary support, or is practice language
  spiritualizing crisis?
- Are accelerators being used wisely rather than as strain or avoidance?
- Is the practitioner learning to enjoy the skills themselves?
