Practice Cycles translate Shinzen's techniques from a menu of options into maintained life practice. Chapter 11 names daily, yearly, and fun cycles; the See Hear Feel introduction adds focus-range application, explicit stillness-motion-life minimums, yearly support rhythm, and method-choice principles.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Practice is maintained by daily, yearly, accelerator, and fun rhythms, then applied through focus ranges, focus methods, and formal-session setup modes chosen for particular situations.
- **Why this matters**: The manual expects formal practice, movement practice, ordinary-life practice, retreat, teacher or coach feedback, intrinsic reward, and situational SHF application to reinforce one another.
- **Key tensions**: The sources give minimums and examples but do not specify retreat safety, coach competence, clinical boundaries, accelerator dosage, or when enjoyment and flexible method choice become avoidance.
- **Source posture**: Strong as manual application guidance; incomplete as a safety or scheduling protocol.

## Daily Cycle
The daily cycle has three venues: practice in stillness, practice in motion, and practice in life ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S5). Chapter 12 repeats this as one of the Ten Elements of Guidance: each focus option should be taught or practiced in all three venues ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S13).

SHF Unit 08 gives the most concrete version of the daily rhythm: formal stillness most days with a recommended minimum of 10 minutes, formal motion most days with the same recommended minimum, and life practice most days through at least 3-6 micro-hits plus background practice with no specified minimum ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S1).

The micro-hits FAQ turns the life venue into a concrete daily procedure.
Shinzen says the practitioner can "stop on a dime" when nothing too
demanding is happening and begin practice for 30 seconds, three minutes, or
even a few seconds, using a simple on-the-fly See-Hear-Feel noting sequence
as one example. He recommends peppering the day with at least a half-dozen
such brief practice contacts ([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]] S3-S5).

The venues imply different tests:

- **Stillness**: reduced external demand makes sensory clarity and concentration easier to stabilize.
- **Motion**: the body moves while the technique continues, testing portability.
- **Life**: ordinary situations, behavior, and relationships become the practice field.

This is the manual's antidote to a seated-only interpretation. A focus option is not fully integrated until it has some expression in ordinary activity.

The same FAQ adds a graded version of that test. A challenge sequence takes
one technique from seated eyes-closed practice into eyes-open sitting,
standing, slow movement, more impactful movement environments, simple tasks,
and eventually demanding tasks. Shinzen compares this to gradually adding
weight or repetitions in training, so life practice develops by progressive
load rather than by an immediate demand for continuous mindfulness
([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]]
S6-S7).

## Focus-Range Application
The See Hear Feel introduction adds a compact implementation distinction. SHF affects happiness generically through regular continued practice that raises baseline CCE, and specifically through versions of the technique chosen for particular situations ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S8).

The source calls the intentionally selected set of categories a "focus range." Beginners are expected to explore four ranges: visual experience, auditory experience, body experience, and any/all experience ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S9). The examples are practical: Focus Out can turn a boring task into sensory engagement, Focus on Rest can support a good night's rest even when sleep is imperfect, and Focus In can deconstruct severe emotional distress into image, talk, and emotional body sensation ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S8).

This links practice cycles to situational routing. A cycle is not only repeated sitting; it is the repeated capacity to select a workable range when ordinary life presents boredom, sleeplessness, distress, pleasure, pain, confusion, or behavior pressure.

## Formal Method Choice
Unit 08 adds three ways to organize formal practice. A practitioner may mostly use one focus method, run a set sequence, or freely shift among methods ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S4).

The shorter YouTube session-setup talk gives the same triad in Basic Mindfulness language. The simplest mode is to choose one technique and reduce choice load. The middle mode is to make a psycho-spiritual workout sequence out of the Five Ways. The most complex mode is to choose a theme, stay with it, or switch when interest, opportunity, or necessity points elsewhere ([[Three Ways to Set Up Your Basic Mindfulness Session]] S1-S7).

Shinzen's sample workout sequence is Focus In, Focus Out, note restful states, Focus on Change, Do Nothing, then Focus on Positive. He presents Focus on Positive as a reconstructive close after deconstructive practice, like a jacuzzi after exercise; that metaphor is useful for sequencing but does not supply dosage or safety criteria ([[Three Ways to Set Up Your Basic Mindfulness Session]] S3-S5).

This choice should be light but not random. Shinzen says method choice can be motivated by interest, opportunity, and necessity; should not be driven by craving, aversion, or unconsciousness; and need not be made into a big deal because every option trains concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S5).

The example sequence is diagnostically rich: a session can move from Focus on Everything to Rest, then to a customized Focus In with strong labels, inclusive emphasis, zooming, and explicit Gone during anger, and finally to Nurture Positive using Flow to radiate lovingkindness ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S6). The source presents this as flexible routing, not a proof that every shift is wise or safe.

## Yearly Cycle
The yearly cycle includes maintaining the daily cycle, periodic retreat, and periodic check-in with a teacher ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S5). Chapter 12's detailed formulation names at least one retreat and at least one check-in with a competent coach as part of the year-to-year cycle ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S4).

Unit 08 sharpens that into a season-to-season rhythm: at least one week-long residential retreat or equivalent each year, plus at least one yearly competent-coach check-in. It also says monthly participation in the Home Practice Program for a year counts as a week-long residential equivalent for many people ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S2).

The retreat-aftercare talk gives the same rhythm as a five-part continuity
rule after intensive practice: a little real formal practice most days, a lot
of practice every once in a while, practice-in-life micro-hits, regular
contact with someone competent enough to monitor the big picture, and keeping
the whole pattern up for the long haul ([[What to Expect and Do After a
Mindfulness Retreat]] S8). Shinzen's post-retreat minimum is "ludicrously
small" but quality-protected: if the formal sit is only 10 minutes, use
spoken labels so the short session is actual practice rather than vague
relaxation ([[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness Retreat]] S8).

The same unit names optional accelerators: duration training, trigger
practice, and motion challenge sequences ([[See Hear Feel Introduction -
Practice Organization and System Transition]] S3). The micro-hits FAQ gives
challenge sequences their first oral definition in this wiki: gradually add
life complexity while maintaining the technique, with formal-practice
momentum, micro-hit "surgical strikes," and progressive challenge training
working together toward the figure-ground reversal where life happens inside
meditation ([[Intermediate FAQ Practice In Daily Life Micro-Hits & Challenge Sequences]] S8-S9). Because neither source gives stop rules, challenge
sequences remain an accelerator pointer rather than a full safety protocol.

This strengthens the support ecology. Basic Mindfulness is modular, but the final manual does not present self-guided practice as the whole support system. Retreat and teacher or coach feedback are normal parts of maintaining the practice.

The Windows/Walls talk adds an unplanned intensive counterpart. Shinzen says most people will not create monastic intensity through traditional retreat, but life may bring injury, illness, betrayal, violence, grief, or moral failure. When the practitioner has internalized the method vocabulary and a trained facilitator can guide the branching, such events may become intensive practice conditions rather than merely disruptions ([[Maximizing Psycho-Spiritual Growth with an Algorithmic Approach (Windows & Walls)]] S4-S6). This is not a substitute for retreat or support; it is a high-risk life-practice claim that depends on guidance and ordinary safety response.

## Fun Cycle
The fun cycle is the manual's motivation model. Chapter 11 says the practitioner should have as much fun as possible by finding something interesting, enjoyable, or productive to focus on and by detecting the distinct reward flavors of concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S6).

Chapter 12 makes the cycle recursive: enjoyment supports skill, skill supports more enjoyment, and more enjoyment supports yet more skill ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S4). This keeps motivation from depending only on discipline, belief, or suffering relief.

The reward flavors belong to [[Mindfulness Skill Triad]]. Concentration can be intrinsically rewarding; sensory clarity can have the taste of crispness or deeper processing; equanimity can feel like stored material breaking up and future suffering decreasing ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S5-S8).

[[Depth and Breadth of Concentration - Part 1 of 3]] adds an oral example of the concentration side of this cycle. Shinzen says boredom can lose its force when a practitioner can taste samadhi while waiting or doing a repetitive task, and he treats simple temple tasks as a bridge from seated concentration into broader life activity ([[Depth and Breadth of Concentration - Part 1 of 3]] S3-S6).

## Boundaries
The cycles are not complete dosage rules. Unit 08 gives basic stillness and
motion minimums, and the micro-hits FAQ gives the practical target of at
least a half-dozen brief daily contacts, but the sources do not say how
intense retreat should be, what counts as safe equivalence, how much motion
or life practice is enough for different risk profiles, or how to dose
accelerators.

Teacher or coach check-in raises competence questions. Chapter 12 names a competent coach, but does not define competence, ethics, supervision, trauma literacy, or scope of practice.

Fun and interest are not the only criteria. Enjoyment can support practice, but it can also bias a practitioner toward easy, pleasant, or identity-confirming options. Unit 08's "not a big deal" advice reduces choice anxiety, and the session-setup talk validates staying with one method, but choice still needs safety, support, and outcome feedback when distress, avoidance, intensification, or behavior is involved.

Looping and branching can optimize effort, but it can also feed choice-confusion or constant self-adjustment. A fixed workout can bring balance, but it can also overexpose a practitioner to deconstructive methods or use Focus on Positive to bypass unfinished material. Those risks route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

Retreat is also not automatically appropriate. Later safety work should specify when retreat, silence, intensive noting, Do Nothing, Gone, or Flow might be destabilizing and require modification or support.

Post-retreat effects also are not success or failure markers by themselves.
Afterglow, aftershock, both, or neither may follow a retreat; Shinzen's own
evaluation standard is whether daily life shows more fulfillment, less
suffering, and better behavior ([[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness
Retreat]] S1-S7).

Challenge sequences also need task-safety criteria. The source says to work
up toward complicated circumstances, but it does not define which activities
should not be combined with explicit technique, when divided attention is
unsafe, or when life practice becomes compulsive self-monitoring rather than
ordinary-life integration.

Unplanned crisis-as-practice needs even sharper boundaries. The Windows/Walls
talk treats life intensity as potential practice, but it does not define when
illness, violence, grief, betrayal, or harmful conduct should route first to
medical, legal, therapeutic, relational, or accountability support instead of
meditation optimization.

## Implications
Practice advice should ask where a method currently lives. A technique may be clear in stillness but absent in motion; useful in motion but unavailable during conflict; fun in formal practice but untested in behavior.

The cycles also give a default implementation check:

- Is there daily contact with practice?
- Does practice include stillness, motion, and life?
- Are micro-hits actually occurring during low-demand moments, or is life
  practice only an aspiration?
- Is the technique being trained through a gradual challenge sequence before
  being expected to hold in complicated circumstances?
- Is formal practice best organized now as one technique, a workout sequence, or flexible branching?
- Are micro-hits or background practice present in ordinary life?
- Is there periodic intensive practice or retreat when appropriate?
- If a retreat just ended, is the practitioner interpreting afterglow,
  aftershock, both, or neither through daily-life behavior rather than state
  comparison?
- Is there competent feedback?
- If life has become intensely monastery-like without being chosen, is there
  competent facilitation plus ordinary support, or is practice language
  spiritualizing crisis?
- Are accelerators being used wisely rather than as strain or avoidance?
- Is the practitioner learning to enjoy the skills themselves?
