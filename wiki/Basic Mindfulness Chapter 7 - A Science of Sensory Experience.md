Chapter 7 of the Basic Mindfulness manual makes the Sensory Grid explicit. It presents See/Hear/Feel crossed with In/Out/Rest/Flow as a simple but ambitious classification system for the "atoms" of sensory experience.

- **Date**: undated in the raw Markdown.

- **Scope**: Defines the desired properties of a sensory classification system, defends the 12-cell Sensory Grid, handles smell/taste and Feel In/Feel Out ambiguities, and distinguishes descriptive from labeling language.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Human sensory states are numerous, variable, partly controllable, and behaviorally consequential; a good classification of sensory states could help people understand experience and objective behavior.
- **S2**: A scientific classification of the senses should be comprehensive, simple, natural, deep, useful, and beautiful.
- **S3**: The Sensory Grid's 12 basic categories are See In, See Out, See Rest, See Flow; Hear In, Hear Out, Hear Rest, Hear Flow; Feel In, Feel Out, Feel Rest, and Feel Flow.
- **S4**: Shinzen presents the grid as useful because it can help reduce suffering, increase fulfillment through Rest and Flow, and enable behavior change by deconstructing negative urges and reinforcing positive intentions.
- **S5**: Smell and taste are classified as Feel Out because they occur through the body, specifically nose and mouth, even though this may feel artificial.
- **S6**: Emotional body sensation is operationally detected by asking whether any emotion is present and whether part of that emotion appears in the body; if the answer is no or uncertain, the practitioner treats it as no Feel In for that moment.
- **S7**: "Out" in the label system means physical in origin rather than external in location, which explains why physical body sensations can be Feel Out even when they occur inside the body.
- **S8**: The grid has two language modes: descriptive language for discussing experience and short rhythmic labels for practice.

## Summary
The chapter uses the periodic table as an analogy. Just as chemistry organizes many substances through a smaller set of elements arranged by rows and columns, Basic Mindfulness proposes a compact table for sensory experience. The rows are modalities: visual, auditory, and somatic. The columns are themes: inwardly arising, outwardly arising, restful, and flowing.

The source claims the resulting grid is comprehensive and useful enough to analyze ordinary and altered sensory states. It also acknowledges artificialities. Smell and taste are folded into Feel Out. Emotional body sensation can be hard to distinguish from physical body sensation, so Shinzen gives an operational test rather than asking practitioners to solve the distinction metaphysically. "Feel In" and "Feel Out" are admitted to be awkward labels, and alternatives such as "Feel Emotional" and "Feel Physical" are allowed.

The chapter's most reusable contribution is the distinction between descriptive and labeling language. "Mental image" and "See In" point to the same box; one is better for explanation, the other for practice.

## Source Frame
The source wants the reader to see the grid as simple, symmetrical, and practical rather than arbitrary jargon. Its scientific rhetoric is aspirational. It borrows the prestige and elegance of the periodic table to frame Shinzen's taxonomy as a workable "science of sensory experience."

The strongest material is procedural and definitional: the 12 cells, the In/Out convention, the treatment of smell and taste, and the operational detection rule for emotional body sensation. The weakest material is the claim that this grid is comprehensive or scientific in a strong empirical sense.

## Weakest Claims
- The periodic table analogy may overstate the grid's scientific status.
- The claim that all important sensory experience can be analyzed into these 12 atoms is useful as a practice convention but not independently established here.
- "Our behaviors are controlled by our sensory states" is too broad without developmental, social, ethical, and environmental qualifications.
- The source says the grid seems comprehensive while also acknowledging that some categories feel artificial.

## Important Omissions
- The chapter does not provide empirical validation of the grid.
- It does not discuss edge cases such as synesthesia, hallucination, trauma flashbacks, depersonalization, panic, or medical sensory symptoms.
- It does not give criteria for when sensory classification becomes over-effortful, dissociative, or bypassing.
- It does not explain how social, cognitive, ethical, or relational factors interact with sensory-state classification.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The source values naturalness but admits three unnatural or artificial-seeming features: chemical senses, emotion-body distinction, and Feel In/Feel Out language.
- The source frames the grid as simple while also asking practitioners to classify ambiguous emotional and physical body events.
- The grid is presented as comprehensive, but smell and taste are compressed into somatic experience rather than receiving distinct categories.
- The behavior-change claim is plausible inside Shinzen's system but could be overpromoted if separated from action, feedback, and support.
