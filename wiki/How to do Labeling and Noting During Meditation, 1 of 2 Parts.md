## Teaching Register
This is procedural instruction with a troubleshooting frame. Shinzen is not introducing a new technique; he is showing how the options inside Noting can be used before a guide has to invent special exercises.

## Why This Talk Matters
Gate 1 already has entry aims, CCE assumptions, path fit, focus coverage, and formal-session setup. ## Key Claims / Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: A typical act of Noting has two parts: a brief clear acknowledgement that a sensory category or event is present, then a few seconds of intense focusing.
- **S2**: If the acknowledged event immediately disappears, the ordinary focus phase cannot happen; Shinzen treats that disappearance, regardless of whether acknowledgement caused it, as important insight into vanishing.
- **S3**: The default ideal is no verbal label, just direct clarity about the category with momentum; because this is often difficult, mental or spoken labels are legitimate supports and practitioners should not fault themselves for needing them.
- **S4**: The Noting apparatus has option categories for labeling, spatial direction during the focus phase, and pacing; these options can partially support concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity, though those skills are precisely what practice is trying to build.
- **S5**: Shinzen's first troubleshooting questions are which technique the practitioner is using and which options they have tried; if the ordinary options do not help, then guidance can become more creative or specialized.
- **S6**: Acknowledgement-phase options include speaking a label aloud, thinking a label mentally, using no label, and, for some people, finger labeling.
- **S7**: Spoken labels can be weak or whispered, medium or ordinary, or strong, where the practitioner forces a clear, crisp, audible label stream and listens to ensure it does not stop.
- **S8**: Shinzen recommends a gentle, matter-of-fact, almost impersonal tone for mental or spoken labels because the tone can help induce equanimity; his phrase for this is "Use the voice."
- **S9**: The equanimity voice does not require suppressing expression: Shinzen says a person may be crying, screaming, or flailing while keeping the label voice gentle and matter-of-fact.
- **S10**: Pacing should be neither too fast nor too slow; too fast can become frenetic, too slow can become spacey, and very rapid sensory streams can be averaged into one note every few seconds.
- **S11**: If the noting rhythm naturally entrains with breathing and works, let it happen; do not try to manufacture it or prevent it.
- **S12**: Repeating the same note can slow the internal clock and make detection easier; "Repeat until Gone" stays with the same event until some abrupt subsidence appears and is then noted as Gone.

## Practice Handles
- Begin from the acknowledge-focus rhythm rather than from labels alone.
- Try direct no-label tracking when clarity and momentum are stable, but use mental or spoken labels when direct tracking becomes vague.
- Escalate label strength when the practice is "going up a hill": whispered or subvocal, ordinary spoken, or strong spoken labels.
- Use the tone of the label stream as practice equipment: gentle, matter-of-fact, impersonal tone is meant to support equanimity.
- Adjust pace by effect: slow down when frantic, speed up when spacing out, and average rapid-fire events rather than chase every event.
- Let breath synchronization occur naturally if it helps, but do not make breath entrainment a target.
- Repeat the same note when continuity with an event is more useful than moving on.
- Use repeat-until-Gone when an event is presenting in a way that makes abrupt subsidence detectable.
- In guidance, ask which Noting options have already been tried before inventing special exercises.

## Summary
Shinzen defines Noting as a rhythm of acts. Each act usually starts with clear acknowledgement that some sensory event or category is present, followed by a few seconds of intense focusing. The exception is important: sometimes the event disappears immediately after acknowledgement, and then the disappearance itself becomes insight into vanishings.

The talk then separates Noting from labeling. Shinzen says the fallback ideal is direct clear awareness without even a mental label, but he immediately lowers performance pressure: most practitioners need labels much of the time, and even he finds no-label tracking hard work. Labeling therefore becomes an adjustable support, not a purity test.

The main teaching body is the options apparatus. Label mode may be none, mental, spoken, or finger-based. Spoken labels may be weak, ordinary, or strong. The label voice should be gentle, matter-of-fact, and almost impersonal; Shinzen says this artificial tone often helps induce real equanimity, even during intense emotional or physical distress. Pacing should avoid both frenetic speed and spacey slowness. Rapid streams may be averaged, breath entrainment may be allowed if spontaneous, repeated notes can slow the internal clock, and repeat-until-Gone can be used when an event can be followed into abrupt subsidence.

## Source Frame

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that an artificial equanimous voice usually induces consciousness itself to drop into equanimity is a practice heuristic, not an independently evidenced outcome claim.
- The example of using labels amid major physical or emotional freakouts is powerful but under-specified for trauma, panic, medical pain, dissociation, shame, compulsion, or unsafe settings.
- The claim that internalizing these variations should deal with the bulk of challenges is plausible inside Shinzen's system but not a complete troubleshooting, clinical, or teacher-competence model.
- The no-label default could be misread as a purity standard even though Shinzen explicitly normalizes needing labels.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- No-label direct awareness is the default ideal, but labels are often needed for actual clarity and momentum; this is best read as a gear model rather than a hierarchy of practitioner worth.
- Strong spoken labels may restore feedback, but forcing labels can also become strain, exposure, or performance pressure if misapplied.
- The equanimity voice can coexist with crying, screaming, or flailing; this helps distinguish equanimity from suppression, but it also raises support and safety questions.
- Repeat-until-Gone trains vanishing detection, while the wider Gone model warns against hunting for disappearance instead of meeting arisings completely.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not treat no-label practice as inherently superior when it leads to vague drifting.
- Do not force strong labels as a universal answer to distress, resistance, or attention difficulty.
- Do not treat a calm label tone as proof that the practitioner is not suppressing, dissociating, or overriding a need for support.
- Do not make breath entrainment into a goal; Shinzen explicitly says not to try to make it happen.
- Do not use repeat-until-Gone to chase absence or avoid complete contact with what is arising.
