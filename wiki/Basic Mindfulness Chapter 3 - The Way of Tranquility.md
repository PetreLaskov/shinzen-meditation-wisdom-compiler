Chapter 3 of the Basic Mindfulness manual gives the tranquility route: cultivate restful sensory experience through Noting, or drop the intention to direct attention through Do Nothing.

- **Origin**: Shinzen Young, Basic Mindfulness manual.
- **Date**: undated in the raw Markdown.
- **Format**: Practice-manual chapter converted to Markdown.
- **Reliability**: Primary source for Basic Mindfulness rest practice and Do Nothing instructions. Strong for technique definitions and routing; weaker for neuroscience, comparative religion, chronic-illness outcome claims, and Source metaphysics.
- **Scope**: Defines Relative Rest and Absolute Rest, gives instructions for See Rest, Hear Rest, Feel Rest, Focus on Rest, and Do Nothing, and explains why rest belongs in the sensory grid.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Tranquility practices develop concentration by focusing on simple soothing experience, creating a positive feedback loop where rest deepens concentration and concentration deepens rest.
- **S2**: The chapter distinguishes Relative Rest from Absolute Rest: Relative Rest finds or creates visual, auditory, and somatic rest; Absolute Rest drops the intention to turn attention anywhere.
- **S3**: Relative Rest is developed through Noting, while Absolute Rest is developed through Do Nothing; the distinction is not a value ranking because each approach teaches something different.
- **S4**: See Rest works with visual rest: eyes-closed grayscale blank or eyes-open soft focus. Patches of eyes-closed visual rest may vanish and be noted as Gone.
- **S5**: Hear Rest works with mental quiet or physical silence. Silence can be heard directionally, found in gaps in mental talk, or sometimes created by listening to white noise; if auditory rest is unavailable, the practitioner should use another rest option.
- **S6**: Feel Rest works with physical relaxation and emotional peace, including both finding rest that is present and creating rest through settling, relaxing body parts, or breathing into emotional sensations without beating them down.
- **S7**: Focus on Rest floats among See Rest, Hear Rest, and Feel Rest and can use exclusive noting, inclusive noting, pair labels, or All Rest; it extends the drill-down/back-up structure used in earlier Ways.
- **S8**: Do Nothing has two basic instructions: let whatever happens happen, and whenever one is aware of an intention to control attention, drop that intention.
- **S9**: Do Nothing asks the practitioner to drop voluntary attention-direction, not to drop sensory experiences, suppress thoughts, continuously check for intentions, or struggle to eliminate intentions that cannot be dropped.
- **S10**: A perfect Do Nothing session may be unpleasant, unproductive, or filled with Monkey Mind; if Do Nothing makes one too spacey, Shinzen recommends Noting for a while, and if Noting makes one too racy, he recommends Do Nothing for a while.
- **S11**: The significance of Rest includes concentration growth, equanimity as a container for active states, daily micro-fulfillment, circumstance-independent contentment, shamatha-vipashyana yuganaddha, clarity around "nothing much," gentleness, easier detection of Flow, and "being meditated" through rest-to-rest feedback loops.
- **S12**: The chapter closes with a nonpreference warning: restful states may be used as doorways when available, but when unavailable the practitioner should become fascinated with active sights, sounds, body sensations, thoughts, and emotions.

## Summary
The chapter presents tranquility as a full Basic Mindfulness Way, not merely as relaxation. Its first route is Relative Rest: the practitioner learns to detect or create visual rest, auditory rest, and somatic rest, then note those restful flavors with concentration, clarity, and equanimity. See Rest uses closed-eye blankness or open-eye defocus. Hear Rest uses physical silence, mental quiet, directional absence of sound, or sometimes white noise. Feel Rest uses physical relaxation, emotional peace, and emotional neutrality.

Focus on Rest backs up from individual rest flavors into a broader rest field. Shinzen distinguishes exclusive noting, where the practitioner chooses one available rest flavor, from inclusive noting, where several restful flavors can be noted together as pairs or All Rest. This matters because rest is not only a calming object; the chapter says discriminating rest flavors lets a practitioner go deep while still tracking details.

The second route is Absolute Rest through Do Nothing. Do Nothing is short in instruction but subtle in interpretation: let whatever happens happen, and drop a noticed voluntary intention to control attention. The practitioner is not asked to drop experiences, suppress thought, keep checking for intentions, make the session restful, or struggle to eliminate uncontrollable intentions. Shinzen explicitly allows that the technique may look like Monkey Mind and says to use another option when it does not function well.

The significance section is unusually dense. Rest builds concentration through positive feedback, creates a container for active states, makes practice gentler, supports daily fulfillment independent of conditions, and integrates samatha with vipassana. It also points toward later Flow and no-self: restful self and world are described as attenuated, porous versions of active self and world, making them easier to penetrate with clarity. The chapter ends by preserving nonpreference: rest is a doorway when available, not a superior object to active sensory life.

## Source Frame
The source is a manual chapter trying to rescue tranquility from two opposite reductions: mere relaxation and state-chasing. It gives concrete rest instructions while repeatedly placing rest inside CCE and nonpreference. Its strongest material is procedural: how to work with See Rest, Hear Rest, Feel Rest, Focus on Rest, and Do Nothing.

Its broader interpretive claims need caution. The chapter mentions alpha waves, many cross-tradition names for Do Nothing, chronic illness and severe limitation as contexts where rest-based fulfillment becomes crucial, and Source/Groundless Ground language around Do Nothing. These frames are valuable for understanding Shinzen's teaching ecology, but this file does not establish the neuroscience, comparative religion, clinical safety, or metaphysics.

- "Anyone can do Do Nothing perfectly" is pedagogically useful but could obscure whether the technique is functioning well for a given practitioner.

## Important Omissions

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The chapter invites practitioners to enjoy and cultivate rest while also warning against preference for restful states over active ones.
- Do Nothing is presented as no technique but still contains a subtle instruction to drop noticed voluntary intention.
- The guideline "if Do Nothing makes you too spacey, try Noting" sits in tension with a later encouragement to let go of intentions to fix dissociation or confusion during Do Nothing.
- Creating rest through relaxation and breathing into emotion must be distinguished from manipulating experience or "beating down" emotional sensation.
- Rest can deepen practice and also become state-seeking if not balanced by clarity, active-sense fascination, and behavioral outcomes.
