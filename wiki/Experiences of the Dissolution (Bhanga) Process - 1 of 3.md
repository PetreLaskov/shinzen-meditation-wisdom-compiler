## Practice Handles
When a practitioner reports dissolution-like terror, do not start with status or metaphysics. Ask what is happening in body sensation, emotional body space, image space, and talk space; ask whether Flow can be detected; and check whether noting is possible or whether a softer Flow focus is the available handle.

The case also suggests that severe bhanga reports should trigger guide contact, grounding checks, and clinical caution. Shinzen's frame can interpret the process as purification, but the transcript does not supply criteria for when terror, disorientation, derealization, shame, or death imagery require stopping, therapy, medical assessment, or emergency support.

## Summary
The student describes leaving spiritual practice after bad experiences with teachers, then attending a Shinzen retreat in 2006. Nothing dramatic happened during the retreat itself, but two or three weeks later waves and impulses began in the solar plexus, along with childhood terrors, chaos, disorientation, and fear of going crazy. Friends advised him to call Shinzen, who asked specific questions, helped isolate sensations, and interpreted the process as purification and dissolution.

The student's later description emphasizes harsh Flow: pounding, twisting, morphing, tingling, incoherent thoughts, unfamiliarity, primordial terror, animal-like survival fear, images of being ripped apart, collapsed sight-space, shame, guilt, and fear of being killed. During a retreat recurrence, the student sometimes used noting, but when the process was too frightening he used Focus on Flow and named the experience harsh Flow.

Shinzen then restates the bhanga model. Some dissolution is blissful, like pleasant vibratory bubbles or massage through body and mind. At the opposite extreme, some dissolution is horrific: jarring Flow, archetypal images, monsters, death, animals eating the practitioner, uncomfortable touch and Feel, tearing energies, and chaotic or irrational talk. Some practitioners have both sides, and some do not pass through dissolution at all.

## Source Frame
The source is strong evidence for how Shinzen handles a severe bhanga report in live teaching. It is weaker as evidence about what objectively caused the student's experience, whether the same report would meet clinical criteria for trauma activation, panic, dissociation, depersonalization/derealization, or another condition, and how shamanic traditions understand comparable imagery.

Shinzen's frame is recognitional and practice-oriented: frightening phenomena are parsed into sensory channels and Flow, linked to purification, and placed inside a non-universal bhanga map. The frame should be preserved, but not used as a stand-alone diagnosis or permission to continue unsupported distress.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen frames the student's process as positive purification while the reported phenomenology includes fear of insanity, unfamiliar surroundings, hiding at home, shame, guilt, and fear of being killed.
- Flow is usually a practice gateway into impermanence and integration, but here Flow is explicitly harsh, jarring, and frightening.
- The shamanic comparison may normalize death imagery, but normalization can also hide clinical, trauma, or safety differentials if overused.

## Easily Misused Claims
- A practitioner or teacher could overuse "bhanga" to spiritualize trauma activation, derealization, panic, psychosis risk, or medical symptoms.
- The phrase "positive experience" could be repeated without the accompanying guide contact, sensory isolation, and safety caution.
- Archetypal death imagery could be romanticized as advanced practice status rather than treated as intense material requiring support.
