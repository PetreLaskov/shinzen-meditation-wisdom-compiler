Impermanence is not just the doctrine that things pass away. In Shinzen's system it is the trainable recognition that every sensory event has changingness, and that changingness can mature into Flow, Gone, purification, unification, and Source-wave freedom.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Ordinary changingness becomes liberating only when concentration, sensory clarity, equanimity, and time make it intimate, continuous, and behaviorally relevant.
- **Why this matters**: Impermanence is the bridge between the skill triad and Shinzen's deepest claims about Flow, no-self, purification, oneness, Source, and freedom in ordinary life.
- **Key tensions**: The same frame can empower practice or become energy-chasing, austerity romanticism, metaphysical overclaim, or bypass of pain, medicine, therapy, and behavior.
- **Source posture**: Strong as Shinzen's own transformation model; probable as an interpretation of his system; speculative when used as physics, theology, or clinical guidance.

## Model
The first face of impermanence is ordinary conditionality. Shinzen accepts the early Buddhist warning that suffering follows when all happiness is pinned on unstable conditions such as health, wealth, reputation, relationships, family, or peace ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S2). This is not his final frame. The chapter's distinctive move is to claim that impermanence also has a positive face: a lasting dimension of happiness available through direct contact with changingness itself ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S3).

The second face is self-as-process. The self that seems like a thing can be analyzed as fluctuating mental talk, mental image, and emotional body sensation. Shinzen therefore reads anatta as the self not being a fixed particle rather than as the simple denial that self appears at all ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S4). This connects impermanence directly to [[Inner Sensory System]] and later no-self material.

The third face is solidity becoming wave. With enough [[Sensory Clarity]], experience is investigated minutely; with enough [[Equanimity]], it is investigated patiently. Either route can reveal seemingly solid self and world as a dance of sensory motion ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S5). This makes impermanence an application of the [[Mindfulness Skill Triad]], not a belief about change.

[[Born Between Expansion and Contraction]] adds a refined temporal face:
passing can become salient as soon as arising begins, not only afterward.
Shinzen treats this passing-as-arising as a near-stream-entry map cue and as a
practice sensitivity to the usually missed passing side of events
([[Born Between Expansion and Contraction]] S1).

[[Paradigms of Change]] makes the change in impermanence paradigms explicit.
Shinzen contrasts early anicca as the suffering risk of clinging to passing
conditions, U Ba Khin-style body sweeping as activated impermanence that may
feel vibratory, bubbly, and purifying, and Sasaki Roshi's
Expansion-Contraction as a still subtler three-dimensional polarity frame
([[Paradigms of Change]] S1-S3, S5-S6).

[[Mindfulness Momentum, Arising and Passing to Simultaneous Expansion and
Contraction]] adds a practice-development condition to that paradigm
sequence. Shinzen says the triple skillset of concentration, clarity, and
equanimity may reach enough critical mass over weeks, months, and years that
the practitioner can choose to work with Expansion-Contraction in the senses
([[Mindfulness Momentum, Arising and Passing to Simultaneous Expansion and
Contraction]] S3). The same talk treats mature impermanence as both seeing
and manifesting: vipassana may begin by observing impermanence and later
riding it, while Zen may train riding before seeing; the mature endpoint
needs both ([[Mindfulness Momentum, Arising and Passing to Simultaneous
Expansion and Contraction]] S6).

[[Zen, Vipassana, and Becoming Impermanence]] isolates that style contrast.
Shinzen says impermanence can be more than a sensory characteristic: it can
present as qi-like vital energy that one rides. In his contrast, vipassana
moves slowly enough through deconstruction that one disappears, while Zen
moves quickly enough through spontaneity and non-fixation that one disappears;
both remain different expressions of the same enlightenment rather than a
tradition hierarchy ([[Zen, Vipassana, and Becoming Impermanence]] S1,
S3-S6).

## Developmental Faces
Chapter 6 gives a useful developmental sequence. Impermanence can first appear as trivial change: attention wandered, then something previously present is absent. With more concentration, change becomes continuous but may feel harsh, jagged, pounding, twisting, stabbing, or driven. As concentration, equanimity, and clarity mature, change can become smooth, fluid, bubbly, and comforting ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S6-S8).

That sequence matters for practice routing. Harsh impermanence is not yet failure; in Shinzen's frame it may be the rough form of the same movement that later becomes [[Flow]]. But the sequence should not be turned into an instruction to seek intensity. The chapter explicitly says heavy-duty practice is not required and that ordinary events such as an itch, ache, or smile can carry the path if met with radical completeness ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S13).

## Flow And Gone
[[Flow]] is Shinzen's name for the positive or activated side of impermanence. It is sensory change as movement, energy, force, vibration, undulation, or expansion-contraction, and it may come to feel like something that helps the practitioner along ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S8-S10). Chapter 6 gives Flow the roles of purifier, guide, and unifier: it can soften congealed experience, feed back when experience is approaching completeness, and reveal inner and outer sensory events as the same vibrating impermanence ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S11, S16-S17).

[[Gone]] is the complementary impermanence route focused on the noticed moment when an event vanishes, drops off, or abruptly diminishes. Chapter 6 emphasizes Flow more than Gone, but the Basic Mindfulness manual makes the pair explicit: Flow covers ongoing dynamic change, while Gone covers abrupt passing ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S8; [[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S5). Science of Enlightenment chapter 9 then gives Gone its strongest book treatment and says the mature broad definition is abrupt decrease, making Gone a special case of abrupt Flow ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 9 - The Power of Gone]] S5-S6).

[[Abrupt Flow Diminishings, Vanishings and Noting Gone]] makes that
vanishing route unusually ordinary. Breath endings, mental-talk sentence
endings, partial collapses, and temporary abrupt decreases are sufficient
practice objects; Shinzen's phrase "all Gones are uncreated equal" means the
small scale of an ending does not disqualify it as a Source-oriented practice
gateway ([[Abrupt Flow Diminishings, Vanishings and Noting Gone]] S1-S4).

Together, Flow and Gone prevent a one-sided model. Impermanence is both ongoing movement and disappearance, both energy and ending, both vitality and release. Chapter 9 adds that the path into passing depends on affirming arising quickly and completely through focus and equanimity, so impermanence practice is not a rejection of appearing experience ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 9 - The Power of Gone]] S7).

[[Flow, Gone and a Figure-Ground Reversal]] adds that Flow and Gone can also
reverse figure and ground. Instead of a stable observer watching change and
ending, the Flow-Gone rhythm can become the ground from which self and seeing
are momentarily born ([[Flow, Gone and a Figure-Ground Reversal]] S5).

Chapter 10 adds a deeper polarity formulation. Shinzen says early Buddhism usually expresses impermanence as arising and passing, but Expansion-Contraction is broader because it can reveal impermanence in stable phenomena as pressure forces rather than visible movement ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S7). It also avoids a simplistic mapping where arising equals expansion and passing equals contraction; in the deeper view, every arising already entails both, and Gone occurs when the polar forces cancel ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S4).

Chapter 7 adds an altered-phenomena face. Shinzen says visionary material can be tracked as Flow, expansion-contraction, fading, and vanishing, and that CCE may reveal impermanence as a creative Flow that animates both visionary and conventional reality. This strengthens the link between impermanence and the [[Intermediate Realm]], while keeping the claim phenomenological rather than ontological ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 7 - The Realm of Power]] S9-S10).

## Source Frame
The deepest chapter 6 claim is that impermanence is the activity of Source. Flow is ordinary experience under extraordinary concentration, clarity, and equanimity, not a special realm apart from ordinary sight, sound, body, thought, or emotion ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S14). If a practitioner wants only pleasant energy and avoids remaining solidity, practice becomes self-limiting because the next insight depends on repeatedly seeing ordinary solid events become Flow ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S14).

The chapter then moves into a speculative advanced frame. It says every experience is already Flow at a preconscious level, and liberation is the freedom to move between ordinary functional solidity and Source-wave perception rather than continuous altered-state absorption ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S15, S19). This belongs with [[Source And Polarities]] and should remain source-attributed when used beyond phenomenological practice.

Chapter 9 supplies the vanishing-side Source frame. Each Gone is said to point to the Uncreated or Source, but Source itself is not a sensory experience; it is indirectly contacted through vanishing, while the aftereffect of vanishing becomes clearer ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 9 - The Power of Gone]] S9). This keeps Source language attached to a practice event without treating it as an ordinary object.

Chapter 10 makes Source the contour of impermanence. A sensation can be followed from solidity to undulation, vibration, simultaneous Expansion-Contraction, cancellation, and repolarization until the practitioner identifies less with mind-body content and more with the field of impermanence itself ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S10). At the same time, the chapter says Source or Zero is known only as afterglow because no self experiences the cancellation directly ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S14-S15).

## Boundaries
Impermanence should not be treated as pessimism, nihilism, or a reason to devalue ordinary conditional happiness. Shinzen's claim is that conditional happiness becomes unsafe only when it is the sole basis of happiness.

Impermanence should also not become energy-chasing. Pleasant Flow, kundalini-style phenomena, bubbles, electricity, expansion, and vastness are not the goal; the goal is insight into ordinary experience when met completely ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S14).

Advanced impermanence maps should also not become comparison fuel.
[[Mindfulness Momentum, Arising and Passing to Simultaneous Expansion and
Contraction]] explicitly says describing possible experiences can motivate
and orient practice while also creating comparison mind, expectation, and
poisoned medicine ([[Mindfulness Momentum, Arising and Passing to
Simultaneous Expansion and Contraction]] S1-S2).

The chapter's comparisons with qi, acupuncture, kundalini, shakti, and Holy Spirit are cross-cultural experiential translations, not proof of a single historical doctrine or objective energy entity ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 6 - The Many Faces of Impermanence]] S12).

Finally, the chapter increases safety pressure. It says start small and that intense training is readiness-dependent, but it does not define readiness, medical stop rules, trauma boundaries, or teacher competence. Questions of pain, illness, deprivation, energy activation, and dying should route through [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

Chapter 9 increases the same safety pressure from the cessation side: intense Goneness, emptiness, and no-self may produce terror, paralysis, aversion, and hopelessness, and Gone-triggered [[Primordial Feel]] may bring chaotic body-emotion agitation ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 9 - The Power of Gone]] S13-S18).

Chapter 10 increases it from the Source side. [[Dissolution]] or bhanga may involve blissful Flow, harsh chaos, fear, disorientation, and loss of center, and the chapter names competent instruction and individual variation without giving full stop rules or clinical boundaries ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S16-S19).

Chapter 7 increases it from the power side. Impermanence can make visions, energy, bliss, light, and other altered phenomena workable, but fascination with those phenomena can become Diversion or spiritual materialism, and the source gives no clinical criteria for hallucination-like events ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 7 - The Realm of Power]] S5, S11-S12).

## Implications
If this concept is right inside Shinzen's system, practice can route impermanence through several doors:

- changingness of ordinary sights, sounds, body sensations, images, talk, and emotion;
- Flow as undulation, vibration, and expansion-contraction;
- Gone as detected passing;
- purification as the felt metabolizing of resistance and stored pattern;
- unification as inner and outer sensory activity becoming one wave;
- Source as the deepest interpretive frame for moment-by-moment arising and dissolving.

This makes impermanence a central transformation mechanism, not a side topic in Buddhist terminology.
