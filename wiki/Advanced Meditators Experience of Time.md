## Key Points
- **Core claim**: The dialogue frames altered time experience as practice-dependent: stronger concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity reduce stickiness, selfing, and drag so chronological activity can continue while the moment feels timeless or less imprisoning.
- **Why this matters**: It adds a time-specific bridge between Shinzen's Source/time-space language and more ordinary practice outcomes such as lighter mood, less reactivity, no-self in activity, and quiet joy.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Har-Prakash says altered experience of time depends on the strength of concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity; when those are high, experience has less grab or stickiness and is processed more fully as it occurs.
- **S2**: Har-Prakash distinguishes clock/calendar time and life responsibilities from the way one shows up inside activity; mature practice can make activity efficient and competent while also less caught in time.
- **S3**: Shinzen gives a compressed contrast: relative time can feel discrete and prison-like, while another mode feels continuous, smooth, not a prison, and related to light/space/spacetime; he speculatively links that subjective geometry to physics and possible future clinical neuroscience.

- **S5**: The dialogue separates energy expenditure from experiential drag: ordinary work such as chopping wood still requires effort, but it need not be heavy when joy, grace, or no-self in activity is present.
- **S6**: Har-Prakash describes selfing as a sliding scale; quiet joy can be present whether the sense of self is attenuated, absent, or relatively solid.
- **S7**: Har-Prakash proposes that detecting very early temporal processing may feel prior to clear See/Hear/Feel differentiation, giving a one-taste or nondual flavor while later ordinary processing still identifies events.
- **S8**: Todd and Har-Prakash connect subtle temporal processing with Nurture Positive: early perturbation or empty transparency may be naturally accompanied by wholesome qualities such as gentle joy, or those qualities may be intentionally evoked.

## Summary
Todd frames the conversation as an inquiry into how advanced practitioners and teachers experience time. Har-Prakash begins by relating timelessness to CCE strength: when concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity reach sufficient critical mass, experience is less sticky, less self-world-time-space bound, and more fully processed as it happens. He keeps this compatible with ordinary obligations: calendar time, farm work, and appointments remain, but the practitioner's relationship to them changes.

Shinzen's main contribution is brief and highly compressed. He contrasts relative time as discrete and prison-like with another experience of time that is continuous, smooth, and not a prison. He then describes that continuum as light/space/spacetime and launches into mathematical and physics analogies: manifolds, Schrodinger equations, projective geometry, subjective complex vector spaces, and possible measurement through clinical neuroscience. Because the transcript is poor and the claims are speculative, the durable value is the existence of Shinzen's Source/time-space idiom, not the details of the science.

After Shinzen drops from the call, Har-Prakash and Todd continue with the practice-facing material. Har-Prakash distinguishes aging-related retrospective time acceleration from moment-by-moment practice experience. He describes enlightenment as felt lightening: purification reduces the weight of unprocessed material, making depression, melancholy, drag, and selfing less dominant. They connect this to ordinary work, no-self in activity, early temporal processing, one-taste language, quiet joy, and the possibility that wholesome qualities can be naturally present or intentionally evoked.
