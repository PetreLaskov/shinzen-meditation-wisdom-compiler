## Practice Handles
- When a person, group, opponent, or problem appears, first identify the sensory mode by which it appears: sight, sound, touch, mental image, mental talk, or emotional Feel.
- Before optimizing the reaction, ask whether the perceived self/world pair can be sensed as arising from a Source-love context. If not, do not fake the state; continue with ordinary CCE, support, and appropriate action.
- Keep judgment and action available. "Love deeply" means the reaction is contextualized, not erased.
- For charged concepts or names, watch the momentary Image/Talk bursts that make meaning possible. The useful practice question is whether those bursts can be seen arising from and returning to emptiness or Source.
- Treat political, enemy, and sacred-text examples as practice objects, not as policy advice, moral equivalence claims, or proof that all conflicts have the same response.

## Summary
Shinzen begins with a question about making the world better. He says he
cannot help with political specifics, but he can offer a general principle:
love deeply and act effectively. Acting effectively may use mindfulness
skills and strategies, but it also requires other forms of knowledge and
competence. Loving deeply, however, depends critically on mindfulness because
it requires perceiving self and world at a subtler sensory level.

He then defines deep love through the development of complete inner and outer
See/Hear/Feel. As concentration, clarity, and equanimity mature, experience
becomes richer while fixation becomes briefer. At the limit, he describes
experience as maximally rich and utterly vacuous: absolute rest where time
and space collapse, followed by expansion and contraction like a "space
fountain." This Source-side context, in his framing, is a nonhuman love
hidden inside human love.

The practical turn is service-facing. Love comes first because any reaction
requires a perception of self and world, and Shinzen says the origin of that
self/world perception is deep love. To love deeply is therefore to notice, as
often as possible, that the other, enemy, or source of the problem is being
loved into existence by Source before ordinary reaction. Judgments,
decisions, confrontation, opposition, and action still happen, but they occur
inside a different context. The final Prajnaparamita passage gives a
lineage-translation practice: when text or thought names things and says they
are empty, each named thing produces a fleeting mental image or word that can
be seen coming from and returning to Source.
