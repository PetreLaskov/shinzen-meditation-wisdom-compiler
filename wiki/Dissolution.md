Dissolution is Shinzen's name for a stage some practitioners pass through when sensory order loosens on the way from surface experience to Source. It can be blissful, chaotic, mixed, or absent, so it belongs equally to transformation theory and safety routing.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Bhanga can be absent, blissful, harsh/purgatorial, or mixed; when it appears, it may manifest as bubbly vibratory Flow or as disorientation where the center, borders, intellect, and bodily comfort seem torn away.
- **Why this matters**: It prevents future agents from treating Source, Flow, Gone, or impermanence as uniformly pleasant, and it gives scary practice reports a Shinzen-specific routing page.
- **Key tensions**: Shinzen recommends equanimity and contact with Expansion-Contraction, but the sources do not supply clinical differential diagnosis, stop rules, or teacher-competence criteria.
- **Source posture**: Probable as Shinzen's own practice-stage model; speculative as clinical guidance or as an interpretation of all destabilizing experiences.

## Model
Science of Enlightenment chapter 7 supplies the broader [[Intermediate Realm]] frame: the zone between ordinary surface consciousness and Source can appear as blockage, power, frightening imagery, bliss, entities, energy, or ordinary sensations. Chapter 10's bhanga is narrower: a surface-to-Source dissolution phase in which ordinary sensory order may break into blissful Flow or harsh chaos ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 7 - The Realm of Power]] S1-S4, S11-S12; [[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S16-S17).

Chapter 10 says meditation may follow the senses back to where they come from, revealing where the practitioner comes from. With commitment and competent instructors, Shinzen says most people can eventually have this Source experience, but it is not always pleasant and can destabilize depending on psychological disposition, early conditioning, genetics, and chance ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S16).

Bhanga is the Sanskrit name Shinzen gives to the dissolution process in which ordinary order may seem to fall apart. Some practitioners feel chaotic tearing, grinding, exploding, crushing, disorientation, and fear before a deeper order is revealed ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S17).

The pleasant side is blissful vibratory Flow. Shinzen compares it to a high-resolution runner's high: bubbles, shimmer, body-wide vibration, and sometimes a scintillating field spreading through body, mind, sight, and sound ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S17).

The harsh side is loss of ordinary comfort and sense-making. The mind cannot get answers, the body cannot get comfortable, the center of gravity is ripped away, and borders feel violated. Shinzen names two comfort routes: equanimity and contact with the forces doing the tearing. Being blown away is expansion; being crushed is contraction. If the practitioner can identify and surrender to those forces, Shinzen says they may evolve into gentle stretching and squeezing ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S18).

[[Dissolution (Bhanga), and T.S. Eliot]] gives the compact oral routing rule:
some practitioners do the path without dissolution, some with heavenly
dissolution, some with purgatorial dissolution, and some with a mixture.
Shinzen again says blissful bhanga may dissolve body, mind, sight, and sound
into pleasant vibratory "champagne" energy, but immediately adds that such
experience is not required for insight and purification ([[Dissolution
(Bhanga), and T.S. Eliot]] S2-S5).

[[Expansion and Contraction - Part 4 Heaven, Hell, Integration and Three
Tastes of Freedom]] repeats the heavenly/hellish router inside the
Expansion-Contraction series. Heavenly dissolution may be blissful,
effervescent Flow; hellish dissolution may include pounding, squeezing,
stretching, twisting, skeletons, rotting corpses, and animals eating the
practitioner. Shinzen says not everyone goes through bhanga, but those who do
may need a frame for either side ([[Expansion and Contraction - Part 4
Heaven, Hell, Integration and Three Tastes of Freedom]] S1-S3).

The same talk sharpens the harsh side through a purgatory-versus-hell
distinction. Within Shinzen's teaching frame, purgatorial dissolution may
feel terrifying, groundless, torn apart, death-imaged, painful, and
illness-like, but is interpreted as uncomfortable purification rather than
pointless suffering. This is a useful source-attributed distinction, not a
sufficient safety differential ([[Dissolution (Bhanga), and T.S. Eliot]]
S4).

[[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 1 of 3]] gives the live
case-report version of the harsh side. The student reports post-retreat onset
of solar-plexus waves, childhood terrors, fear of going crazy, unfamiliarity
of surroundings, hiding at home, collapsed sight-space, shame, guilt, fear of
being killed, and animal-death imagery. Shinzen's response, as reported in
the exchange, includes guide contact, specific sensory questioning, isolation
of sensations, and a practice handle of harsh Flow when full noting is too
frightening ([[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 1 of 3]]
S1-S5).

[[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 2 of 3]] adds the
disclosure and support rule. Shinzen says teachers talk about bhanga because
practitioners may need to know why it is happening and what to do, but the
same disclosure can scare people, create craving, or make those who have not
had dissolution feel deficient. The student adds that without Shinzen's
guidance he might have sought psychiatric help that misunderstood the
process, and Shinzen says the strongest support for such a case is someone
who has personally been through it and made it through ([[Experiences of the
Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 2 of 3]] S1-S4).

[[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 3 of 3]] completes the
interactive sequence with the positive Flow branch. After the severe and
disclosure-heavy framing, Shinzen coaches the student's presently available
pleasant vibratory field by having him note Flow, zoom out broadly through
body, mind, and the external world, include eyes-open sight as light, and
then test whether Flow contact remains during eye contact. This makes
blissful bhanga an integrative transfer practice rather than only a private
altered state ([[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 3 of 3]]
S2-S6).

The "awkward intermediate zone" names a practical liminal stage. The old coping mechanism of tightening up and turning away is being shed, but the new coping mechanism of opening up and turning toward is not yet strong enough to provide stable safety and fulfillment. The instruction is to meet both rocky tension and luscious bliss with equanimity ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S19).

The Gate 2 letting-go talk gives a shorter, practice-report version of the
same pattern. Shinzen says deeper relaxation can expose "icky sticky" or
antsy sensations, vulnerability, and disorientation; he describes this as an
awkward intermediate phase where the mesh of being has opened enough that old
armor no longer shields, but not enough that impacts pass through without
catching ([[Open Up and Turn Towards Challenging Letting Go States]] S1-S5).

[[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness Retreat]] adds the retreat-exit
version of this transition. Shinzen calls the outside-retreat form
"aftershock": hypersensitivity, vulnerability, no-skin exposure to ordinary
environments, disorientation, and inner material percolating up after the old
ordering principle has been weakened. He interprets it as the same awkward
intermediate stage between tightening-and-turning-away and the deeper
open-up-and-turn-toward coping style ([[What to Expect and Do After a
Mindfulness Retreat]] S3-S4).

Chapter 9 adds the Gone-side family resemblance. Intense Goneness, emptiness, and no-self can produce dark-night or pit-of-the-void reactions such as terror, paralysis, aversion, hopelessness, and disorientation. Shinzen recommends accentuating subtle positive aspects of the void, deconstructing negative reactions into image/talk/body sensation, reconstructing positive emotions, behaviors, and cognitions, and using ongoing teacher or practitioner support in extreme cases ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 9 - The Power of Gone]] S13-S14).

## Boundaries
Dissolution is not a universal stage. Chapter 10 says some people have blissful dissolution, some have harsh dissolution, some have a mixture, many never pass through bhanga, and some simply drop into freedom ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 10 - Return to the Source]] S17). The Eliot talk makes this non-universality explicit as a four-way rule: none, heavenly, purgatorial, or mixed ([[Dissolution (Bhanga), and T.S. Eliot]] S5).

It is also not a generic instruction to intensify distress. The sources give practice interpretations and internal interventions, but they do not distinguish bhanga from panic, trauma activation, dissociation, depersonalization, derealization, depression, mania, psychosis risk, medical crisis, sleep deprivation, or unsafe retreat conditions.

The first interactive dissolution talk makes that caution sharper rather
than weaker. Shinzen names the student's process as positive purification and
bhanga, but the report includes clinically adjacent features such as fear of
insanity, disorientation, derealization-like unfamiliarity, hiding at home,
and recurring waves over years. Future routing should preserve Shinzen's
practice interpretation while checking support, grounding, and differential
risks ([[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 1 of 3]] S1-S5).

The second interactive talk makes the teaching-disclosure caution equally
explicit. Public bhanga maps can prevent confusion, but they can also create
status comparison, dread, or craving. The same source also pressures the
clinical boundary in both directions: spiritual-emergence reports can be
misread by clinicians unfamiliar with them, and the transcript still does not
define when psychiatric or medical support is needed ([[Experiences of the
Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 2 of 3]] S2-S4).

The third interactive talk adds a pleasant-Flow caution. Broad waves,
sight-to-light perception, and eye-contact transfer are source evidence for
one advanced student's stabilized branch, not criteria every practitioner
should seek. Positive bhanga can still become status comparison, visual
effect chasing, social performance, or teacher-pressure territory if it is
separated from consent, pacing, and ordinary behavior checks ([[Experiences
of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 3 of 3]] S3-S6).

The purgatory-versus-hell language should not be used by itself to decide
whether suffering is purifying. The transcript gives a motivational and
comparative contemplative frame, but not criteria for when frightening
dissolution has become pointless suffering, destabilization, illness, or a
reason to stop and seek support ([[Dissolution (Bhanga), and T.S. Eliot]]
S4).

The rhetoric of trusting the forces that tear the practitioner apart should stay source-attributed. It may be meaningful within Shinzen's advanced frame, but it should not be used to override consent, medical care, ordinary grounding, therapy, sleep, community support, or stopping when risk is high.

The fourth Expansion-Contraction talk intensifies that boundary. Shinzen's
reassurance that the practitioner should not be afraid and will "be torn
apart" belongs to the teaching frame in which one becomes life and death; it
does not supply readiness, grounding, emergency, clinical, or teacher-support
criteria ([[Expansion and Contraction - Part 4 Heaven, Hell, Integration and
Three Tastes of Freedom]] S1-S4).

The same caution applies to the lighter "price one pays" language in the
letting-go talk. A metastable or awkward state may be one Shinzen-recognized
transition, but the source does not distinguish it from panic, trauma
activation, dissociation, depersonalization, medical discomfort, medication
effects, or retreat overpressure ([[Open Up and Turn Towards Challenging
Letting Go States]] S5).

Dissolution also differs from [[Primordial Feel]]. Chapter 9's primordial Feel is a Gone-triggered chaotic body-emotion stratum Shinzen interprets as infant-like and purgatorial. Chapter 10's bhanga is broader: a surface-to-Source destabilization that can involve body, mind, sensory fields, and the collapse of ordinary ordering.

Dissolution also differs from the broader [[Intermediate Realm]]. Power phenomena may include visions, entities, bliss, light, heat, out-of-body impressions, or spiritual materialism without necessarily being bhanga. Conversely, bhanga can occur as a destabilizing Source-side transition even without elaborate power content.

## Implications
For practice routing, dissolution reports should trigger four checks:

- Is the experience presenting as blissful Flow, harsh chaos, Gone-related void distress, primordial Feel, or something outside Shinzen's frame?
- Are concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity increasing or decreasing?
- Does the practitioner have competent support, ordinary grounding, sleep, medical stability, and relational feedback?
- Is continued practice increasing freedom and responsiveness, or intensifying fear, disorientation, collapse, grandiosity, or avoidance?

These checks keep bhanga available as a Shinzen concept without turning it into a blanket explanation for every destabilizing state.
