## Teaching Register
The register is live coaching plus conceptual setup. Shinzen first gives a practice taxonomy, then asks the student about the flavor of change in the discomfort, explains the available branches, lets the student's curiosity choose the starting branch, and begins a guided sit.

## Why This Talk Matters
The prior physical-discomfort source gives a turn-toward sequence. This one adds the higher-level routing fork that precedes such sequencing: discomfort does not automatically mean "turn toward." It may be met by direct contact, legitimate turn-away concentration, or change/Flow practice.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen distinguishes behavioral challenges, such as procrastination or smoking, from sensory challenges, where an uncomfortable body, mind, sight, sound, or other sensory event is the challenge.
- **S2**: Physical discomfort here is framed as uncomfortable touch, with "touch" meaning physical-type body sensation.
- **S3**: Sensory challenges have three broad practice strategies: turn toward the challenge with concentration, clarity, and equanimity; turn away to a pleasant or restful object while allowing the challenge; or focus on change through Flow and vanishing.
- **S4**: Turning toward may temporarily intensify the discomfort, but Shinzen says full experience can eventually break up the challenge.
- **S5**: Turning away is not necessarily denial or suppression, because the practitioner gives the challenge permission to be present while intentionally stabilizing attention elsewhere.
- **S6**: Turn-away practice can develop concentration, equanimity, and sensory clarity because the practitioner must detect subtle pleasant or restful events even while a strong pain or emotion state remains present.
- **S7**: Focus on change asks whether the apparently solid discomfort is moving, flowing, popping, vibrating, expanding, contracting, disappearing, or vanishing moment by moment.
- **S8**: When the student reports needle-like, popping, bubble-like changes in itchy discomfort, Shinzen treats that as a reason to choose focus on change as the first guided branch.
- **S9**: If Flow is found outside the painful area, Shinzen says it may spread and break up solidity in the painful area; this makes Flow practice field-wide rather than limited to the strongest sensation.
- **S10**: Shinzen names two turn-toward sub-branches, touch itself or Feel/Image/Talk reactions, and two turn-away sub-branches, restful states or deliberately created positive image/talk/feel.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **Strategy legitimization**: S3-S6 prevent both common failures: assuming turn-toward will only worsen pain and assuming turn-away must be avoidance.
- **Phenomenological check**: S7-S8 convert a vague pain report into Flow diagnostics: waviness, vibration, popping, expansion, contraction, disappearance, and vanishing.
- **Collaborative branch choice**: S10 shows Shinzen offering clear branches, then asking what appeals to the student before beginning practice.

## Practice Handles
A practitioner or guide can use this talk as a pre-sequence routing checklist:

- First ask whether the problem is behavioral or sensory.
- If it is physical discomfort, name it as uncomfortable touch before adding reactions or meanings.
- Offer three families of strategy: turn toward, turn away, or focus on change.
- For turn toward, choose between the touch itself and Feel/Image/Talk reactions.
- For turn away, choose between restful states and positive image/talk/feel.
- For focus on change, ask what flavor of Flow or vanishing is already detectable.
- Treat student preference and curiosity as useful branch data when safety does not already dictate another move.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by distinguishing behavioral challenges from sensory challenges. In this case the sensory challenge is uncomfortable physical touch. He says three broad strategies can be applied to such a challenge: turn attention toward it and bring mindful awareness to it, turn attention away toward pleasant or restful objects while allowing the challenge to remain, or focus on change until moving, flowing, and disappearing dominate the experience.

He then corrects three failure beliefs. Turning toward can seem as though it will only make pain worse, but full experience can break up the challenge. Turning away can seem like denial, but it can be done with equanimity and real concentration rather than suppression. A solid pain can seem unchanging, but microscopic attention may reveal moment-by-moment Flow and vanishing.

The interactive part begins when the student says the discomfort changes like many tiny needle-pricks or pops in one area. Shinzen identifies that as a Flow cue and offers focus on change as a plausible starting branch. He still lays out the other options clearly: turn toward the touch or reactions, turn away to rest or positive states, or find change wherever it is available and see whether it spreads into the painful area.

## Source Frame
The talk is trying to prevent strategy collapse. Shinzen does not treat direct exposure, distraction, and impermanence practice as moralized opposites; he reframes them as disciplined attentional strategies when they are supported by concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity. Its strongest evidence is for how he teaches and routes live practice, not for medical, psychological, or causal claims about pain resolution.

## Weakest Claims
- The statement that full experience will break up a sensory challenge is central to Shinzen's practice frame but should not be promoted as a medical or guaranteed psychological outcome.
- The claim that Flow found elsewhere can spread into a painful area is useful phenomenology but is not a safety criterion.
- Turn-away practice is legitimized, but the source does not define when turn-away becomes avoidance, dissociation, or failure to take needed ordinary action.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen strongly legitimizes turn-away, but the broader system also values complete experience of the challenge; future routing needs criteria for when each serves CCE.
- Focus on change can loosen solidity, but it may also become a way to chase Flow instead of responding to pain, fatigue, or medical signals.
- The talk offers a clean three-option menu, while real situations may require ordinary action before meditation strategy selection.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Turn away" could be used to rationalize avoidance if the practitioner is not actually allowing the challenge and developing concentration, clarity, and equanimity.
- "Turn toward" could be used as pressure to endure unsafe pain if the three-strategy menu is ignored.
- "Focus on change" could become Flow chasing or proof-of-progress thinking.
- Student preference could be overvalued when medical, clinical, relational, or ethical signals should govern the next step.
