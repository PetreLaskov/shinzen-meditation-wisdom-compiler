## Teaching Register
Short oral instruction with a technical practice pointer. Shinzen moves from obvious visual shifts to subtle attentional shifts, briefly speculates about saccades and central nervous system movement, then returns to the practice instruction: detect shifts at multiple scales.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk adds a compact route into visual Flow. It prevents See Flow from being limited to dramatic shimmering, closed-eye phenomena, or unusual visual energy by making ordinary shifts in sight space the operative object.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Visual experience shifts at several scales: body or hand movement, large eye movements, and smaller movements of attention from place to place within an object.
- **S2**: Any large or small shift can count as a new instance of visual experience, and detecting these shifts is the "whole trick" for getting Flow in sight space.
- **S3**: Like the body becoming undulatory and soft when attentional movement is noticed, external visual experience can soften into Flow through contact with each shift.
- **S4**: Practicing with glasses on and off can both be useful: normal acuity supports ordinary sight, while blur can be an artificial but useful platform for learning.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: The scale distinction makes See Flow trainable from ordinary perceptual mechanics rather than waiting for special visual effects.
- **S2**: Treating a shift as a new visual instance gives the practitioner an actionable detection task: notice transitions inside sight, not just objects.
- **S3**: The body analogy links visual Flow to an already familiar Flow route: attention movement can reveal undulation and softness across domains.
- **S4**: The glasses-on/off suggestion preserves pragmatic experimentation while keeping ordinary clear sight in the practice field.

## Practice Handles
Practitioners can look for visual Flow by noticing gross scene shifts from body movement, larger eye movements, and tiny attention shifts within the same object. Guides can suggest practicing under normal visual conditions and, if appropriate, with softened or blurred vision, while treating blur as a temporary learning aid rather than the definition of Flow.

## Summary
Shinzen says visual shifting has several sources. Some are obvious, such as turning the hand or body. Some come from large-scale eye movements. Others are smaller movements from place to place within an object, and he treats each of those as another sight event.

He speculates that small shifts may combine physical saccadic movement with central attentional movement in the visual cortex. The practice point is not the neuroscience but the detection rule: shifts at any scale can be taken as new visual experiences, and detecting them is the core trick for eliciting Flow in sight space.

He compares this with body practice: when attention shifts are noticed in the body, the body can become undulatory and soft. External visual experience can change similarly when each shift is tracked. He encourages trying the practice both with glasses on for normal acuity and with glasses off as an artificial platform for learning, then reports his own sight as total pixelated Flow.

## Source Frame
The talk is trying to make See Flow operational. Its speculative neuroscience is secondary and should not be treated as established mechanism. The durable teaching frame is phenomenological: visual experience is not only a static object field; it includes shifts that can be detected and used as Flow.

## Important Omissions
- The talk does not explain when to choose visual Flow rather than another practice object.
- It gives no safeguards for visual strain, dissociation, task interference, eye conditions, or obsessive monitoring of sight.
- It does not define how long to practice with blurred vision or how to transition back to ordinary seeing.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The glasses-off suggestion is useful as upaya, but it could be mistaken for manipulating perception to manufacture Flow. The better reading is experimental support for learning, not a requirement.
- The neuroscience aside can help modern students, but it risks overexplaining a phenomenological instruction with underdeveloped mechanism.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not treat blur, pixelation, or high-frequency visual vibration as proof of attainment.
- Do not use visual Flow practice in situations that require full visual attention for safety.
- Do not force eye movement, strain the eyes, or turn small-shift detection into compulsive visual scanning.
