Do Nothing is the manual's low-effort route into Absolute Rest. Its instruction is not to produce a calm state, but to stop adding voluntary attention-direction when that intention is noticed.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Do Nothing drops the noticed voluntary intention to control attention, not the experiences attention happens to be with.
- **Why this matters**: It prevents non-effort practice from becoming suppression, self-monitoring, or a covert effort to manufacture tranquility.
- **Key tensions**: The technique can be centering, decentering, restful, confusing, or Monkey Mind; the manual gives a spacey/racy switch rule but no full clinical safety map.
- **Source posture**: Strong as Shinzen's instruction; weaker for cross-tradition identifications and Source metaphysics.

## Model
The basic instructions are short: let whatever happens happen, and whenever one is aware of an intention to control attention, drop that intention ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S8; [[Do Nothing Meditation]] S4).

The subtlety is what the instruction does not require. A spontaneous thought does not need to be dropped. A sound, body sensation, emotion, image, restful state, or Flow state does not need to be dropped. Only the voluntary intention to direct attention around those experiences is dropped, and only when that intention is noticed ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S9; [[Do Nothing Meditation]] S5-S6).

The chapter also narrows "intention" to what is voluntary. If an intention cannot be dropped even for a moment, Shinzen says it is not functioning as voluntary in this practice context, so it does not need to be dropped. Dropping means letting go in the moment, not getting rid of the intention permanently ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S9).

Do Nothing therefore has a deliberately modest success condition. The practitioner need not continuously check for intention, because continuous checking would itself create continuous intention. A session may be pleasant, unpleasant, productive, or indistinguishable from Monkey Mind and still follow the instruction ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S9-S10).

The YouTube instruction adds Shinzen's clearest oral mechanism: Do Nothing is designed with time to sensitize the practitioner to the attention-control function and set up conditions in which that control may briefly turn off. Trying to force the turn-off keeps the control function active, so the practice works by not feeding that control project ([[Do Nothing Meditation]] S2).

## Attention-Control And Choice
The target is not experience in general but the place of choice and attention management. Shinzen specifically points to initiating thought, suppressing thought, directing thought, pursuing or redirecting thought, and using thought to manage thought as the zone Do Nothing will pinpoint with time ([[Do Nothing Meditation]] S8).

This makes choice-confusion practice-relevant. When a practitioner feels "I do not know what to do with my meditation right now," Shinzen treats that as a useful moment because the choice-making function is strongly activated. It may be a good time to Do Nothing, provided the practitioner is not using Do Nothing to avoid needed guidance, support, or ordinary action ([[Do Nothing Meditation]] S3).

Do Nothing also permits automatic skill momentum. Shinzen says automatic concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity built by previous practice should not be interfered with; in this technique, prior practice momentum and what he variously calls nature, Spirit, time, God, or grace are "meditating" the practitioner ([[Do Nothing Meditation]] S9). The metaphysical vocabulary remains source-attributed; the practice handle is that automatic CCE need not be shut down just because the practitioner is not intentionally doing it.

[[Parts & Wholes, Efforting & Do-Nothing A Certain Momentum]] gives a smaller, practice-specific example of that momentum. Shinzen says local efforting with parts and the separate practice of Do Nothing can combine so global awareness covers a large field with little effort while retaining clarity from prior parts work. In that mode, practice is partly like Do Nothing but still has the crispness of bear-down penetration ([[Parts & Wholes, Efforting & Do-Nothing A Certain Momentum]] S3-S7).

## Routing Rule
The chapter gives one especially useful practice-selection rule:

- If Do Nothing makes the practitioner too spacey, use [[Noting]] for a while.
- If Noting makes the practitioner too racy, use Do Nothing for a while.

This rule locates Do Nothing inside the larger manual rather than making it a superior endpoint. Basic Mindfulness has many other focus options when Do Nothing does not function well ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S10; [[Do Nothing Meditation]] S7). The YouTube talk adds a sharper stop cue: if the experience is horribly agitating or uncomfortable, Shinzen says there is no reason to continue Do Nothing at that time ([[Do Nothing Meditation]] S7).

[[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] adds the larger effort-regulation frame. Do Nothing is Shinzen's ease-up counterpart to effortful methods such as Noting, but it is not a one-sided replacement for them. The test of productive non-effort practice is whether the practitioner can still implement systematic focusing with high concentration, clarity, and equanimity; otherwise "call off the search" may become avoidance or phobia of bearing down ([[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] S4-S8).

Chapter 9 reinforces that non-superiority structurally. Do Nothing is one of three techniques, alongside [[Noting]] and [[Nurture Positive]], that can be learned in any order, need not all be learned, and can cycle together if all three are learned ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 9 - The Full Grid]] S5).

Chapter 12 places Do Nothing inside the guidance toolkit as one possible strategy, especially on the turn-away, soothing, or rest side of practice ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S12). That placement reinforces the routing rule: Do Nothing is a tool, not a universal instruction.

[[A.D.D. & the Do Nothing Technique]] adds the strongest single-technique permission so far. Shinzen says that if a person with ADD finds Do Nothing is the only method that works, it is in theory sufficient and can be pursued as the main path. The safeguard is not solo certainty: the practitioner should get input from someone competent with Do Nothing at least once or twice a year, and one sign that the practice works is eventual improvement enough to implement systematic or organized techniques ([[A.D.D. & the Do Nothing Technique]] S4-S6).

[[Focus on Rest - Standard (Relative Rest) and Advanced (Do Nothing)]] places
Do Nothing inside the Rest family from the other direction. Shinzen says
Focus on Rest has a standard form, which works with restful flavors, and an
advanced form, which he calls Do Nothing ([[Focus on Rest - Standard
(Relative Rest) and Advanced (Do Nothing)]] S6). That does not collapse Do
Nothing into ordinary relaxation; it makes Do Nothing the no-effort or
Absolute Rest side of a tranquility route that can still disclose
impermanence.

## What The Technique Is Not
Do Nothing is not an attempt to freeze experience, stop thinking, maintain awareness, achieve equanimity, stay with a good state, fix a bad state, understand experience, or continuously verify that practice is happening. If the practitioner notices an intention to do any of those, the instruction is to drop that intention ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S10).

It is also not pure passivity in ordinary life. The technique applies during a practice period. It should not be used to avoid necessary action, safety response, medical care, relational repair, or ethical behavior. That boundary is an inference from the manual's broader outcome criteria and safety gaps, not an explicit instruction in this chapter.

## Source And Nondual Frame
Shinzen links Do Nothing to many historical or modern labels, including choiceless awareness, open presence, just sitting, Dzogchen/Mahamudra, passive contemplation, and nondual awareness ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S9). Those comparisons route interpretation but should not be treated as exact equivalences without separate evidence.

The chapter eventually frames Do Nothing as "Call Off the Search": stop trying to get to Source and give Source a chance to get to the practitioner. This is Shinzen's contemplative interpretation of the technique, not a claim the wiki should promote as established metaphysics ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S10).

Chapter 8 adds a terminology caution. Some people call Focus on Everything "Choiceless Awareness," while others apply that term to Do Nothing ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S4). Future agents should identify the actual instruction rather than relying on that label.

The Focus on Rest talk adds Shinzen's clearest caveat around comparative
labels. He presents Do Nothing as his modified reworking of no-effort,
anti-meditation, just-sitting, choiceless-awareness, nondual-awareness, and
Dzogchen-like themes, but explicitly says he is not claiming exact identity
with those traditions ([[Focus on Rest - Standard (Relative Rest) and
Advanced (Do Nothing)]] S7-S8). In his own model, the spacious nature of
consciousness also has effortless dynamism; when it presents as effortless
expansion-contraction, Do Nothing remains adjacent to [[Flow]] and [[Way of
Flow|Focus on Change]] without becoming an effortful change technique ([[Focus
on Rest - Standard (Relative Rest) and Advanced (Do Nothing)]] S9).

## Boundaries
Do Nothing has a built-in anti-struggle safeguard: if dropping requires struggle, the chapter says one does not need to drop. This keeps the technique from becoming a hidden exertion practice.

The safety boundary is still open. The chapter includes guidance to let go of intentions to fix dissociation or confusion during Do Nothing, while also saying that if Do Nothing makes one too spacey one should try Noting. The YouTube talk adds that agitation or discomfort can also be a reason not to continue at that time. Future routing needs to distinguish ordinary decentering from clinically relevant dissociation, collapse, panic, or destabilization ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S10; [[Do Nothing Meditation]] S7; [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]]).

The effort-regulation talk adds a second boundary: non-effort can itself become a rigid identity. If a practitioner refuses any formal goal or systematic focus while remaining unconscious and driven in daily life, Do Nothing language may be protecting aversion rather than freeing practice ([[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] S6).

The parts-and-wholes talk adds a third boundary: minimum effort is meaningful only when clarity momentum is present. A broad state with little effort may be mature integration, but it may also be vagueness, dullness, or loss of contact if the crispness from parts work is absent ([[Parts & Wholes, Efforting & Do-Nothing A Certain Momentum]] S5-S7).

The ADD talk adds a guidance boundary. Do Nothing may be the only workable technique for some practitioners, but Shinzen still warns that practicing it on one's own without occasional competent input is dangerous. The talk does not define ADD, clinical care, guide competence, or what to do when transfer to systematic techniques never appears ([[A.D.D. & the Do Nothing Technique]] S2, S5-S6).

The advanced-Rest framing adds a passivity boundary. Shinzen says Do Nothing
is not thumb-twiddling or simply letting the mind wander; there is a clear
way to do nothing that can make it a growth experience ([[Focus on Rest -
Standard (Relative Rest) and Advanced (Do Nothing)]] S7). The transcript does
not by itself define that clear way as fully as [[Do Nothing Meditation]], so
future agents should route technique details through the dedicated Do Nothing
instruction rather than through this comparative talk alone.

## Implications
For practice reasoning, Do Nothing is especially useful when effort has become self-defeating. It trains confidence that experience can unfold without continuous managerial attention.

For advanced practice, the key diagnostic is subtle control. The object is not a sensory event but the impulse to run attention. The moment that impulse is noticed and dropped, the practice is complete for that moment.
