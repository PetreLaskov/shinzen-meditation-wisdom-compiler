This 2.5-minute Shinzen Young Q&A answers whether a person with ADD can use
Do Nothing as the only workable technique. Its durable contribution is not a
new Do Nothing instruction, but a sufficiency-and-safeguard rule: the method
may be enough in theory, but should be checked occasionally by someone
competent, and one sign of success is growing ability to use more organized
techniques.

## Teaching Register
The register is short Q&A guidance. Shinzen answers permissively, but almost
immediately adds conditions: the practice should be done optimally, checked
by someone competent, and judged partly by whether it eventually allows
organized techniques.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk adds a missing Gate 1 routing case. Earlier pages already had
racy/spacey switching, effort cross-testing, and clarity momentum; this talk
adds the case where a practitioner with ADD finds Do Nothing is the only
technique that works. Shinzen does not reject that path, but he removes the
solo purity story: competent input and later cross-capacity matter.

## Key Claims / Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: Shinzen says that, in theory, a practitioner can do only Do Nothing
  for the rest of life and that it can take one "all the way to classic
  enlightenment."
- **S2**: He immediately adds that the practitioner should make sure the
  technique is being done optimally by occasionally touching base with
  someone competent to guide that specific technique.
- **S3**: He frames Do Nothing as a classic path discovered and rediscovered
  under names such as non-meditation, just sitting, call off the search, and
  choiceless awareness.
- **S4**: For someone with ADD who finds Do Nothing is the only method that
  works, Shinzen says it is in theory sufficient and says to "go for it."
- **S5**: He recommends input from someone familiar with the technique once
  or twice a year at least so the practitioner knows they are on course.
- **S6**: He gives a practical sign that the method is working: somewhere
  down the line, the person's ADD should improve enough that they can
  implement other systematic or organized techniques.

## Practice Handles
- If Do Nothing is the only workable technique, it can be used as the main
  practice rather than treated as a failure to do "real" meditation.
- That permission is conditional. The practitioner should get periodic
  competent input, especially because self-guided Do Nothing can drift into
  vagueness, passivity, or misunderstanding.
- The longitudinal test is transfer. Do Nothing is working well if it
  eventually increases capacity for more systematic techniques, not if it
  permanently justifies inability or refusal to use structure.

## Summary
Shinzen answers a question about ADD and Do Nothing with a qualified yes. Do
Nothing can, in theory, be one's sole lifetime practice and can be a complete
path. He places that claim in a broad contemplative frame by naming similar
approaches from Tibetan, Zen, Papaji, and Krishnamurti-associated language.

The practical guidance is more important than the comparative frame. He warns
that practicing only Do Nothing alone carries real danger unless the
practitioner occasionally receives input from someone competent with that
technique. For the ADD case, he permits Do Nothing as the only workable
method, recommends at least yearly or twice-yearly input, and says a sign of
success would be later ability to implement systematic or organized
techniques.

## Source Frame
Shinzen is protecting a permissive non-effort route from two distortions. He
does not force an ADD practitioner into systematic methods when Do Nothing is
the only thing that works. He also does not validate unguided lifelong
drifting under a non-effort label. The talk's frame is practice guidance, not
clinical advice; ADD is treated as a practical attention difficulty, not as a
diagnosed condition with medical or psychological care criteria.

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that Do Nothing can take one all the way to classic enlightenment
  is source-authoritative for Shinzen's teaching, but not independently
  established by this short transcript.
- The cross-tradition naming list is a routing aid, not proof that all named
  traditions or teachers mean exactly the same practice.
- The claim that ADD should improve enough to allow systematic techniques is
  a practice-routing marker, not clinical evidence for ADD treatment.

## Important Omissions
- No criteria are given for diagnosing ADD, distinguishing ADD from anxiety,
  trauma, sleep problems, depression, medication effects, or ordinary
  restlessness, or deciding when clinical support is needed.
- "Someone competent" is not defined: the talk gives no credentials,
  supervision, consent, referral, or adverse-event criteria.
- The talk does not say what to do if Do Nothing remains the only workable
  method for years without transfer to systematic techniques.
- No details are given for session length, frequency, retreat intensity, or
  when to stop, switch, ground, or seek support.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk permits a single-technique lifetime path, while the larger Basic
  Mindfulness architecture offers many focus methods and repeatedly values
  method flexibility.
- The ADD permission and the transfer test pull in opposite but useful
  directions: Do Nothing may be sufficient now, yet one sign of sufficiency is
  later ability to use methods that are not Do Nothing.
- The comparative framing of Do Nothing as classic global practice can
  overexpand the claim unless future pages keep the local instruction clear:
  drop noticed voluntary attention-control.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Do Nothing is sufficient" can be misused to avoid guidance, medical or
  psychological support, ordinary action, or technique feedback.
- "ADD will improve" can be misread as a treatment promise rather than a
  Shinzen-specific practice marker.
- "Only Do Nothing works for me" can be a legitimate fit report, but it can
  also protect avoidance of structure, shame around effort, or drifting.
