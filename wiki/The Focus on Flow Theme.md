## Teaching Register
The talk is compact instruction with live Q&A. Shinzen defines the dynamic aspect of ordinary and restful experience, answers a student's worry that heartbeat-like pulsing might not count, and then adds a disclosure warning about always-flow claims.

## Why This Talk Matters
The durable value is boundary-setting. It prevents future agents from treating Flow as exotic energy, excluding ordinary pulse sensations, forcing Flow when static experience dominates, or presenting advanced always-flow claims without comparison-mind safeguards.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Ordinary or restful experience can present a static aspect or a dynamic aspect; the dynamic aspect means movement, change, and the forces or energies that could produce movement and change.
- **S2**: Shinzen calls the continuous side of dynamic change Flow and the abrupt or discontinuous side Gone or vanishing.
- **S3**: Any experience where the practitioner can focus on changingness counts as Flow, including vibratory or pulsatory qualities even when they are clearly caused by heartbeat or blood circulation.
- **S4**: Flow has many flavors: vibratory, bubbly, scintillating, mist-like, twinkly, sparkly, pulsatory, wavy, lava-lamp-like, seaweed-like, streaming, spreading, collapsing, outward pressure, inward pressure, scattering, gathering, and expansive-contractive flavors.
- **S5**: In Shinzen's vipassana frame, Flow is observed when present and not pursued when absent; intentional generation or manipulation of energy in qigong or kundalini yoga may be valid practice but is not Focus on Flow strictly speaking.
- **S6**: With enough time and practice, resolution may become so great that Flow is always detectable, but Shinzen explicitly warns that this true statement can be harmful if it creates failure, frustration, or a need to have that experience.
- **S7**: When Flow appears in one area, it may naturally induce Flow in a more solid area; the guide can help "introduce" the flowing zone to the solid zone, but this is somewhat esoteric and harder to reproduce alone.
- **S8**: Flow and Gone can combine when bursts of Flow noticeably arise and subside.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves

- **S5**: The observe-versus-create distinction protects Focus on Flow from being confused with energy-generation disciplines while still respecting those disciplines as valid outside this strict vipassana frame.
- **S6**: The disclosure warning directly calibrates advanced-map transmission: true claims are not automatically useful claims.

## Practice Handles
If experience is stable, the practitioner can let it be stable rather than searching for Flow. If dynamic change is available, any changingness may be used: pulse, vibration, waves, pressure, scattering, gathering, expansion, or contraction. If Flow is present in one zone and solidity in another, a guide may invite contact between them and then stop manipulating, allowing the Flow side to influence the solid side if it naturally does.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by distinguishing static and dynamic aspects within ordinary or restful experience. Dynamic means movement, change, and the forces or energies behind movement and change. Its continuous side is Flow; its abrupt side is Gone or vanishing. One can practice with Flow, Gone, or their combination when flows arise and then noticeably subside.

In response to a question about whether heartbeat-like pulsing is "just" physical and therefore not Flow, Shinzen says it definitely counts. Any focus on changingness counts as Flow, including vibratory or pulsatory sensations produced by circulation. He then gives a broad range of Flow flavors, from vibration and pulse to waves, spreading, collapsing, outward and inward pressures, scattering, gathering, and expansive-contractive flavors.

The talk closes with guardrails. In this system, Flow is something observed when present; if it is not present, the static aspect is dominant. Shinzen distinguishes this from practices that intentionally create energy, such as qigong or kundalini yoga, which can be valid but are not strictly Focus on Flow. He then says that with time and practice resolution can become so great that Flow is always present, but immediately warns that such statements can inspire some people and damage others by creating failure, frustration, or comparison mind. Finally, he names a guided strategy in which Flow in one domain may induce Flow in a more solid domain.

## Source Frame
The talk is trying to make Flow usable without making it special. Shinzen's criterion is phenomenological and attentional: if changingness can be detected, it can be Flow practice. The pulse example deliberately demystifies Flow by allowing gross physiological origins. The qigong/kundalini contrast separates Shinzen's strict vipassana observation frame from energy-generation practices without rejecting those practices.

## Easily Misused Claims
