This 5m14s `@ShinzenVideos` short preserves a state-to-action integration
handle. Shinzen recalls an early Zen/Shingon training aphorism and translates
it into a practice rule: when action is needed, the practitioner need not
abandon samadhi in order to function.

## Teaching Register
Short autobiographical transmission note and advanced practice instruction.

## Why This Talk Matters
The wiki already had daily-life practice, retroactive meditation, and mature
nonduality pages. This talk adds a missing bridge: when a deep state is
available and ordinary action is required, practice does not always mean
exiting the state first or processing the activity afterward. Needed doing
can arise from the state, while the fear, disorientation, or reactivation
that seems to pull the person out becomes no-self material.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen recalls his first teacher giving one-line teaching phrases
  just before ending long Zen sits; the setting made the phrase land while
  practitioners were still in deep concentration.
- **S2**: The remembered phrase means, in Shinzen's paraphrase, that samadhi
  one goes into and comes out of is not samadhi. The exact Japanese wording
  is auto-caption-fragile.
- **S3**: Shinzen first understood the phrase as "stay in these states all
  the time," but later read it more deeply: when it is time to act, do not
  assume one must return to the normal way of being before functioning.
- **S4**: The practice move is to let whatever doing is needed arise from
  that state; fear or disorientation during the transition should be met with
  equanimity rather than used as a reason to abandon the state.
- **S5**: A practitioner may come out of samadhi, but does not need to leave
  samadhi in order to function.
- **S6**: Shinzen says one comes out because the inner system reactivates; if
  the practitioner sees where that reactivation comes from, they can see the
  self being born from no-self.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S2-S3**: The phrase blocks a state-only reading of samadhi. The issue is
  not merely entering depth, but how depth relates to ordinary doing.
- **S4-S5**: The central handle is samadhi-to-function integration: action is
  not automatically outside practice or outside the state.
- **S4**: Fear and disorientation are not bypassed. They become sensory
  material to equanimize during transition.
- **S6**: The talk links integration to no-self: the apparent loss of state
  can reveal the inner-system reactivation by which self re-forms.
- **S1-S2**: The oral lineage story matters as transmission context, but the
  phrase should stay practice-bounded rather than becoming lineage
  authorization or proof of attainment.

## Practice Handles
- After a deep sit, retreat period, or absorption-like state, notice the
  impulse to "go back to normal" before acting.
- If action is ordinary-life appropriate, let the needed doing begin from
  the available state rather than first constructing a separate normal-self
  mode.
- If fear or disorientation appears, meet it with equanimity and sensory
  clarity instead of treating it as proof that the state has failed.
- If the state collapses, inspect the inner-system reactivation: Image, Talk,
  Feel, orientation, fear, planning, or body contraction may show self
  forming again.
- Do not use this handle for safety-critical action, professional duties, or
  clinical instability without ordinary task safety and support. The source
  does not license acting from impaired absorption.

## Summary
Shinzen remembers his first teacher as a forceful Zen-style trainer who
would deliver dramatic one-line teachings just before ringing the bell at
the end of a long sit. One phrase stayed with him: the samadhi one goes into
and comes out of is not samadhi. He says it took him years to understand the
phrase.

His initial reading was that one should stay in deep states all the time.
The deeper reading is more functional. When it is time to take care of
business, the practitioner does not need to switch back into a normal
ordinary mode before acting. Whatever function is needed can arise from the
state itself. If that produces fear or disorientation, the instruction is to
meet that material with equanimity and continue functioning.

Shinzen then gives the no-self integration point. A practitioner may come
out of samadhi, but the reason is the reactivation of the inner system. If
that reactivation is seen clearly, the practitioner sees self being born from
no-self. The integration problem is therefore not solved by clinging to the
state, but by seeing how the state, action, reactivation, and self-forming
relate.

## Source Frame
The talk is strong evidence for Shinzen's state-to-function teaching
posture. It is weak evidence for historical, linguistic, or lineage claims
about the exact Japanese phrase or the teacher's tradition. It also requires
safety calibration: the oral phrase "throw caution to the wind" belongs to a
brief training story and cannot be generalized into unsafe action from
disorientation.

The safest reading is practice-bounded: do not make ordinary action depend
on abandoning samadhi, and do not make a collapsed state into failure. Track
the fear, disorientation, and inner-system reactivation through which a
functional self reappears.

## Weakest Claims
- The exact Japanese phrase and lineage context are transcript-limited and
  should not be quoted or translated without audiovisual or external spot
  check.
- "Throw caution to the wind" is risky oral training language; the source
  gives no safety criteria for when action from a deep state is appropriate.
- The claim that seeing inner-system reactivation removes "difficulties
  whatsoever" in integration is compressed and capacity-dependent.
- The talk does not prove that every practitioner can function skillfully
  from samadhi or safely observe self-birth during state transition.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The source says one may come out of samadhi but does not need to leave
  samadhi in order to function. The useful distinction is between state
  change and abandoning the practice relation to action.
- The instruction encourages functioning from the state, while several
  safety pages restrict altered, inward, Source-facing, or disoriented
  practice during driving, machinery, care duties, and high-stakes action.
- The phrase can sound like a demand for continuous samadhi, but Shinzen's
  deeper explanation makes the reactivation of self part of the practice
  rather than proof of failure.
- The talk uses a forceful Zen training memory, but Shinzen's own modern
  system elsewhere rejects making old-school intensity into a default model.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use this talk to drive, operate tools, perform professional duties,
  or handle conflict from an impaired or disoriented altered state.
- Do not shame a practitioner for needing to ground, rest, simplify, or
  switch out of a deep state before acting.
- Do not treat spontaneous action from samadhi as proof of ethical maturity,
  teacher authority, or final realization.
- Do not make "self being born from no-self" into metaphysical proof or a
  reason to bypass ordinary self-function, relationship repair, or clinical
  support.
- Do not use the lineage story to romanticize harsh training or old-school
  toughness.
