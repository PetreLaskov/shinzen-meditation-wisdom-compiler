## Teaching Register
This is oral practice-organization and guidance instruction. Shinzen speaks less as a manual writer than as a guide explaining why his system is built as a routable vocabulary: a practitioner may use it alone, but a trained facilitator can use the same categories to optimize practice when ordinary life becomes overwhelming.

## Why This Talk Matters
The earlier session-setup talk already established one-method practice, workout sequencing, and looping-and-branching. This talk shifts the center of gravity from formal session design to live help: the algorithm exists so a facilitator can route the practitioner through ordinary states, Rest, Flow, Gone, Do Nothing, and Nurture Positive when a Wall or Window appears.

## Key Claims / Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: Shinzen presents the Basic Mindfulness system as a catalog of practice procedures: ordinary states, restful states, dynamic states such as Flow and vanishing, Do Nothing, and Focus on Positive can each be used as a standalone purchase, a sequence, or a flexible algorithm.
- **S2**: The workout sequence he gives moves through ordinary experience, corresponding restful states, Flow, vanishings, Do Nothing, and then positive reconstruction, with the option to cycle back through ordinary experience after the sequence has changed the field.
- **S3**: The looping-and-branching algorithm lets practice start anywhere and go anywhere, depending on what arises, which gives flexibility for Windows of opportunity and Walls that challenge the practitioner.
- **S4**: Shinzen says most people will not do traditional monastic intensity, but illness, injury, betrayal, violence, grief, or one's own harmful action may bring monastery-like intensity into ordinary life.
- **S5**: When a practitioner already knows the system vocabulary, Shinzen claims a trained facilitator can quickly help them turn a catastrophe into an intense training situation rather than leaving them blown away by it.
- **S6**: The facilitator's larger algorithm contains more specialized exercises than the practitioner's local version; the practitioner can "dock" their simpler practice program inside the facilitator's broader routing capacity.

## Practice Handles
- Use the practice menu in three scales: one method for simplicity, a workout sequence for broad coverage, or loop-and-branch routing for live optimization.
- In a workout sequence, begin with ordinary sensory events, then Rest, Flow, Gone, Do Nothing, and Focus on Positive when the session calls for deconstruction followed by reconstruction.
- Treat a Wall or Window as a routing problem: what is present, which category clarifies it, and what branch follows if that category becomes strong or unavailable?
- Use trained facilitation when overwhelm makes self-routing unlikely, especially after intense life events.
- Treat "life as monastery" as a conditional teaching handle: ordinary crisis can become practice only if contact, guidance, support, ethics, and ordinary action are preserved.
- Distinguish the practitioner's simplified internalized algorithm from the facilitator's larger library of branches and special exercises.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by reviewing the Basic Mindfulness menu. A practitioner can work with ordinary sensory states, restful states, dynamic states such as Flow and vanishings, Do Nothing, or Focus on Positive. One use is to choose a single method and ignore the rest. Another is to arrange several methods into a psycho-spiritual workout cycle.

He then gives a full cycle: note ordinary experience, note the corresponding restful states, note Flow, note vanishings, let accumulated CCE momentum support Do Nothing, and then reanimate in a positive way through Focus on Positive. The sequence can return to ordinary experience, but the ordinary field may now be different because it has passed through the cycle.

The strongest Gate 2 material comes when Shinzen explains why the system was designed for interactive guidance. The practitioner can internalize a compact algorithm, but when a major Wall or Window arises, a trained facilitator can use a larger looping-and-branching algorithm to optimize the experience. Shinzen frames severe ordinary-life events as potential monastery-like intensives: not because harm is good, but because high intensity can be worked with when the practitioner has the vocabulary and the guide can keep the process on track.

## Source Frame

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that a trained facilitator can get someone back on track within a short phone session after severe events is asserted from Shinzen's experience, not validated by criteria or outcome evidence.
- "Profound permanent growth" from catastrophe is an oral teaching claim here; the transcript does not give follow-up evidence, adverse cases, or limits.
- "Life as monastery" can be useful, but the talk does not distinguish spiritual training from trauma exposure, medical crisis, legal crisis, coercion, or ordinary obligations.
- The algorithm metaphor can imply more precision and reliability than the transcript demonstrates.

- No dosage or stop rules are given for working with grief, violence, betrayal, injury, illness, or one's own harmful behavior.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The same system is presented as a simple catalog from which one can buy one method and as a large looping-and-branching algorithm that may require trained facilitation.
- Ordinary catastrophe is framed as practice intensity, but the talk does not state the parallel need for ordinary repair, protection, medical care, grief support, or accountability.
- The workout cycle ends with positive reconstruction, but positive practice after catastrophe can either support reanimation or bypass unresolved pain and action.
- The algorithm is said to optimize experience, but optimization language can obscure consent, readiness, and relational power dynamics.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "the monastery comes to you" to romanticize trauma, violence, illness, betrayal, or preventable harm.
- Do not infer that meditation facilitation should replace emergency care, therapy, legal process, medical assessment, or social support.
- Do not treat a trained Shinzen facilitator's confidence as proof that a crisis is safe to intensify.
- Do not use the algorithm metaphor to pressure a practitioner to keep practicing when they need to stop, ground, leave, repair, report, or rest.
- Do not use Focus on Positive as a premature resurrection frame when grief, accountability, or practical response is still primary.
