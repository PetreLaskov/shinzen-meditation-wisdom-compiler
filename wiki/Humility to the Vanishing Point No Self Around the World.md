Its durable contribution is not a general history of religions, but a no-self and Source translation move: traditions name the emptying of self/world, then often name a return in which life, doing, goodness, or divine participation reappears from nothingness.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk sharpens no-self as humility carried to the point where self and world lose somethingness. It also confirms a recurring Shinzen pattern: the deepest emptying is not blank annihilation, because several traditions are read as pairing emptying with stabilized Source participation, doingness, divine attributes, compassion, or service.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen equates Sufi *fana* with the Buddhist no-self territory, then pairs it with *baqa*, a stabilized relation to Source/God in which the world can be understood as arising from Allah.
- **S2**: He glosses Hebrew *bittul ha-yesh* as annihilation of the somethingness of self and world, followed by participation in continuous arising of things from divine nothing.
- **S3**: He reads Christian *kenosis* as emptying out somethingness and links deep kenosis to *theosis*, becoming more God-like in goodness and compassion rather than omnipotence.
- **S4**: He places Buddhist *anatta*, Tibetan *dagme*, Chinese *wu wo*, Vietnamese *vo nga*, Korean *mu*, Japanese *muga*, and Taoist *xu* in the same broad humility/no-self/emptiness family.
- **S5**: Shinzen's "nano-Christ" phrase translates the kenosis/theosis pattern as a miniature dying of self in order to be of service to others.

## Practice Handles
For a practitioner or guide, the useful handle is to treat humility as de-objectification rather than self-hatred. The thingness of self and world can vanish, but the expected mature direction is stabilized re-entry: doing, goodness, compassion, and service appear through the emptied person rather than being owned by an ego-thing.

## Summary
Shinzen begins with Sufi *fana*, then moves to Jewish *bittul ha-yesh*, glossed as annihilation of the somethingness of self and world. He emphasizes that the emptying has a flip side: Jewish creation from divine nothing and Sufi *baqa*, where one is stabilized with Source and sees the world coming from God.

He then turns to Christian Greek *kenosis*, linking it with Sanskrit *shunya* and interpreting it as emptying out somethingness. Deep kenosis is paired with *theosis*, which he defines as becoming God-like in goodness and compassion, culminating in his deliberately irreverent "nano-Christ" phrase: a miniature dying in order to serve others.

The remainder surveys Buddhist and Asian no-self vocabulary: Pali *anatta*, Tibetan *dagme*, Chinese *wu wo*, Vietnamese *vo nga*, Korean *mu*, Japanese *muga*, and Taoist *xu*. The point is a cross-cultural family of terms for humility to the vanishing point, not a detailed doctrinal comparison.
