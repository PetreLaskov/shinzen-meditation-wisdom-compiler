- **S6**: He sketches Mahayana as making one's own liberation and others' liberation or welfare equally important, and Vajrayana as a later Buddhist development heavily centered on rites and rituals.
- **S7**: He says Vajrayana is not therefore a degradation of the Buddha's teaching because ritual can be a legitimate meditation vehicle when it creates an ideal situation for concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity.

## Summary
Shinzen begins with the practical question people ask before retreat: whether they will have to participate in ceremonies, rituals, or bowing. He says no, jokes that bowing would make him uncomfortable, and immediately turns that discomfort into practice material as a self-referential arising. He then says he is happiest when people treat him like anyone else, including disagreeing with or challenging him.

He then gives a compressed history of ritual in Buddhism. The early Buddha, in Shinzen's account, denied that rites and rituals were effective in themselves, a revolutionary move in a culture where religion centered on ritual. But Buddhism itself is conditioned and changes. Shinzen sketches later developments: Mahayana gives equal footing to one's own liberation and others' welfare, and Vajrayana later becomes strongly ritual-centered.

The resolution is the practice rule. Ritual is not effective by itself, but its simplified situation, repetition, symbolism, and intentionality can become a legitimate meditation vehicle when used to cultivate concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity. Shinzen illustrates this with a Kabbalistic claim about kavanah and then with optional sweat lodge participation around some retreats.
