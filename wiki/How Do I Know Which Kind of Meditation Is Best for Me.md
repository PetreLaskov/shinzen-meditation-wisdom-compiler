## Teaching Register
Beginner public Q&A and teacher-orientation. Shinzen answers a practical "which kind?" question by translating traditions into focus-object and system-design choices while keeping teacher personality and explanation quality visible.

## Why This Talk Matters
[[The Best Path]] already says path fit is person-and-time-specific. This talk makes that posture operational for beginners: read traditions, check teacher style, notice what sensory object the method uses, and ask whether the method belongs to a monolithic or algorithmic system whose rationale the teacher can explain.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen says choosing among meditation kinds involves trial and error, reading different traditions, and noticing which way of working appeals intellectually.
- **S2**: Teacher style, personality, and emphasis are part of method fit; teachers differ, and Shinzen characterizes his own style as precise or sharp rather than mainly heartfelt.
- **S3**: A main difference among meditation traditions is what they have the practitioner focus on to build concentration.
- **S4**: Most meditation methods use a sensory object, though objectless or formless methods exist and are more specialized.
- **S5**: Common focus objects include breath/body sensation, external sound or music, external sight such as a candle or flower, mantra or repeated internal talk, mental image such as light or darkness, and pleasant emotional body feeling such as love.
- **S6**: Whether a practitioner changes objects day to day or stays with one object depends on the meditation system's internal consistency; teachers should be able and willing to explain why they ask a student to practice in a certain way.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S2**: The talk makes method choice explicitly relational and cognitive, not only technical: fit includes how the tradition makes sense to the student and how the teacher teaches.
- **S3-S5**: Shinzen reduces many traditions to a shared attentional-training structure without erasing their differences: different objects train the generic skill of returning attention.
- **S6**: The accountability move is small but important. The teacher's assignment should not be a bare command; the student should be able to ask why the system chooses one object, alternates objects, or keeps one object stable.

## Practice Handles
For a practitioner or guide:

- Compare candidate practices by four variables before ranking them: intellectual model, teacher style, sensory focus object, and system design.
- Ask whether the method is training attention through breath/touch, sound, sight, mantra/talk, image, emotional body feeling, or a more specialized objectless approach.
- Do not assume day-to-day object switching is either wrong or best; ask whether the system is designed for algorithmic variation or monolithic repetition.
- Treat "try what works" as trial and error inside a system, not random technique shopping.
- Expect teachers to explain the rationale for assignments, especially when the system asks for one object always or different objects by condition.

## Summary
Shinzen answers the beginner question by saying meditation selection is partly trial and error. A practitioner can read about traditions, notice which intellectual model appeals, and meet different teachers to sense their style, personality, and emphasis. He names his own style as more precise or sharp than mainly heartfelt, making teacher style part of the fit rather than an incidental packaging issue.

He then gives a simple technical comparison: most traditions build concentration by giving the practitioner an object, then training return when attention wanders. Typical objects are sensory: breath sensations, sound or music, a candle or flower, mantra, mental image, or a pleasant emotional body feeling such as love. The object differs, but the generic attentional skill is strengthened through repeated return.

The final move distinguishes arbitrary mixing from system-guided variation. Some systems let practitioners vary objects according to a rule or algorithm; others are deliberately monolithic, such as always returning to breath sensation. Which route is appropriate depends on the internal consistency of the system being practiced. Shinzen closes by saying a meditation teacher should be willing and able to explain why they ask a student to do things in a particular way.

## Source Frame
The source is a beginner explanation, not a taxonomy of all contemplative systems. Shinzen translates traditions into a practical focus-object frame because the question is how to choose and how to understand apparent differences. That translation is strong as Shinzen's teaching posture and weaker as a full comparative account of any tradition.

The transcript quality is usable for routing but not clean enough for exact quotation of names or phrases. The "sharp compassion" self-description is retained as a style signal, not as a load-bearing quotation.

## Weakest Claims
- The sensory-object framing is pedagogically useful but does not fully describe traditions whose aims, vows, ritual forms, ethics, lineage commitments, or view teachings are load-bearing.
- The transcript's teacher-style phrase is quality-B and should not be over-quoted without audio or caption spot-checking.
- "Teachers should explain why" is clear as a norm in the talk, but it does not define what counts as a sufficient explanation or what a student should do when the rationale is weak.

## Important Omissions
- No criteria are given for knowing whether a method is actually working beyond appeal and trial.
- The talk does not address safety, destabilization, trauma, clinical referral, coercive teacher pressure, or when a beginner should stop rather than persist.
- The advantages and disadvantages of particular objects are deferred; Shinzen lists objects but does not give a robust routing table.
- The talk does not distinguish casual experimentation from formal lineage commitment, retreat discipline, or therapeutic support.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Trial and error supports exploration, while internal system consistency warns against arbitrary mixing.
- Teacher explanation supports accountability, but an explanation can still be rhetorically persuasive, incomplete, or outside the teacher's competence.
- Reducing methods to focus objects clarifies beginner choice but can underrepresent ethical, relational, devotional, ritual, or philosophical dimensions of a tradition.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "trial and error" to justify restless novelty seeking, avoidance of difficulty, or shopping for a teacher who confirms preference.
- Do not treat the focus object as the whole tradition.
- Do not treat a teacher's ability to explain a method as proof of safety, ethics, realization, or clinical competence.
- Do not use system consistency to override consent, distress signals, ordinary functioning, or support needs.
