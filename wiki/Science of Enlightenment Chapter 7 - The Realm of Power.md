Chapter 7 of *The Science of Enlightenment* gives Shinzen's map for altered, frightening, empowering, or visionary phenomena that can arise between ordinary surface experience and Source.

## Source Snapshot
- **Path**: `raw/Shinzen Sources/science-of-enlightenment/07-the-realm-of-power.md`
- **Origin**: Shinzen Young, *The Science of Enlightenment: How Meditation Works*, Sounds True.
- **Date**: 2016.
- **Format**: Book chapter.
- **Reliability**: Primary for Shinzen's teaching model and practice rhetoric; anecdotal and interpretive for powers, visions, shamanism, cross-tradition mappings, and altered-state safety.
- **Scope**: Explains the intermediate realm as blockage and power, gives practice instructions for unusual phenomena, and warns against freak-out and spiritual materialism; it does not establish objective powers, clinical differentials, or ethical safeguards for power use.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen frames the path as a 90-degree turn from surface experience through an intermediate layer toward Source, not as a 180-degree rejection of the world.
- **S2**: Surface consciousness treats self and objects as solid, separate things in rigid space and linear time; Source contact is said to reveal nonseparate self/world, elastic or collapsing space, and cyclic arising and returning.
- **S3**: Source is described as ground state, God, Brahman, Tao, Unborn, Zero, Emptiness, or Completeness, but Shinzen also says the level metaphor is misleading because Source is formless activity rather than a thing.
- **S4**: The intermediate layer is identified with the subconscious and can be interpreted as the realm of blockages, samskaras, archetypes, powers, or sambhogakaya.
- **S5**: Shinzen is skeptical and agnostic about the objective status of psychic powers, entities, spirits, past lives, and out-of-body travel; vivid subjective sensory reality is not the same as objective existence.
- **S6**: Extraordinary phenomena are not required for extraordinary growth; ordinary sensations plus consistent concentration, sensory clarity, equanimity, and improving daily life can traverse the whole path.
- **S7**: When unusual phenomena occur, Shinzen says they can be treated as combinations of physical body sensation, emotional body sensation, mental image, and internal talk.
- **S8**: Spiritual maturity is measured by relation to intermediate-realm content: meet frightening, blissful, empowering, or ordinary events with the same concentration, clarity, and equanimity rather than craving, fearing, or comparing experiences.
- **S9**: Shinzen's own waking visions of giant insects are presented as functional, nonpsychotic intermediate-realm phenomena that he recycled into mental-image, emotional-body, Flow, fading, and vanishing practice rather than suppressing them or taking messages from them.
- **S10**: CCE can sometimes make visionary material more realistic before it dissolves; Shinzen interprets this as insight into impermanence as a creative Flow that animates both visionary and conventional reality.
- **S11**: Freak-Out may begin through a cultivated path, illness, trauma, sleep deprivation, drugs, fasting, or chance; the chapter classifies three extreme responses to the realm of power: Freak-Out, Diversion, and Plumb Line.
- **S12**: Diversion names horizontal exploration of powers, visions, entities, status, and spiritual materialism rather than vertical movement toward Source.
- **S13**: Shinzen treats powers as potentially good signs of deep-mind contact and possible service tools, but says serious cultivation of them is best after Source contact; liberation is dimensionally independent from power.
- **S14**: Oblique paths mix power exploration and liberation; Shinzen uses shamanism, Lakota terminology, Castaneda, Wuguang, and Buddhist stories to distinguish power, insight, and their combination.
- **S15**: Power phenomena can be used as upaya by carrying clarity and equanimity down into the depths, rather than only as messages from the depths to the surface.
- **S16**: Specific upaya routes include tracking heat or nectar as impermanence, using out-of-body impressions as a witness platform, widening mind-reading or past-life impressions toward boundless consciousness, meeting bliss with equanimity, detecting subtle vibration in light, and using entities as conduits for clarity and equanimity.
- **S17**: Shinzen's final instruction is a middle position: decompose altered phenomena into body sensation, mental image, internal talk, impermanence waves, and cessation, then watch where the waves go as a route toward Source.

## Summary
The chapter begins with a three-layer map. Ordinary surface consciousness experiences separate selves and objects in rigid space and linear time. Source is the deepest layer, described through many religious and secular names but treated as formless ground-state activity. Between them is an intermediate layer, identified with the subconscious and interpreted through both blockage language and power/archetype language.

Shinzen's most useful move is epistemic. He does not claim to know whether powers, entities, spirits, past lives, or out-of-body travel objectively exist. He says they can become subjectively vivid, but vividness is not objective existence. The practical question is how to use them. If nothing unusual happens, the practitioner may still be doing fine when ordinary experience is met with CCE and daily life improves. If unusual phenomena do happen, they are practice material: image, talk, emotional body, physical body, Flow, fading, and Gone.

The chapter then gives a maturity taxonomy. Freak-Out turns back toward normalcy in fear. Diversion turns horizontally into fascinating or status-granting content, producing spiritual materialism. Plumb Line keeps the vertical orientation by meeting all content with the same concentration, clarity, and equanimity. Oblique paths mix power and liberation, and Shinzen places much classical shamanism on that continuum.

The final section reframes power phenomena as upaya. Heat, nectar, bliss, light, out-of-body states, mind-reading impressions, past-life impressions, entities, and archetypes can be used to move clarity and equanimity into deep mind. This makes powers neither forbidden nor final. They are useful only insofar as they support insight, purification, and Source contact.

## Source Frame
The source wants the reader to neither dismiss nor chase altered phenomena. It protects ordinary practice by saying unusual experiences are not necessary, protects epistemic humility by distinguishing vivid subjective experience from objective existence, and protects practice depth by warning against spiritual materialism.

Its dominant frame is contemplative phenomenology. Freud, Jung, Buddhist trikaya language, Christian mysticism, Zen makyo warnings, shamanism, Lakota terms, Castaneda, and Buddhist stories are used as interpretive bridges, not as independently evidenced comparative scholarship. The chapter's practical center is narrow and strong inside Shinzen's system: whatever arises should be decomposed into sensory components and met with CCE.

A strong critic would press the clinical and ethical gaps. The chapter says illness, trauma, sleep deprivation, drugs, fasting, or random chance can initiate intermediate-realm descent, but it does not give differential diagnosis, stop rules, medical guidance, or teacher qualifications. It also says powers can help others after Source contact, but does not define consent, competence, verification, or power-safety safeguards.

## Weakest Claims
- The Source, ground-state, God, ground of being, and physics analogies are interpretive and should not be promoted into metaphysics or science.
- The Freud/Jung/Buddhist/subconscious mapping is a useful teaching comparison, not demonstrated psychology or comparative religion.
- Shinzen's claim that his waking insect visions were not psychotic depends on his functioning and interpretation; it is not a general clinical differential for hallucinations.
- Claims about Wuguang curing illness, locating children, or exorcising possession remain teaching anecdotes, not verified evidence for psychic or healing powers.
- Shamanism, Lakota terms, Castaneda, and Buddhist stories are used rhetorically and should not become authority for historical or ethnographic claims.
- The instruction that all unusual phenomena reduce to sensory components is central to Shinzen's system but not a complete safety rule.

## Important Omissions
- No criteria distinguish practice-relevant visions from psychosis, mania, trauma activation, dissociation, medication effects, sleep deprivation, substance effects, neurological issues, or medical crisis.
- No stop rules, grounding rules, referral criteria, or teacher-competence requirements are given for frightening or intensifying altered phenomena.
- No ethical safeguards are given for healing, exorcism, mind-reading claims, channeling, past-life claims, out-of-body claims, or using powers to help others.
- No verification standards distinguish post-Source service from charisma, grandiosity, status-seeking, or teacher power abuse.
- No developmental criteria specify when power cultivation is safe or appropriate after Source contact.
- No strong evidence is offered for the objective existence of powers or entities, and Shinzen explicitly remains skeptical or agnostic.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The chapter says powers are not required and can divert practice, but also calls them good signs of deep-mind contact and possible service tools.
- The chapter's direct-Source-contact language sits in tension with chapter 10's later and stricter claim that Source or Zero is known only afterward as afterglow because no self experiences the cancellation directly.
- The chapter treats hallucination-like material as workable practice for Shinzen, while also naming illness, trauma, sleep deprivation, drugs, fasting, and chance as possible conditions for descent into the intermediate realm; this creates a major clinical boundary.
- The chapter warns against status around powers while using teacher stories and cross-cultural power examples that could themselves attract status-seeking.
- The chapter's "be indifferent to what they mean, fascinated with how they move" instruction is excellent for practice routing but can conflict with contexts where meaning, medical assessment, relational repair, or safety planning matters.
