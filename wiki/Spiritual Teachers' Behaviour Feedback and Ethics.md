## Teaching Register
The register is informal ethics guidance and teacher-accountability commentary. Shinzen uses public teacher scandals as the entry point, then shifts from condemnation to a practical diagnosis: pedestal dynamics and teacher independence can remove the feedback loops that would reveal blind spots.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk adds a missing operational ethics handle to Gate 6A. It does not make ethics primarily an attainment claim or a legal code; it makes ethics a living feedback ecology, with humility about false positives and enough openness for real patterns to emerge.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Reports of abusive spiritual teachers should be held together with two calibrations: many teachers are not abusive, and many practitioners make radical life improvements despite uneven growth.
- **S2**: Uneven teacher development often comes from unwillingness or inability to receive behavioral feedback.
- **S3**: Pedestal structures can block feedback because students may interpret perceived teacher flaws as flaws in themselves, while teachers may refuse continued senior supervision or dismiss feedback from family and non-meditators.
- **S4**: A teacher should decide from the beginning to listen to feedback from everyone, including children, adults, advanced practitioners, and people with no spiritual-path knowledge.
- **S5**: Much student criticism may be projection or "student stuff," but teachers still need to invest the time because repeated consensus can reveal their real blind spots.
- **S6**: Shinzen treats the four basic Buddhist precepts as guidelines, but says ethical cultivation mainly comes through general openness to feedback from all human beings about how one carries oneself in the world.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S2**: The source turns uneven development from a mystery about realization into a correctable feedback-loop problem.
- **S3**: Shinzen identifies both structural blockage and personal refusal: students may not speak, senior-teacher links may be dropped, and ordinary-family feedback may be dismissed.
- **S5**: The "80 or 90 percent may be student stuff" caveat preserves discernment without excusing closedness; the point is to stay available long enough for consensus patterns.
- **S6**: The ethics move is not anti-precept but anti-legalism: rules remain minimal guidelines, while behavioral feedback is the ongoing cultivation method.

## Practice Handles
For a teacher, guide, or community, this talk suggests:

- Build feedback channels before status and independence make them hard to establish.
- Keep senior-teacher, peer, student, family, and non-practitioner feedback available instead of only listening to those who share the meditation frame.
- Treat individual criticism as data, not automatic truth; look for consensus patterns over time.
- Budget time and emotional energy for feedback even when much of it turns out not to be actionable.
- Use precepts as basic guidelines, but do not mistake rule familiarity for ethical maturation.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by calibrating the scandal frame. Some prominent spiritual teachers commit flagrant abuses, but many teachers do not, and many practitioners who never become public teachers make radical overall improvements while still showing uneven growth. He also warns against remembering only the single public failure and losing the larger picture of transformation.

He then gives a feedback-loop explanation for uneven teacher growth. Teachers may be unable to get feedback because students place them on a pedestal and assume the teacher's flaws must be the student's flaws. Senior teachers could provide correction, but many long-practicing teachers do not want to remain under a more senior teacher. Family may provide feedback, but a teacher may dismiss it when family members are not meditators.

The practical answer is a standing willingness and structure for feedback from everyone. Shinzen acknowledges that student feedback often reflects the student's own material and consumes scarce time, but insists that teachers should invest that time because consensus can reveal blind spots. He then contrasts rule-based or legalistic ethics with his own posture: the basic Buddhist precepts are real guidelines, but ethical cultivation mainly means openness to human feedback about one's conduct.

## Source Frame
This is a practitioner-teacher accountability teaching, not a formal institutional policy. Shinzen wants to keep scandal, transformation, and uneven growth in one picture: misconduct is real, spiritual practice can radically improve lives, and continued feedback is needed because development remains uneven. The talk's strongest contribution is the feedback mechanism; its weakest institutional side is that it does not specify reporting structures, protection for harmed students, retaliation prevention, or what to do when consensus shows serious harm.

## Weakest Claims
- The counterfactual claim that flawed teachers may have made "stunning transformation" relative to who they would otherwise have been is plausible inside Shinzen's frame but not evidenced in the transcript.
- The estimate that 80 or 90 percent of student criticism may be "student stuff" is an illustrative teaching estimate, not a measured finding.
- Feedback openness is necessary but not sufficient for ethics; serious misconduct may require protection, investigation, repair, reporting, removal, or outside authority.

## Important Omissions
- No formal misconduct-response process, grievance channel design, anti-retaliation rule, consent standard, financial safeguard, or reporting threshold.
- No criteria for distinguishing useful consensus from groupthink, community capture, teacher charisma, retaliation fear, or factional conflict.
- No account of how harmed students, vulnerable students, or non-student family members should be protected when giving feedback to a powerful teacher.
- No detail on what teachers should do when feedback reveals serious misconduct rather than ordinary blind spots.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen asks listeners to keep the big picture of teacher transformation while also naming flagrant abuse; without formal accountability, the big-picture frame could minimize concrete harm.
- He says much student feedback may be projection, but also says teachers need student feedback to reveal blind spots; the balance depends on consensus, time, and structures that the talk does not define.
- He criticizes legalistic ethics but still relies on basic precepts; the relationship between minimal rules and feedback-based cultivation remains only sketched.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Keep the big picture in mind" could be used to soften or excuse abuse unless paired with protection, repair, and accountability.
- "Most feedback is student stuff" could become a defensive shield if the teacher or institution controls the interpretation.
- "Ethics as feedback openness" could be too weak where legal, clinical, or organizational intervention is needed.
- Family or non-practitioner feedback should not be dismissed as spiritually uninformed, but it also should not be treated as automatically correct without inquiry.
