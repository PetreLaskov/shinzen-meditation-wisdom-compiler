This nine-minute Shinzen Young YouTube talk continues the concentration series. Its main teaching move is definitional: concentration is attending to what one deems relevant, and that relevance can be narrow, broad, external, constructive, or ordinary-life complex without requiring suppression of everything else.

## Teaching Register
Instruction with autobiographical and comparative framing. Shinzen uses his own early attraction to samadhi, positive psychology vocabulary, athletic idiom, Sasaki Roshi's teaching, and driving examples to correct common assumptions about concentration.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk gives [[Concentration Power]] its cleanest oral definition and adds two safeguards. First, concentration is not identical with narrow attention; broad subjective or outward fields can be equally concentrated when they are relevant. Second, concentration practice should become a positive feedback loop, not a struggle against excluded material.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: High concentration is intrinsically rewarding, reduces the problem of uncomfortable experience, supports performance, and connects Shinzen's practice to a cross-cultural contemplative phenomenon.
- **S2**: Csikszentmihalyi's positive-psychology "flow" names the intrinsic reward of high concentration, while Shinzen's [[Flow]] names change, energy, and force; the terms overlap in life but should stay conceptually distinct inside his system.
- **S3**: Concentration power is the ability to attend to what one considers relevant, in time; relevance is chosen contextually and may differ by person and situation.
- **S4**: Concentration need not mean narrow restriction; breath at the nostrils is contractive samadhi, while simultaneously holding positive image, talk, and emotional feeling across subjective space is expansive samadhi.
- **S5**: Broad outward activity can be concentration practice: driving in rush-hour traffic can become deep repose when touch, sight, and sound linked to the task remain continuously relevant.
- **S6**: Concentration is distorted when the practitioner fights, tenses around, pushes down, or rejects material outside the focus range.
- **S7**: The intended feedback loop is that stronger focus feels better; aversive suppression makes focus uncomfortable because the practitioner is literally fighting with oneself.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S2**: The terminology warning prevents positive-psychology flow state from being collapsed into Shinzen's technical Flow route.
- **S3-S5**: Relevance, not narrowness, is the governing criterion. This lets concentration include breath focus, Nurture Positive, driving, and other broad fields.
- **S6-S7**: Non-suppressive focus is a safety and effort calibration. The object can be selected without making non-selected material an enemy.

## Practice Handles
- Ask, "What is relevant now?" before deciding whether concentration should be narrow, broad, internal, external, or constructive.
- Use small objects such as nostril breath or abdominal movement for contractive samadhi when narrowing is useful.
- Use image, talk, and positive emotional body sensation together when constructive expansive samadhi is the better route.
- Treat ordinary tasks such as driving as possible broad concentration only when the task-relevant sensory links stay clear and continuous.
- When distractions arise, return to the chosen object without tensing around, pushing down, or resenting the other material.
- Keep Shinzen's Flow vocabulary separate from "flow state" unless the topic explicitly concerns their overlap.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by extending part 1's claim that concentration is intrinsically fulfilling and useful in difficulty. He links that reward to Csikszentmihalyi's positive-psychology term "flow," then immediately warns that this English word collides with his own technical use of Flow for change, energy, and force. The two are related in practice because deeper focus can reveal changingness and energetic process, but they are not the same concept.

He then defines concentration as the ability to attend to what one considers relevant. That definition deliberately excludes the assumption that concentration always narrows attention. Breath sensations at the nostrils or abdomen are examples of contractive samadhi. A positive image, matching internal talk, and corresponding positive emotional feeling held together across subjective space are examples of expansive samadhi. Driving supplies an outward broad example: the relevant field is mostly physical sight, sound, and touch linked to the road and car.

The talk closes with an effort warning. Shinzen says he spent years practicing concentration while subtly suppressing distractions. He would choose an object, then tense around anything else that appeared. That aversive agenda dissipated energy and made concentration painful. The correction is to focus on what is relevant without rejecting everything outside the focus.

## Source Frame
The strongest evidence is Shinzen's own definition and practice calibration. The positive psychology reference functions as a vocabulary contrast and outside analogy, not as the wiki's independent claim about Csikszentmihalyi's research. The driving and Nurture Positive examples are teaching illustrations for broad relevance-based focus; they do not prove that any complex activity is automatically meditative.

## Important Omissions
- The talk does not provide a full protocol for working with distractions without suppression.
- It does not distinguish productive return-to-object from avoidance, dissociation, dullness, or hypervigilance.
- It does not yet complete the three-part concentration series' model of depth and breadth.
- It does not explain how expansive samadhi interacts with sensory clarity and equanimity beyond the no-fighting warning.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Concentration chooses relevance, but the choice can still become rigid or aversive if non-relevant material is treated as a threat.
- Shinzen distinguishes positive-psychology flow from his technical Flow, while also admitting that deeper focus and awareness of change are not entirely unrelated.
- The talk values broad task concentration, but ordinary complex tasks may have safety demands that override meditative experimentation.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Driving can be samadhi" should not be used to justify risky introspection, altered-state pursuit, or reduced attention to road conditions.
- "Expansive samadhi" should not become forced positivity or overmanaged image-talk-feel construction.
- "Do not fight distractions" should not be confused with letting attention drift away from the relevant object indefinitely.
- The flow-state vocabulary should not erase Shinzen's technical Flow category.
