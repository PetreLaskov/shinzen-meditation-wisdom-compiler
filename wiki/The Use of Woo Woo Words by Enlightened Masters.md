This three-minute `@ShinzenVideos` short is an informal teaching-language
calibration. Shinzen treats "woo woo" words as neither automatically wise nor
automatically fraudulent: they can help, they can get in the way, teacher
incentives can be mixed, and his own later practice experience changed how
he read earlier Zen master language.

## Teaching Register
Informal Q&A-style transmission calibration. The talk is not a method
instruction; it is Shinzen exposing his own interpretive shift so students do
not either swallow mystical language whole or dismiss it reflexively.

## Why This Talk Matters
The wiki already has jargon, lineage translation, and teacher-authority
boundaries. This talk adds a small but important middle position: skepticism
about teacher incentives is warranted, but cynicism can also miss the way
practice may make strange words feel phenomenologically natural.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen says woo-woo words can help and can also get in the way.
- **S2**: He says teachers have a range of motivations, including possible
  financial or institutional incentives around keeping students engaged.
- **S3**: He reports that as a young student he was highly cynical about
  mystical language, sometimes reading it as invented to deceive believers or
  as an attention-grabbing Zen style.
- **S4**: After later practice and teaching, he recognizes himself saying
  similar things and reinterprets some earlier master-language as arising
  from practice realization or teaching pressure rather than only deception
  or affectation.
- **S5**: The talk leaves teacher motivation plural and case-specific; it
  does not claim that all woo-woo language is sincere, useful, or harmless.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: The practical rule is two-sided: unusual mystical language can be a
  practice aid or a practice obstacle.
- **S2**: Teacher incentives remain part of interpretation, especially where
  special language reinforces payment, loyalty, or student dependence.
- **S3-S4**: Shinzen's autobiographical reversal protects against premature
  cynicism without removing the need for translation, testing, and
  accountability.
- **S5**: The source keeps authority calibrated: recognizing a sincere
  possibility is not the same as validating every teacher or every phrase.

## Practice Handles
- When a teacher uses mystical, Source, Zen, or "woo woo" language, ask what
  the words help a practitioner notice, do, or re-understand.
- Keep teacher incentives visible: does the language clarify practice, or
  does it make the teacher, lineage, payment stream, or special vocabulary
  more central than the student's direct experience?
- Do not convert skepticism into reflexive dismissal. Some strange language
  may be a compressed attempt to speak from or toward practice experience.
- Do not convert sympathy into reverence. Even sincere strange language still
  needs translation, direct testing, feedback, and ordinary accountability.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by saying that mystical or "woo woo" words can be useful and
can also interfere. He immediately ties that ambiguity to teacher motivation:
teachers are not all moved by one pure or impure cause, and some language may
serve institutional or financial continuity.

He then describes his earlier cynicism. As a young person interested in Asian
languages and contemplative material, he thought a lot of mystical talk was
made up, either to deceive faithful students or to grab attention through
strange Zen-style speech. He reports hearing major Japanese Zen teachers in
Asia and initially interpreting their language through that suspicious lens.

The turn in the talk comes when Shinzen says he now finds himself saying
similar things. That does not make every teacher sincere or every odd phrase
valid; it changes the interpretive default. Strange master-language may still
need translation and accountability, but it may also come from direct practice
pressure rather than manipulation or stylistic affectation.

## Source Frame
The source is a teaching-transmission confession more than an argument. Its
strongest evidence is for Shinzen's own posture: he wants students to hold
skepticism and receptivity together when evaluating mystical vocabulary.

The weakest frame is other-teacher motivation. Shinzen explicitly names a
range of motives, but a short informal clip cannot establish what specific
teachers intended. Use the source to calibrate interpretation of teacher
language, not to diagnose individual teachers, Japanese Zen history, or the
validity of any particular mystical phrase.

  student or donation streams is plausible as an incentive warning, but the

- The retrospective claim that he now understands the motivation behind some
  master-language is based on his own later experience; it should not be
  generalized to all teachers or traditions.
- The transcript is quality B and noisy around proper names and some idioms,

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen validates suspicion about teacher incentives while also warning
  against his own former over-cynicism.
- He treats some strange language as retrospectively understandable while
  still saying such words can obstruct.
- The clip uses informal insider-teacher talk while the wiki has to turn it
  into a bounded accountability and translation rule.

## Easily Misused Claims
- The talk can be misused to license vague mystical language without
  translation, practice function, or accountability.
- It can also be misused for blanket cynicism: if a teacher uses unusual
  words, assume manipulation.
- Teacher-incentive suspicion can become gossip unless tied to concrete
  effects, feedback channels, consent, dependency, or financial pressure.
- Shinzen's later sympathy for strange language should not be treated as
  endorsement of every Zen, Source, or guru-style phrase.
