Its durable contribution is not mathematical precision but a compact practice model for how discomfort and pleasure become either binding or fulfilling.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Suffering equals discomfort multiplied by resistance, where discomfort means physical or emotional uncomfortable body sensation and resistance means interference with those sensations' natural expansion and contraction.
- **S2**: Most resistance is not merely mental attitude; Shinzen says the body circuits themselves can interfere with their own sensory productions at a deep, primordial level.
- **S3**: Large discomfort with little resistance can produce little or no perceived suffering, while tiny or even subliminal discomfort with huge resistance can produce huge suffering and distort behavior.
- **S4**: Purification equals discomfort multiplied by equanimity; discomfort with enough noninterference can become a positive taste of purification and fulfillment.
- **S5**: Frustration equals pleasure multiplied by resistance; pushing and pulling on pleasure's arising or passing prevents pleasure from becoming fulfilling.
- **S6**: Fulfillment equals pleasure multiplied by equanimity; even tiny pleasures can yield large fulfillment when the pleasure circuits are trained out of impedance.
- **S7**: Shinzen closes by saying consciousness can purify in two ways: discomfort with equanimity and pleasure with equanimity.

## Practice Handles
A practitioner or guide can use the formulas diagnostically. With discomfort, the question is not only "how intense is this?" but "how much interference is present in the body-mind circuits?" With pleasure, the question is not only "how much pleasure is available?" but "is craving or resistance preventing fulfillment?" The talk also points attention toward subtle body-emotion: wispy, barely noticed impatience, fear, shame, or sadness may matter because high subliminal resistance can amplify suffering and distort behavior.

## Summary
Shinzen defines discomfort as uncomfortable physical body sensation or emotional body flavors such as anger, fear, sadness, shame, or impatience. Resistance is any pushing, pulling, fighting, holding, or interference with the natural flow of those sensations. He states the first formula as suffering equals discomfort times resistance, then gives its two-sided implication: high discomfort with low resistance can mean low suffering, while low discomfort with high subliminal resistance can mean large suffering and behavioral distortion.

He then gives the discomfort-side positive formula: purification equals discomfort times equanimity. Equanimity is the opposite of resistance, approximately reciprocal to it. When discomfort is met with enough noninterference, it can become not only less suffering but a positive taste that something useful is happening in the depths of one's being.

The pleasure side mirrors the same structure. Pleasure multiplied by resistance gives frustration, because pleasure's arising or passing is being pushed and pulled. Pleasure multiplied by equanimity gives fulfillment; even small pleasures can yield deep fulfillment when the pleasure circuits are trained into noninterference. Shinzen ends by equating the purification flavor from pleasure-with-equanimity with the purification flavor from discomfort-with-equanimity.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk makes it theoretically possible for suffering to reach zero even with huge discomfort, but the transcript gives no diagnostic for distinguishing that from dissociation, collapse, shock, or denial.
- It says small pleasures can produce large fulfillment with enough equanimity, but does not explain how to distinguish fulfillment from subtle pleasure-chasing, positivity bypass, or state preference.
- The formulas are useful because they are simple; the source itself admits the underlying process is probably more complex.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Suffering can be zero" could be misused to minimize pain, illness, coercion, trauma response, or ordinary needs.
- "Tiny resisted sensations can cause huge suffering" could become overconfident interpretation of another person's behavior without evidence.
- "Pleasure with equanimity purifies" could be used to defend indulgence or bliss-chasing if equanimity, clarity, behavior, and support are not checked.
