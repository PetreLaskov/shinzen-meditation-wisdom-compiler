The Sensory Grid is the manual's working classification system for experience. Chapter 7 makes the periodic-table analogy explicit: a small set of sensory "atoms" can be used to analyze complex experience, guide practice, and support behavior change.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: The grid classifies sensory experience by modality and theme: Basic Mindfulness uses See/Hear/Feel crossed with In/Out/Rest/Flow plus Gone, while the SHF introduction presents See/Hear/Feel crossed with In/Out/Rest/Flow/Space.
- **Why this matters**: It turns "be mindful" into routable practice: drill into one cell, float through a column, float through a row, note everything, make Gone itself the focus, or use the grid as a guidance and teaching toolkit.
- **Key tensions**: Shinzen presents the grid as comprehensive, simple, natural, deep, useful, and beautiful, while also admitting that smell/taste placement, Feel In/Feel Out, and emotional-body detection can feel artificial.
- **Source posture**: Strong as Shinzen's Basic Mindfulness taxonomy and label system; weaker as a literal scientific or exhaustive model of all human experience.

## Model
The Basic Mindfulness grid has three modality rows and four theme columns ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S3):

- **Visual**: See In, See Out, See Rest, See Flow.
- **Auditory**: Hear In, Hear Out, Hear Rest, Hear Flow.
- **Somatic**: Feel In, Feel Out, Feel Rest, Feel Flow.

The columns organize themes:

- **In**: inwardly arising mental image, mental talk, and emotional body sensation.
- **Out**: physically originating sight, sound, and body sensation.
- **Rest**: visual blankness or defocus, auditory quiet or silence, and somatic relaxation or emotional peace.
- **Flow**: movement, change, energy, or force in visual, auditory, or somatic experience.

The later SHF introduction preserves the three rows but presents five themes: In, Out, Rest, Flow, and Space ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Five Themes Space and Depth Boundary]] S1). Space is short for Spaciousness, defined as the openness around and/or thinness within what is seen, heard, or felt; the cells are See Space, Hear Space, and Feel Space ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Five Themes Space and Depth Boundary]] S2).

Unit 06 also turns the columns into five focus families. Focus In untangles self, Focus Out merges with the surrounding scene, Focus on Rest attenuates self and world into rest states, Focus on Flow dissolves them into energy, and Focus on Space shows "the perfection that was always there" in Shinzen's source language ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Five Themes Space and Depth Boundary]] S3-S5). That last slogan should remain source-attributed because the unit gives little operational detail for Space beyond the definition.

The source explicitly gives two language modes. "Mental image," "physical sound," and "somatic flow" are descriptive language for discussing experience. "See In," "Hear Out," and "Feel Flow" are short rhythmic labeling language for practice ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S8).

The SHF introduction later compresses the grid into a three-point modality triangle. See, Hear, and Feel are the root labels in that order. With an unrestricted range, See can label physical sight, mental image, visual rest, visual flow, or visual spaciousness; Hear can label physical sound, mental talk, auditory rest, auditory flow, or auditory spaciousness; Feel can label physical body sensation, emotional body sensation, smell and taste, body rest, body flow, or body spaciousness ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S1-S3).

## Cell Meanings
The first five manual Ways define the cells in practice.

- **See In**: mental images, including body/self image, ambient place image, and central memory-plan-fantasy imagery when those distinctions matter ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 1 - The Way of Thoughts and Emotions]] S7; [[Working with Images and Image Space]] S1-S5).
- **Hear In**: mental talk ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 1 - The Way of Thoughts and Emotions]] S8).
- **Feel In**: emotional body sensation ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 1 - The Way of Thoughts and Emotions]] S9-S10).
- **See Out**: physical sight ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 2 - The Way of the Physical Senses]] S4-S5).
- **Hear Out**: physical sound ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 2 - The Way of the Physical Senses]] S6-S7).
- **Feel Out**: physically originating body sensation, even when located inside the body ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 2 - The Way of the Physical Senses]] S8-S10).
- **See Rest**: visual rest, including eyes-closed blankness or open/closed-eye soft focus ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S4).
- **Hear Rest**: auditory rest, including mental quiet, physical silence, or white noise used as rest ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S5).
- **Feel Rest**: somatic rest, including physical relaxation, emotional peace, or temporary absence of emotional body sensation ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S6).
- **See Flow**: visual movement, morphing, fading, animation, vibration, pixilation, shimmering, or detectable shifts from body movement, eye movement, and small attentional movement within sight space ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S5; [[Sight Space How Detecting Shifts Can Lead to Flow]] S1-S3).
- **Hear Flow**: auditory vibration, spread, grip, hum, subtle talk flow, or moving physical sound ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S5).
- **Feel Flow**: somatic movement, pressure, spread, collapse, vibration, size change, frequency change, or intensity change ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S5).

[[Gone]] cross-cuts all cells. It marks the noticed moment when all or part of a sensory event drops away or drops off. Continued absence after the vanishing is Rest, not Gone ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S5).

[[Dynamic Aspects of the Sensory System]] adds a compact oral overlay to this table. Shinzen says the ordinary flavors, Touch, Sight, Sound, Feel, Image, and Talk, and the restful flavors, relaxation, light, silence, peace, blank, and quiet, may be still and stable or may show dynamic aspect: change, waviness, vibration, undulation, spreading, collapsing, inward/outward force, Expansion, or Contraction. Continuous dynamic change routes to Flow; discontinuous abrupt vanishing routes to Gone ([[Dynamic Aspects of the Sensory System]] S1-S5).

## Operational Conventions
Chapter 7 is unusually useful because it records the grid's conventions and artificialities.

[[6 Buddhist Consciousnesses and the 12 Sensory States]] gives the oral bridge from traditional Buddhist categories to Shinzen's working grid. Shinzen starts with hearing, seeing, smelling, tasting, body sensation, and thinking, then splits thinking into Talk and Image, body sensation into Feel and Touch, and folds smell and taste into physical body sensation for simplicity and symmetry ([[6 Buddhist Consciousnesses and the 12 Sensory States]] S1-S4).

[[Mindfulness and the Categories of Sensory Experience]] gives a second oral rationale: Shinzen says the Basic States were designed around contrasts that could support meditator insight and possible fMRI comparison. The relevant contrasts are auditory/visual/somatic, relatively objective/subjective, and ordinary active/restful ([[Mindfulness and the Categories of Sensory Experience]] S1-S2, S5). This makes the grid a pragmatic contrast system rather than a literal claim that experience naturally comes in exactly these boxes.

[[Sensory Clarity Insight Through Monitoring Ordinary and Restful States]]
shows those contrasts becoming a live experiment. Focus on Ordinary can work
with Image/Talk, Touch/Sight/Sound, or combinations; Focus on Rest can float
among relaxation, peace, blank, quiet, light or soft focus, and silence. The
same grid also reveals self-arising when outer contact triggers reactive
Feel/Image/Talk and no-self when the inner system is inactive or remains
untangled ([[Sensory Clarity Insight Through Monitoring Ordinary and Restful
States]] S1-S7).

[[Natural Sensory Space Combinations]] adds the flexibility rule for the same
six spaces. Shinzen says one can group them as body/mind/world, as
subjective Feel/Image/Talk versus objective Touch/Sight/Sound, or as
somatic/visual/auditory pairings. The path makes distinctions and destroys
distinctions, so the correct grouping is not one fixed partition but the one
that works under the larger practice criteria ([[Natural Sensory Space
Combinations]] S1-S5).

[[Working with Images and Image Space]] applies that flexibility inside a
single cell. See In may need to be subdivided by image-space location:
body/self imagery, ambient imagery that gives a sense of place, and central
memory-plan-fantasy imagery. The grid still labels all of this as image, but
practice sometimes benefits from knowing which image location is active
([[Working with Images and Image Space]] S1-S4).

[[Discrimination and Unification - Part 4 of 4]] gives the same taxonomy a
path-level rationale. Shinzen says the important healthy distinctions include
somatic, visual, and auditory experience; subjective Feel/Image/Talk versus
objective touch/sight/sound; smell and taste folded into touch; and six
corresponding restful states. The strong "all and only" wording should remain
source-attributed, but it clarifies why the grid exists: these distinctions
make experience manageable without opposing oneness ([[Discrimination and
Unification - Part 4 of 4]] S6-S8).

Smell and taste are classified as Feel Out because they occur through the body, specifically nose and mouth. This keeps the grid simple, but it is a convention rather than an obvious natural category ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S5).

Unit 03 keeps the same convention in the simplified SHF interface. Smell and taste still fall under Feel because they are experienced somatically, and because the simplified system prioritizes symmetry and usability over a perfectly intuitive category boundary ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S3).

"Out" means physical in origin, not outside the skin. This explains why a stomach sensation, muscle pain, nausea, or breath sensation can be Feel Out if treated as physically originating, while emotional fear in the belly can be Feel In ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S7).

Feel In is operational rather than metaphysical. The practitioner asks whether anything emotional is present anywhere in awareness, then asks whether part of that emotion appears in the body. If no emotion is present, or if no/not-sure body component is detected, the practitioner does not label Feel In for that moment; if yes, label Feel In ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S6).

This convention matters because the manual does not ask practitioners to solve ambiguous body sensations through analysis. It asks them to use a simple detection procedure and move on.

## Focus Options
The grid is scalable. Practitioners can drill down into one cell, then back up into wider fields. Shinzen says this trains both individual sensory circuits and integrated systems ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S1).

Column options:

- **Focus In** floats among mental image, mental talk, and emotional body sensation.
- **Focus Out** floats among physical sight, physical sound, and physical body sensation.
- **Focus on Rest** floats among visual, auditory, and somatic rest.
- **Focus on Flow** floats among See Flow, Hear Flow, and Feel Flow.
- **Focus on Space** floats among visual, auditory, and somatic Spaciousness in the later SHF table, though this unit gives only a definition and effect slogan rather than a detailed technique.

Row options:

- **Focus on See** floats among mental image, physical sight, visual rest, visual flow, and Gone.
- **Focus on Hear** floats among mental talk, physical sound, auditory rest, auditory flow, and Gone.
- **Focus on Feel** floats among emotional body sensation, physical body sensation, somatic rest, somatic flow, and Gone.

Global and special options:

- **Focus on Everything** floats among all 12 grid cells plus Gone, making 13 noteable objects ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S3).
- **Just Note Gone** focuses on vanishings themselves when they are frequent or salient enough ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S5-S8).
- **Expansion-Contraction** can replace modality emphasis when Flow is better known as outward/inward, increasing/decreasing, scattering/gripping, or both ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S6-S7).

Unit 03 adds the modern flexible-label rule: the meaning of the label depends on the focus range. If the range is restful states, See/Hear/Feel mean visual, auditory, and body rest. If the range is inner activity, the same labels mean mental image, mental talk, and emotional body sensation. If the range is outer activity, they mean physical sight, physical sound, and physical body sensation. Breath focus and body scan can use the single label Feel because the focus range is already somatic ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S7-S8).

Unit 04 defines focus range directly as the set of experiences selected for a practice period and makes four ranges the standard SHF beginner entry: Focus on See, Focus on Hear, Focus on Feel, and Focus on Everything ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S1-S2). These can be used singly, chosen in the moment, or sequenced as Everything, See, Hear, Feel, Everything, so the modern interface starts broad, scrolls through the modality rows, and returns to broad ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S3).

The same unit also maps simultaneous modalities. Exclusive emphasis chooses one target when multiple modalities arise together. Inclusive emphasis notes the co-occurrence through See Hear, See Feel, Hear Feel, or All. Temporal closeness is operational: if two events are close enough for the practitioner, they may be noted inclusively; if separated enough, they may be noted one after the other ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S4-S5).

Unit 04 also specifies the contact stance for those starter ranges. In Focus on See, Hear, or Feel, the practitioner actively returns when pulled outside the modality, while passively allowing inner activity, outer activity, or rest states within that modality. In Focus on Everything, the starter stance is passive and the starter co-occurrence rule is exclusive emphasis. If nothing much comes or calls, visual rest, auditory rest, and body rest still count as sensory experience and are labeled See, Hear, and Feel ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S6-S8).

The Focus Methods talk adds a coverage strategy over the grid. The same categories can be approached by free-floating among what calls, systematically inventorying a body or sensory field, or evenly covering a broad range. Shinzen maps Focus In, Focus Out, and Focus on Rest to free-floating as their usual mode, body scanning to systematic inventory, and zoomed-out Flow across all sensory spaces to even coverage ([[Focus Methods in Mindfulness Advantages and Disadvantages]] S1-S4, S8-S9).

Chapter 9 confirms these as the full standard focus-option chart available in Basic Mindfulness up to that point ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 9 - The Full Grid]] S1-S5).

Chapter 10 adds a symbol and mnemonic layer. The sequence "See, Hear, Feel; In, Out; Rest, Flow; Good To Go" is meant to compress the major categories, with Good pointing to positive states, "To" to the two Flow flavors, and "Go" to Gone ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 10 - Beauty]] S2-S4). Chapter 12 then says the full grid can be mined for self-practice strategy, teaching, handling Walls and Windows, sensory classification, and historical influence mapping ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S9).

## Construction
The grid is not only deconstructive. [[Nurture Positive]] uses the same inner image, talk, and emotional body systems constructively. A practitioner chooses a positive theme, checks it through talk-feel and image-feel, chooses the most natural system, and holds that theme in image, talk, emotional body, a pair, or all three together ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 5 - The Way of Human Goodness]] S10-S12).

This means the grid can locate both what is already arising and what is deliberately cultivated.

## Boundaries
The grid is a practice taxonomy, not proof that experience is only 12 boxes plus Gone or 15 boxes plus Space. Its value is operational: it gives [[Sensory Clarity]] something concrete to detect and discriminate. The simplified SHF triangle is likewise an interface, not a replacement ontology; its compact labels work only when the active range is understood.

The short natural-combinations talk strengthens that non-ontology boundary.
Different partitions can be equally legitimate when they serve different
practice needs; "it worked" should still be read through CCE, safety, and
behavioral criteria rather than preference alone ([[Natural Sensory Space
Combinations]] S5).

The chapter's own honesty about artificiality is important. The chemical senses, emotional-body ambiguity, and Feel In/Feel Out language should prevent future agents from treating the grid as a literal ontology ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S5-S7). Unit 06 adds an old/new interface tension: the Basic Mindfulness grid foregrounds Gone as a cross-cutting option, while the later SHF table foregrounds Space as a fifth theme. Both should be kept as teaching interfaces unless later sources justify a stronger synthesis.

The grid also does not solve safety. More precise labeling can help distinguish rest from shutdown, Flow from fixation, Gone from dissociation, and positivity from bypass, but classification alone does not supply stop rules, trauma criteria, medical triage, or behavioral verification.

Chapter 12's guidance use intensifies that boundary. A grid option can be a helpful tool for a Wall or Window, but the grid by itself does not decide scope of practice, teacher competence, clinical referral, or behavior follow-up.

## Implications
Practice routing can now ask two compact questions:

- Is the best move a cell, a column, a row, all experience, Gone, Do Nothing, or Nurture Positive?
- Should the selected field be free-floating, systematically inventoried, or evenly covered?
- Is the active need concentration, clarity, equanimity, relief, grounding, Flow, reconstruction, or safety/support?

This keeps the manual modular. A practitioner can stay with one box, alternate drill-down and back-up, cycle techniques, or route away from a destabilizing option.
