This 4-minute Shinzen Young YouTube talk is a compact instruction on the "dynamic aspect" of the sensory system. Its central contribution is to place Flow and vanishing inside all ordinary and restful sensory categories rather than beside them as a separate ontological layer.

## Teaching Register
Brief instructional taxonomy. Shinzen is not guiding a live student or arguing a full map; he is adding a practice option to the 12 ordinary/restful sensory experiences by naming their dynamic presentation when it becomes salient.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk prevents a common routing error: treating Touch, Sight, Sound, Feel, Image, Talk, and the six restful flavors as static boxes, then treating Flow or Gone as a separate exotic category. Shinzen instead says any of these categories may be still, stable, changing, forceful, energetic, flowing, or abruptly vanishing.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen lists six ordinary flavors, Touch, Sight, Sound, Feel, Image, and Talk, and six restful flavors, relaxation, light, silence, peace, blank, and quiet, as the 12 basic sensory experiences he has people work with.
- **S2**: Any ordinary or restful sensory category may be completely still and stable, or may have a dynamic aspect: change, waviness, vibration, undulation, spreading, collapsing, inward/outward forces, expansive forces, or contractive forces.
- **S3**: Shinzen uses "dynamic" because it means relating to change or to the forces and energies that produce change.
- **S4**: If the dynamic aspect is continuous change, Shinzen calls it Flow; if the change is discontinuous and something abruptly vanishes, the practice label is Gone.
- **S5**: Practitioners may focus on ordinary experience, restful experience, or the dynamic aspect when it presents itself and when they wish to emphasize it.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S2**: The 12 sensory experiences are not frozen states. The same category can be stable or dynamic, so the grid needs a state-versus-dynamic overlay.
- **S3-S4**: Flow and Gone are technical practice discriminations within the larger dynamic aspect: continuous change routes to Flow; abrupt disappearance routes to Gone.
- **S5**: The option is permissive rather than compulsory. The practitioner may shift to dynamic aspect when it becomes evident, but ordinary and restful experience remain valid focus objects.

## Practice Handles
- While noting any ordinary or restful sensory event, check whether the event is stable or dynamic.
- If the dynamic quality is wavy, vibratory, undulatory, spreading, collapsing, expansive, or contractive, emphasize Flow if useful.
- If the event abruptly disappears or drops off, note Gone.
- If no dynamic aspect is evident, stay with the ordinary or restful sensory category rather than manufacturing movement.

## Summary
Shinzen begins with the 12 basic sensory experiences: six ordinary categories and six restful contrasts. He then says any category may be still and stable or may reveal a dynamic aspect. That dynamic aspect includes change, vibration, waviness, spreading, collapsing, and inward/outward or expansive/contractive forces.

He chooses "dynamic" because the word joins change with the forces or energies that produce change. He briefly links "energy" to Christian contemplative vocabulary before its use as a physics term, interpreting it as Spirit moving through body, mind, and world, breaking things up and reducing them back toward Source.

The practical instruction is simple: if dynamic aspect presents itself, one may focus on it. Continuous change is Flow. Discontinuous change, where something abruptly vanishes, is vanishing or Gone. This creates a third possible emphasis alongside ordinary and restful experience: focus on the dynamic aspect when nature offers it.

## Source Frame
The talk is primarily a practice-taxonomy clarification. Its strongest evidence is for Shinzen's own language: dynamic aspect, Flow, vanishing, and their relation to the 12 sensory experiences. The Christian-energy and Source language functions as interpretive color and lineage translation; it should not be promoted into established history, physics, or metaphysics.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk says dynamic aspect may appear in any category while also preserving ordinary and restful focus. This creates a productive tension: Flow can unify the grid, but practice still needs category discrimination before unification is useful.
- Rest can be stable or dynamic. This pressures any simple calming-practice reading in which rest only means stillness.
- Energy language helps bridge Christian contemplation, Spirit, and Source, but can be misused as metaphysical proof or energy-chasing encouragement.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Energy" should not become a free-standing subtle-substance claim.
- "Spirit moving" and "taking you back to the Source" should remain source-attributed teaching language.
- Dynamic aspect should not become a demand to find movement in every object.
- Focusing on Flow or Gone should not override grounding, ordinary action, bodily care, or support needs.
