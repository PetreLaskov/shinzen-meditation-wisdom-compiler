This Shinzen YouTube talk is a compact instruction on Noting Gone inside Focus
on Change. Its main contribution is to make Gone both technically precise and
ordinary: any noticed abrupt diminishing counts, including partial,
temporary, breath-level, or mental-talk-level endings.

## Teaching Register
Instruction and advanced-map clarification. Shinzen is correcting common
misreadings of Gone while giving a practice confidence message: the category
does not require exotic perception, and one does not need to find vanishings
in every sensory channel.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk protects Gone from two opposite distortions. It prevents overmystic
scarcity by saying every breath ending and many talk endings are valid Gone
objects. It also prevents sloppy terminology by distinguishing abrupt
diminishing from continuous Flow and by insisting that Gone need not mean
permanent, whole-object disappearance.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Gone means a noticed abrupt diminishing; it does not imply gone for
  good, total disappearance, or every part of the observed event vanishing.
- **S2**: Continuous or fluid change belongs to Flow, while macroscopic abrupt
  diminishing is what Shinzen labels Gone.
- **S3**: Gone practice does not assume the practitioner can detect vanishings
  in all sensory categories; it assumes one can find Gone somewhere, such as
  breath endings or the endings of mental-talk sentences.
- **S4**: Shinzen says ordinary Gones are "uncreated equal": trivial endings,
  repeated over weeks, months, or years, gradually sensitize attention to the
  unborn Source of consciousness.
- **S5**: Noting vanishings can bring obvious relief with unpleasant
  experience, counterintuitive fulfillment with pleasant and neutral
  experience, Rest aftereffects, and a sense of richness rather than
  bleakness.
- **S6**: Shinzen connects endings to Sanskrit nirodha and nirvana, glossing
  nirvana as both blowing out or quenching suffering and quenching thirst as
  complete fulfillment.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: This is the practice definition that keeps Gone usable in real
  sensory fields where events partly collapse, reduce, or return.
- **S2**: The Flow/Gone boundary preserves two distinct Focus on Change
  routes: continuous movement versus abrupt drop-off.
- **S3**: The talk removes performance pressure from Gone practice. Breath and
  chatter endings are enough to practice the technique "absolutely perfect."
- **S4**: "All Gones are uncreated equal" is important oral idiolect: it makes
  small vanishings the same practice gateway as dramatic ones without making
  their sensory scale equal.
- **S5**: The relief/fulfillment/richness sequence explains why Gone practice
  is not merely absence training or pessimistic impermanence contemplation.

## Practice Handles
A practitioner using Noting Gone can count partial collapse, sudden reduction
in intensity, smaller size, or the end of a sensory flavor as Gone when the
transition is noticed. If no dramatic vanishing is available, breath endings
and mental-talk periods are sufficient. The practical assumption is not
"find Gone everywhere"; it is "find a real ending somewhere and note it
cleanly."

## Summary
Shinzen begins by warning that his technical word Gone differs from colloquial
English. Gone need not mean permanent disappearance and need not mean the
whole object is absent. If a visual image loses an arm, if a quadrant of body
pain abruptly collapses, or if an event suddenly diminishes to a lower level
or smaller size, that counts as Gone when noticed.

He then distinguishes Gone from Flow. Flow is continuous or fluid change,
including wavy or pixelated movement that presents as a stream; Gone is
abrupt diminishing at a macroscopic scale. He rejects the idea that Gone is an
esoteric category available only after decades of practice. Every in-breath
and out-breath ends, external sounds end, and mental-talk sentences have
periods. Those ordinary endings are valid practice objects.

The advanced claim is that repeated ordinary Gones gradually direct attention
toward the unborn Source of consciousness. Shinzen says Source is not itself
a sensory thing, but can be contacted through the senses, and its aftereffect
becomes more defined in the sensory circuits. The sensory results include
relief with unpleasant experience, fulfillment with pleasant and neutral
experience, Rest aftereffects such as quiet or peace, and an unexpected
richness in the void. He closes by linking ending, nirodha, nirvana,
cessation, and complete fulfillment.

## Source Frame
The source is trying to make Noting Gone precise, accessible, and deep at the
same time. The practice-level claims are strong evidence for Shinzen's
teaching: what counts as Gone, how to practice when Gone seems unavailable,
and why trivial endings matter. The Source, Unborn, bliss-void, and nirvana
interpretations should remain Shinzen's contemplative frame rather than
independent metaphysical or philological proof.

## Weakest Claims
- The claim that repeated trivial Gones gradually sensitize attention to the
  unborn Source is established as Shinzen's teaching, not as independent
  metaphysical evidence.
- "Complete enlightenment" through breath endings is a strong oral Zen-linked
  claim and should not be turned into a universal guarantee or simplified
  attainment recipe.
- The Tibetan bliss-void and Sanskrit nirvana links are comparative and
  interpretive inside Shinzen's teaching frame.

## Important Omissions
- No stop rules for practitioners who become bleak, dissociated,
  frightened, or compulsive around endings.
- No dosage or readiness criteria for intensive Gone practice.
- No detailed instruction for balancing Gone with Flow, Rest, Nurture
  Positive, ordinary functioning, or teacher support when Source language
  becomes destabilizing.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk makes Gone radically accessible, but its Source and complete
  enlightenment language can create attainment comparison or premature
  metaphysical certainty.
- It says breath and talk endings are sufficient for perfect practice, while
  adjacent sources warn that advanced Gone, emptiness, and no-self territory
  can be destabilizing and may require support.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Treating ordinary breath-end Gone practice as a promise of complete
  enlightenment.
- Hunting endings or suppressing experience instead of noticing real
  diminishings.
- Reading Source, Unborn, bliss-void, nirodha, or nirvana as established
  objective claims rather than Shinzen's contemplative interpretation.
- Assuming relief, fulfillment, or richness must appear on demand.
