## Teaching Register

## Why This Talk Matters
The wiki already tracks Shinzen's precision-first teaching style and his
accountability boundaries. This talk adds a smaller but durable handle:
precision includes listening to the effect of one's wording, not only
forming a careful idea internally.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen encourages practitioners and students to train themselves
  to "say what you mean," distinguishing it from merely meaning what one
  says.
- **S2**: Saying what one means requires checking whether the idea in the
  mind is accurately represented by the words that come out; he says this is
  difficult and needs training and care.
- **S3**: He frames himself as fussy about this discipline while also
  acknowledging that he repeatedly fails at it.
- **S4**: The correction case is a prior comment about conservative or
  Republican people. Shinzen says he meant that the path should be available
  and comfortable across the full spectrum of culture, philosophy, and
  politics, but the wording sounded dismissive.
- **S5**: Someone pointed out the mismatch, and Shinzen accepted the
  correction. The teaching move is that intended inclusivity does not erase
  the need to hear how the words actually landed.
- **S6**: Shinzen adds that people who would map roughly to conservative
  types in the local culture were, in Asian contexts, among his teachers and
  deeply helped him; this turns the political correction into a
  lineage-gratitude and cultural-translation boundary.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1-S2**: Precision is not only technical vocabulary. It is a
  transmission discipline in which the teacher compares intended meaning,
  actual wording, and audience reception.
- **S3-S5**: Shinzen uses his own failure as the example, so the source
  strengthens teacher accountability rather than teacher image management.
- **S4-S6**: Inclusivity claims are tested by how the excluded or caricatured
  group could reasonably hear the words, not only by the teacher's private
  intention.
- **S6**: The cross-cultural example matters because the same social type
  can be interpreted very differently across cultures; lineage gratitude
  should make the teacher more careful with local political shorthand.

## Practice Handles
- Before teaching, ask whether the words being used actually encode the
  intended instruction, analogy, correction, or invitation.
- After teaching, treat listener correction as possible data about the
  transmission, not merely as misunderstanding or resistance.
- When a comment sounds dismissive of a group despite inclusive intent,
  correct the wording directly and restate the actual invitation.
- For politically or culturally charged examples, avoid relying on the
  teacher's private goodwill. Check whether the surface wording could make
  the practice setting feel unavailable to the named group.
- When compiling or paraphrasing Shinzen, distinguish his intended teaching
  move from caption-fragile or orally imprecise wording, and spot-check
  exact phrases before using them as evidence.

## Summary
Shinzen opens by encouraging people to train themselves to say what they
mean. He traces the phrase to the Alice tea-party exchange, with a digression
through Lewis Carroll, Charles Dodgson, and Victorian mathematics. The
literary point is that meaning what one says and saying what one means are
not the same: a person can have an evidence-based, logical idea internally
and still speak words that do not accurately express it.

He then applies the point to himself. Despite working at precision, he says
he still sometimes says something that does not match what he meant. The
concrete example is a prior comment about conservative or Republican people.
He meant to communicate that Shinzen's path is available across the full
range of culture, philosophy, and politics, but he says the comment came out
as though he were making fun of those people.

The correction becomes the teaching. Someone pointed out the mismatch, and
Shinzen accepted it. He closes by adding that, in Asian contexts, the kinds
of people Americans might call conservative were among his teachers and
deeply helped him. The talk therefore preserves a small but important
transmission rule: speech precision, cultural humility, and willingness to
be corrected are part of how an inclusive practice setting is maintained.

## Source Frame
This is primary evidence for Shinzen's correction posture and teaching
precision. It is not a source for partisan analysis, Lewis Carroll
scholarship, Victorian mathematics history, or a complete ethics procedure.
The talk's value is the live teaching move: the teacher's intended meaning
must be checked against the words' actual effect, especially when the subject
is culturally or politically charged.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen's precision-first teaching method coexists with spontaneous oral
  imprecision; the source makes correction part of precision rather than a
  contradiction to hide.
- A speaker can mean inclusivity and still speak in a way that sounds
  dismissive. The tension is resolved only by listening to how the words
  landed and correcting them.
- The talk uses political labels to repair an overgeneralizing political
  comment; the labels remain rough and culturally situated.
- The source strengthens feedback humility but does not specify the
  structures needed when the issue is not merely wording.

## Easily Misused Claims
- Do not use "say what you mean" to demand sterile, over-polished teaching
  that prevents live correction or honest oral exploration.
- Do not use Shinzen's inclusive intent to dismiss listeners who heard the
  wording as exclusionary or mocking.
- Do not turn the political example into a claim that any group is spiritually
  superior, inferior, more Asian, or more authentic.
- Do not quote the captioned literary or mathematics claims as scholarship
  without checking the audio and independent sources.
- Do not treat one public correction as sufficient evidence of complete
  teacher accountability.
