Shinzen responds to students asking "what would Shinzen say" and uses that moment to deflate his own authority, the authority of long-term meditators, and the authority of scripture when it cannot be questioned in dialogue.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Shinzen says hearing people ask "what would Shinzen say" gives him pleasant self-referential body sensations, but his immediate Talk reaction is that these are only his casual opinions.
- **S2**: He says he knows little about enlightenment and broadens the warning: any teacher knows little about enlightenment.
- **S3**: He describes three bases for knowing: direct experience, logical inference, and reliable authority, then says the Buddha's distinctive move was to reject reliable authority as a basis of knowing.
- **S4**: A good person, ancient scripture, or teacher with fifty or sixty years of meditation experience may be useful, but their statement is not necessarily true.
- **S5**: Teachers should still be listened to because they may have experience and can point, but their statements should not be inflated beyond the best opinion they can formulate.
- **S6**: Buddhist canon or scripture can be used and can teach a lot, but its communication is limited because practitioners cannot dialogue with the Buddha or his immediate disciples.
- **S7**: Shinzen says his own retreat teaching usually requires interaction: he asks what a student means, distinguishes possible interpretations, and often cannot answer well without back-and-forth.
- **S8**: Even if the canon had been memorized word for word before being written down, that would still not supply the missing dialogue needed to sharply map a modern teaching to the path of classical liberation.

## Practice Handles
- Treat teacher statements, including Shinzen's, as hypotheses or fingers pointing toward practice, not as pronouncements.
- When a teacher, scripture, or lineage claim is invoked, ask what direct experience or inference could test it.
- Prefer live clarification when possible: ask what the student means, which interpretation is active, and what practice result would confirm the answer.
- Use the Buddhist canon with modest expectations when the question requires fine-grained modern technique mapping or classical-liberation interpretation.

## Summary
Shinzen begins by noticing the pleasant self-referential effect of students asking what he would say, then immediately undercuts it. His statements are casual opinions, and even experienced teachers know little about enlightenment compared with what there is to know.

He frames the authority issue through three bases of knowledge: direct experience, logical inference, and reliable authority. In his telling, the Buddha rejected reliable authority as decisive. A good person, scripture, or long-time meditator can be worth listening to, but their statement remains a pointer and should not be given more weight than it can bear.

He then applies the same boundary to Buddhist scripture or canon. Texts can be valuable, but the Buddha and early disciples are not available for dialogue. Shinzen contrasts that with his own teaching style, where he usually needs clarifying back-and-forth before answering. Even perfect memorization would not remove the communication limit created by a one-way text.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- Shinzen rejects authority as decisive while still saying teachers and texts can be useful and should be listened to.
- The talk values live dialogue, but many students will only encounter Shinzen through recordings, books, and transcripts, which share the one-way limitation he attributes to canon.
- The canon can point toward classical liberation, yet Shinzen says precise mapping is limited without dialogue with the original teachers.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "Just casual opinion" can be misused to dismiss useful expertise, avoid disciplined practice, or collapse all teachers into equal unreliability.
- "Direct experience" can be misused as private certainty if not checked by logic, feedback, behavior, and competent guidance.
- The critique of scripture can become shallow anti-traditionalism if separated from Shinzen's statement that the canon can teach a lot.
- The Buddha-authority claim should not be used as a scholarly trump card without stronger textual evidence.
