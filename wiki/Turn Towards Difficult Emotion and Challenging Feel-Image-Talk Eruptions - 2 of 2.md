## Teaching Register
The register is direct instruction with an explanatory aside. Shinzen answers a practitioner report about not having a single emotional flavor by showing how to become a connoisseur of affective body sensation without turning the practice into psychological story analysis.

## Why This Talk Matters
Part 1 says to untangle Feel, Image, and Talk. Part 2 says what to do when Feel itself is still too fused: subdivide it by flavor, then by location and spatial sector, work each time-space-volume until awareness can soak into it, and then return to the broad Feel/Image/Talk whole.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: A named emotion such as anger may contain subtle secondary flavors such as fear or sadness; if unnoticed, those mild flavors can make the whole state feel multiplicative rather than additive.
- **S2**: If ordinary Feel/Image/Talk untangling does not work, Shinzen's rule is to subdivide until the field becomes conquerable: divide Feel into flavors, then divide each flavor into locations and radial sectors.
- **S3**: Radial sweeping works one emotional flavor through spatial sectors, such as right, left, front, behind, above, or below, until the practitioner has a manageable time-space-volume of one flavor of sensation.
- **S4**: "Penetrate" means awareness soaks into the sensation and being opens to it; the practitioner then integrates by returning from worked parts to the broader Feel/Image/Talk constellation.
- **S5**: The practitioner must release the agenda to get answers or get comfortable; the alternative reward is the taste of purification, even when answers and comfort have not arrived.
- **S6**: Complete feeling is hard but learnable; Shinzen frames emotional Feel as an evolutionary behavior-control system whose sensations should be loved to completeness rather than denied, suppressed, or eliminated.
- **S7**: When Feel is complete, it motivates and directs behavior without driving and distorting it.

## Practice Handles
A practitioner or guide can use the talk as a second-stage emotional turn-toward sequence:

- Start with Feel/Image/Talk when the emotional whole is workable.
- If Feel remains fused, name the major and minor emotional flavors rather than accepting a single label such as anger.
- Treat mild secondary flavors as significant because they may multiply suffering when unnoticed.
- Work one flavor at a time until clarity and equanimity increase.
- If one flavor is still too large, locate it and sweep radial sectors from a core sensation outward.
- Work a manageable time-space-volume of one flavor, then return to the whole constellation.
- Drop the demand for insight answers or immediate comfort; look for purification taste as the practice reward.
- Evaluate completion partly by behavior: does the Feel motivate and direct, or does it still drive and distort?

## Summary
Shinzen begins from the observation that a challenging emotion may not be one flavor. Anger can contain sadness or fear, and those subtle flavors matter. His arithmetic remains the same as part 1: if secondary flavors are unnoticed, the state may feel as if its factors multiply rather than add.

He then gives the finer technique. If Feel/Image/Talk is not enough, divide Feel into flavors. If that is not enough, divide flavors into locations and radial sectors. With sadness, for example, a practitioner might find a heart ache, sweep awareness to the right edge of the sensation, then left, front, behind, above, and below. The goal is a manageable volume of one flavor of sensation that can be penetrated, then reintegrated into the whole.

The final frame is motivational and behavioral. The normal reward agenda is to get answers or comfort; Shinzen says those agendas need to be released so the taste of purification can become sufficient. He calls complete feeling a major human attainment. Emotional Feel is not to be denied or removed; it is loved to completeness so it can motivate and direct without driving and distorting.

## Source Frame
The talk is trying to make strong emotional Feel workable when the whole state or even the broad Feel category is too fused. Its strongest observed instruction is progressive subdivision. Its strongest interpretation is evolutionary: emotional Feel was shaped to produce immediate, approximate, intense animal responses, which can distort modern human behavior unless clarified and completed. That is useful as Shinzen's practice explanation, not as a full biological, trauma, or social theory.

## Weakest Claims
- The evolutionary-robot framing is Shinzen's motivational explanation, not established evidence for how all emotional Feel should be treated in every nervous system or social context.
- "Anyone can do this if they want to and take measures" is strong as encouragement but under-specified around trauma, illness, neurodivergence, medication, acute risk, teacher competence, and support.
- The taste of purification is presented as a sufficient reward, but the transcript does not give operational criteria for distinguishing purification from resignation, numb endurance, or escalating distress.

## Important Omissions
- No criteria for trauma activation, panic, dissociation, self-harm risk, rage behavior, grief crisis, humiliation injury, abuse history, or when to stop and turn away.
- No relational or ethical guidance for emotions that are tied to conflict, harm, repair, boundaries, or accountability.
- No dosage, pacing, retreat-context, teacher-qualification, consent, or aftercare guidance for radial sweeping through intense emotional Feel.
- No exact criteria for complete feeling beyond the behavioral distinction between motivating/directing and driving/distorting.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk says practitioners must release agendas for answers and comfort, but many difficult emotions also require ordinary understanding, repair, protection, grief support, or action.
- "Subdivide until you conquer" can preserve workable contact, but it can also become over-effort or avoidance of the whole relational situation if used without guidance.
- The claim that every person can do this is inspiring within practice instruction but unsafe if detached from readiness, support, and scope-of-practice limits.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "You just have to want it" could become shame or coercion when a practitioner needs support, rest, medical care, therapy, or protection.
- "Love it to death to completeness" could be misread as overriding boundaries, anger signals, or necessary action.
- "Let go of the agenda to get comfortable" could become permission to endure unworkable distress rather than a refinement of practice reward.
- Evolutionary-robot language could make emotional Feel seem like an enemy to defeat rather than a sensation to clarify and complete.
