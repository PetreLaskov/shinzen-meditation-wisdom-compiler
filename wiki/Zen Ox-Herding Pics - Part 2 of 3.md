## Teaching Register
This is advanced explanatory Dharma teaching keyed to the classical pictures. Shinzen translates Zen imagery into his own impermanence, surrender, Source, repose, and ordinary-appearance vocabulary rather than giving a sequential practice prescription.

## Why This Talk Matters
The talk gives the middle of the ox-herding map a vivid practice handle. Taming is not yet riding; riding means being carried by impermanence while giving up the need to know where experience is going.

## Key Claims
- **S1**: Taming the ox means internalizing enlightenment so it no longer slips away or runs wild; the ox follows the practitioner, but this is still not the same as riding it.
- **S2**: Riding the ox backward symbolizes surrender to impermanence: the practitioner gives up investment in where the great beast is going and falls into what needs to be done moment by moment.
- **S3**: Shinzen distinguishes faith from belief: belief holds on to something, while true faith lets go of the need to be oriented or to have answers.
- **S4**: Homecoming means final repose of the spirit: the ox is gone, no fixed position remains, and only the authentic human remains without needing mind as an object.
- **S5**: Shinzen reports an oral tradition in which the last three pictures show the substance, form, and use of enlightenment; in this part, substance is no substance and form is the ordinary world as enlightenment's appearance.

## Load-Bearing Teaching Moves
- **S1**: Taming separates stabilization from first capture and prevents a first no-self/kensho handle from being treated as complete realization.
- **S2**: Riding backward gives a precise surrender handle for Flow, Gone, and Source-facing impermanence practice.
- **S3**: The faith/belief contrast protects surrender from becoming a new doctrine; the practice move is relinquishing orientation, not adopting an idea.
- **S4**: Homecoming links no-self to ordinary humanity rather than self-erasure: the ox disappears, but the person remains as authentic human expression.
- **S5**: The substance/form frame bounds emptiness: no substance is immediately paired with ordinary appearances of the world.

## Practice Handles
A practitioner or guide can use this part to distinguish stabilization from surrender. If the person can keep the ox from running wild, the next issue may be whether they still need to steer, orient, or know what comes next.

The caution is that "riding backward" is not passivity or disregard for safety. In Shinzen's map, it presupposes enough stabilization that experience can carry the next action without the practitioner securing a fixed position.

## Summary
Shinzen begins with taming the ox: enlightenment has been internalized enough that it does not slip away and the ox no longer runs wild. He immediately says this cannot compare with riding the ox. Riding is frightening because the person gives up investment in where the ox is going.

The image of riding backward becomes the talk's central practice metaphor. Shinzen says enlightenment has no informed consent: it is better than expected and not what people imagine. Riding the ox backward means being carried by a powerful beast, surrendering to the Flow of impermanence, falling up into existence moment by moment, and letting action arise without needing orientation.

After the ox carries the person home, the ox is gone and the person rests without a fixed position or need to objectify mind. Shinzen then reports an oral tradition in which the final three pictures present the substance, appearance, and use of enlightenment. This part covers the first two: the substance is no substance, and the form is the ordinary world, where mountains, cherry blossoms, and every thing appear as enlightenment.

## Source Frame
The source is a short oral teaching with visual references and classical terminology. Its strongest evidence is Shinzen's practice-stage translation: stabilization, surrender to impermanence, repose, no substance, and ordinary appearance.

The talk wants practitioners to re-understand enlightenment as something that cannot be controlled from a fixed position. It does not provide safety criteria for surrender, verification criteria for homecoming, or the final service/use picture, which is deferred to the next part.

## Important Omissions
- The talk does not finish the final "use" picture of enlightenment; the service/marketplace frame remains dependent on the fuller talk and the third part.
- It gives no readiness criteria for riding the ox backward or for distinguishing surrender from passivity, dissociation, collapse, or fatalism.
- It does not explain how ordinary action, ethical feedback, teaching competence, or clinical support should govern late-stage realization language.

## Contradictions/Tensions
- The talk says enlightenment has "no informed consent" while the broader wiki's safety posture requires consent, support, and non-coercion. The phrase should be read as epistemic humility about imagining enlightenment beforehand, not as permission to override safety.
- Homecoming is called complete enlightenment, but the map still has three more pictures. This is a productive tension: repose is complete in one sense, while substance, appearance, and use still need articulation.
- Surrender to impermanence can sound like loss of agency, while the talk also says one falls into what needs to be done. Future reasoning should preserve responsive action as part of the surrender claim.

## Easily Misused Claims
- "No informed consent to enlightenment" could justify pressure or destabilizing practice if divorced from ordinary consent and safety.
- Riding backward could be misread as fatalism, passivity, or dissociation rather than stabilized responsiveness to impermanence.
- No-substance language could become nihilism unless paired with ordinary appearance and, in the full map, service.
