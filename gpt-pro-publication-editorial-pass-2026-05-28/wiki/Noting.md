Noting is the manual's main technique for turning sensory experience into trainable practice objects. It is not identical with verbal labeling; labeling is a support that can be used, shortened, changed, or dropped when it no longer helps.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: An act of Noting acknowledges a sensory event and then knows it through focused contact; labels and other options route attention but are not the essence.
- **Why this matters**: The same technique can drill into one cell, float through a column, float through a modality row, note all experience, or specialize in Gone.
- **Key tensions**: The full grid greatly increases precision and choice, while flexible labels and the Four Okays reduce load; labels can also agitate, reify, expose uncomfortable scatter, or become performative, and the sources still give limited dosage, overload, and clinical safety criteria.
- **Source posture**: Strong as Shinzen's manual instruction; incomplete for later life-practice and guidance chapters.

## Model
The Basic Mindfulness introduction defines a typical act of Noting as two moves: clearly acknowledge the presence of a sensory event, then focus intently on that event ([[Basic Mindfulness Introduction]] S9). Labeling is optional. A label is a word or phrase that describes the event being noted, but the relationship is instrumental: labeling facilitates noting, and noting facilitates mindfulness.

The SHF introduction adds an equally important setup variable: focus range, the set of experiences the practitioner has decided to focus on for a period of time ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S1). Noting does not happen in empty space; its labels and contact stance are interpreted inside the active range.

[[What Is Mindfulness]] gives the more detailed representative-practice version. A period of Noting is a sequence of acts; each act usually has an initial noticing followed by intentional knowing, soaking into, opening to, or penetrating the focus object for a fraction of a second to several seconds ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S12). This makes Noting a CCE training method, not a verbal classification task.

Unit 07 restates the same hierarchy in the later SHF idiom. A typical act of Noting has an acknowledging phase and a focusing phase; the label can occur in either phase or both, and in practice the phases may merge into one act ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S1, S4).

This hierarchy matters:

- [[Mindfulness Skill Triad]] is the target skillset.
- Noting is a technique for training that skillset.
- Labeling is an optional aid for doing Noting.

Chapter 7 clarifies why the labels are short and rhythmic. The [[Sensory Grid]] has descriptive language for discussing experience and labeling language for practice ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 7 - A Science of Sensory Experience]] S8).

## Scope Options
The first four Ways show Noting by cell and column. A practitioner can note inner image/talk/body emotion, physical sight/sound/body sensation, rest, Flow, or Gone through the specific labels introduced in the first four manual chapters.

Chapter 8 expands the same logic:

- **Drill down**: work with one sensory atom such as See In or Feel Flow.
- **Back up by column**: Focus In, Focus Out, Focus on Rest, or Focus on Flow.
- **Back up by row**: Focus on See, Focus on Hear, or Focus on Feel.
- **Back up globally**: Focus on Everything, using the 12 grid categories plus Gone.
- **Specialize in passing**: Just Note [[Gone]] whenever vanishing or drop-off is detected ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S1-S5).

The SHF introduction makes four of these row/global options the standard beginner entry: Focus on See, Focus on Hear, Focus on Feel, and Focus on Everything. They can be practiced singly, selected as needed, or sequenced as Everything, See, Hear, Feel, Everything ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S2-S3). Range width also regulates load: narrow from Everything to one modality when too much is happening, or broaden to Everything when holding one modality becomes taxing ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S4).

[[Shinzen Guides Steph in Focus Out]] gives a live example of range-specific
labels. Inside Focus Out, Touch means non-emotional physical body sensation,
Sight means eyes-open seeing, and Sound means external sound. If multiple
outer events are available, the beginner does not need an inclusive label or
whole-field hold; Shinzen has the student choose one, label it, and focus for
a few seconds before noting again ([[Shinzen Guides Steph in Focus Out]]
S1-S3).

Chapter 9 consolidates those options into the full grid and confirms that Noting sits alongside [[Do Nothing]] and [[Nurture Positive]] as one of three techniques ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 9 - The Full Grid]] S1-S5).

The Focus Methods talk adds a coverage-strategy overlay to these scope options. Focus In, Focus Out, and Focus on Rest are generally free-floating among their categories, but zooming out can create brief even-coverage moments; body scanning is a systematic inventory; and broad Flow practice can become even coverage across all sensory spaces when visual, auditory, and somatic flow are held together ([[Focus Methods in Mindfulness Advantages and Disadvantages]] S3, S8-S9).

## Labeling Modes
The definitional article adds a useful support ladder. Noting can happen with no labels, mental labels, subvocal labels, ordinary spoken labels, or strongly spoken labels. Shinzen recommends shifting to a stronger mode when the practitioner is spaced out or caught up, then dropping to a weaker mode when focus stabilizes ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S12).

The Gate 1 Part 1 labeling talk gives the oral default behind that ladder. Shinzen says the fallback ideal is no verbal label, just direct clarity and momentum around the sensory category, but he immediately says that this is often difficult and that practitioners should use labels when no-label tracking turns into vague drifting ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S3). This keeps no-label practice from becoming a purity standard.

[[Forcing Spoken Labels]] gives the strong-label end its own practice rationale. Shinzen says forced spoken labels can make an invisible force of attentional scatter appear as tangible impatience or agitation in Feel. The discomfort is then noted as body sensation with location, intensity, and flavor, because a hidden force that simply carries attention away has become trackable and therefore potentially tractable ([[Forcing Spoken Labels]] S1-S4).

The same talk makes label strength a gear rather than a status marker. No-label practice may remain the general direction when direct clarity is stable, but steep conditions created by outside challenge or inner activation may call for first gear: solid spoken labels or sotto voce labeling. Needing first gear does not make the practice inferior; it means the apparatus is being used for difficult terrain ([[Forcing Spoken Labels]] S6-S8).

[[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] places this apparatus inside a wider effort polarity. Noting is Shinzen's example of bearing down because it separates phenomena and soaks attention into what arises. That does not make Noting spiritually inferior or superior; it means the technique uses effort in a way that should eventually support the ability to drop effort into Do Nothing ([[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] S2-S7).

The SHF introduction gives the same ladder a more concrete mechanics layer. A label is a word that names the present sensory event; it may be mental or spoken, and the label stream should be paced comfortably rather than too racy or too spacey ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S5, S7). Pace primarily supports concentration, wording supports sensory clarity, and a low, gentle, matter-of-fact voice supports equanimity ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S6).

Ordinary labels should take only a small portion of attention, with most attention going into knowing the object. Strong spoken labels are an exception used when gross wandering or unconsciousness needs stronger feedback ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S12). Unit 02 says the strong version divides attention between the labeled event and the label stream, so losing the stream exposes lost mindfulness and restoring it reestablishes a basic CCE level ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S9).

This makes labeling a dosage variable. Strong labels are not more advanced; they are a support when the practice needs more feedback. No labels are not more pure if the practitioner is simply drifting. Labels are also scaffolding: although they are auditory events, Unit 02 explicitly says not to label the labels themselves ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S10).

Unit 05 adds the minimum-correct-practice rule. SHF is being done properly when labels move at a comfortable pace, use words that correspond to what is being experienced, and are held in an equanimous voice rather than repeated as a mechanical mantra ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S9). Labels are not inferior "training wheels"; labeled and unlabeled noting are different gears chosen for the terrain, especially when a practitioner forgets, drifts, or meets a large challenge ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S10).

Unit 07 names five labeling modes as a spectrum: no labels, mental labels, subvocal labels, ordinary spoken labels, and strongly spoken labels. The practical rule is to shift stronger as soon as attention is spaced out or caught up, then shift weaker again if focus stabilizes and the practitioner wants less explicit support ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S2-S3). The Part 1 talk gives the same spoken-label ladder as weak or whispered, medium or ordinary, and strong, where the practitioner makes the labels loud, clear, and crisp and listens to ensure the label stream does not stop ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S7).

The live Focus Out clip shows the same gear shift in a beginner setting:
Shinzen first listens to spoken labels for pace, wording, and object choice,
then asks for mental Touch/Sight/Sound labels once the range is stable. The
shift is not a status upgrade; it is a support adjustment when the student
can keep contact without the audible label stream ([[Shinzen Guides Steph in
Focus Out]] S2, S6).

The same talk gives the strongest oral form of the equanimity-voice cue. Shinzen tells practitioners to create a gentle, matter-of-fact, almost impersonal tone in mental or spoken labels and calls this "Use the voice"; he explicitly says outer crying, screaming, or flailing need not stop if the label voice stays equanimous ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S8-S9).

The Gate 2 Flow-labeling talk shows the ladder functioning as live quality
control. Shinzen first has the student say "Flow" out loud every four to six
seconds so he can hear pace and tone, then moves to mental labels once the
gentle matter-of-fact voice is internalized, and finally permits dropping
labels only when direct awareness has enough momentum. If the student spaces
out, mental labels should return; if spacing out is strong, spoken labels
return as first gear ([[Turn Towards Flow (Change) Using the Labeling Gears
& Options]] S2-S9).

Unit 02 also sharpens the acknowledge-focus rhythm. Noting typically acknowledges a sensory event and briefly focuses on it, but events that vanish too quickly are not failures; they become opportunities to detect [[Gone]]. If observation changes the event, the alteration is observed like any other sensory phenomenon ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S11-S13).

[[Shinzen Young Retreat at the Monastic Academy 05.27.2017]] gives a
retreat-stream version of this rhythm. In Just See, Just Hear, Just Feel, and
Note Everything, each contacted event either disappears, creating a passing
to detect, or stays available for soaking and getting to know it
([[Shinzen Young Retreat at the Monastic Academy 05.27.2017]] S3-S5). The
later yes/no/both/rest exercise is therefore an extension of Noting's
acknowledge-focus and Gone logic, not a separate replacement technique.

Unit 03 adds two semantic rules. First, See, Hear, and Feel are simple modality labels. When two or three modalities arise together, exclusive emphasis chooses one target, while inclusive emphasis notes the co-occurrence with See Hear, See Feel, Hear Feel, or All. In Unified Mindfulness, emphasis is a technical term for this exclusive-versus-inclusive choice, not a generic name for range, label, or re-noting options ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S1-S5).

Second, the meaning of a label depends on the focus range. See always means something visual, but it may mean visual rest during Focus on Rest, mental image during Focus In, physical sight during Focus Out, visual flow during Focus on Flow, or any visual event during row practice. Hear and Feel work the same way; a breath focus or body scan can use Feel because the active range is already somatic ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S7-S8).

This makes labels compact rather than vague. The missing specifier is supplied by the selected range. If the range is not explicit, the same compactness can become ambiguous.

[[Watching Subconscious Processing]] gives a subtle Focus In example of that
range dependence. If the range is inner experience and the field is not All
Rest, a practitioner may note See In, Hear In, Feel In, a combination, or All
In based only on the tug toward image space, talk space, or
emotional-feeling space. The label acknowledges where the pull is occurring;
it does not claim that hidden content has been identified ([[Watching
Subconscious Processing]] S1-S7).

## Minimum And Optional
Unit 05 names the Four Okays: it is okay to miss, guess, be a little late, and average rapid streams. The requirement is a quality moment with the chosen target, not exhaustive detection, perfect timing, or separate labels for every fleeting event ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S1-S5).

The Part 1 talk adds pacing diagnostics to the same anti-perfectionism. If labels are too fast, practice can become frenetic; if too slow, it can become spacey; rapid-fire sensory streams can be averaged into one label every few seconds; and breath entrainment should be allowed only when it arises naturally and works ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S10-S11).

Those rules turn anti-perfectionism into technique design. If the same event is noted repeatedly, that can still be valid because Noting trains quality moments with something sensory, not novelty seeking ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S6). If doubts arise about imagination, spontaneity, doing it right, suppression, or who observes, the SHF move is to [[Recycle The Reaction]] as inner See/Hear/Feel activation rather than solve it abstractly during the practice period ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S8).

What is optional can still deepen the practice. A practitioner may restrict the focus range, use a more active or passive focus stance, track detail, look for differentiation, rest in integration, or notice Gone, but none of that is required for valid basic SHF ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Four Okays and Required vs Allowed]] S11). Focus stance names the attitude taken during the focus phase: from letting the event focus the practitioner, through easy intentional turning, to actively thrusting awareness into the event.

## Guidance Options
Chapter 12 adds guidance-level Noting options under the mnemonic L-Z-R-R-E-I-S: Labeling, Zooming, Range, Re-noting, Exclusive versus Inclusive emphasis, and Subdividing categories ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S13).

The Gate 1 Part 1 talk makes these options a first-line troubleshooting layer. Shinzen says that when someone reports practice difficulty, he first asks what technique they are using and then which options they have used; only after ordinary options fail does he move into special exercises or more creative guidance ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S4-S5).

Unit 07 fills in several of those definitions. Re-noting intentionally notes the same event a set number of times, such as double, triple, or quad noting, unless the event vanishes first. "Note 'til gone" stays with the same event until all or part of it vanishes ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S6).

The Part 1 talk gives a slightly different practice handle: repeating the same note can slow the internal clock and make detection easier, while "Repeat until Gone" locks on to one event until some part abruptly subsides and can be noted Gone ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S12).

[[Appreciate the Senses Transcend the Self Express the Source]] places those
options inside broad Note Everything. When the whole range feels too busy,
Shinzen recommends narrowing to one modality, re-noting the same event, or
using inclusive noting to acknowledge co-occurring modalities, including All
or None when those are the actual field ([[Appreciate the Senses Transcend
the Self Express the Source]] S4-S6). The point is not maximal label
complexity; it is restoring quality contact with the sensory situation.

[[Four Ways Forward - June Shinzen Retreat]] repeats the same option logic
with a different emphasis. The first Note Everything period starts
passively, with attention going where it is pulled; the later period adds
optional active contact, double/triple/quad noting, and explicit Gone for
all-or-part vanishings. The source keeps all of these as options rather than
requirements, useful especially when broad practice floods or needs to slow
down ([[Four Ways Forward - June Shinzen Retreat]] S2-S3, S11).

[[Zooming]] changes the spatial scope of focus. The practitioner may zoom in to a local area, zoom out to the whole area of the event, zoom beyond the event to find openness around it, or zoom both ways by attending to a local intensity while spreading attention as broadly as possible ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S7).

The Gate 1 Part 2 talk adds the oral mechanics and a crucial default. During the focus phase, if the acknowledged event persists rather than immediately vanishing, the default is not to control spatial attention. Zooming in chooses a small part of the event, often a weaker perimeter region around pain or intense emotion. Zooming out spreads over the whole event or as much as is detectable. Zooming in and out at the same time holds the most intense local point while spreading awareness through the whole body ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S1-S5).

That simultaneous local-global option carries Shinzen's stronger purification frame. He says much suffering is resistance to subtle body-wide spread, including spread below awareness, and that local-global zooming can "grease the rails" for spread, release, and the taste of purification. He also warns that it may initially feel overwhelming or like loss of control, and says never using it is fine ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S6-S10).

Stance has two layers. Contact stance is the active or passive role taken in contacting experience; focus stance is the active or passive role taken after contact during the focus phase ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S8). Unit 05's focus-stance material therefore names only one side of a wider stance distinction.

## FAQ Patterns
Unit 07 gives a compact coaching FAQ that preserves the Four Okays while adding context sensitivity. Beginner self-monitoring, guessed labels, late labels, and temporary reinforcement of a meditating self are normal training issues, not automatic evidence that the technique has failed ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S9).

Two FAQ answers are especially load-bearing. First, observation-induced change counts as what is actually present: if focusing modifies the event, the modified event is still valid sensory material ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S9). Second, repeated contact with the same event is not wasted because each note is another moment of focusing and opening, not a demand for novel content ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S10).

Mental chatter cannot be routed by symptom alone. If the practitioner is doing See Hear Feel Everything, talk is noted as Hear, distinct burst endings can be noted as Gone, and triggered emotional body sensations are noted as Feel. If the chosen range excludes talk, the instruction is to let talk come and go in the background with equanimity while returning clarity and concentration to the range; if that fails, shift the range to include the talk ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S13).

## Gone And Rest
Gone is now more than a casual add-on to labels. It is the noticed moment when all or part of an event drops away or drops off. Continued absence after that moment is Rest, not Gone ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S5).

This distinction matters in practice. A burst of mental talk may end: note Gone. If mental talk remains absent: note Hear Rest. A physical sensation may suddenly diminish: note Gone. If the body then feels relaxed or quiet: note Feel Rest.

## Boundaries
Noting is not analysis of content. In the inner Way, the practitioner notes mental images, phrases of self-talk, and emotional body locations rather than debating stories or interpreting biography ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 1 - The Way of Thoughts and Emotions]] S2-S3).

The inner-space tug case intensifies that boundary. Noting can acknowledge a
pre-content cue, but it should not become a hunt for subconscious meaning,
hidden memory, or diagnostic significance ([[Watching Subconscious
Processing]] S5-S7).

Noting is also not forced labeling. If labels become mechanical, compulsive, or distracting, the issue is at the support layer, not necessarily in the underlying act of mindful acknowledgement.

Unit 03 makes that boundary explicit. Labels can agitate, reify, create associations, trigger performance anxiety, require effort, create observer/observed distance, or seem to interfere with the observed event. Shinzen gives a quick fix and a deep fix: drop labels if direct awareness can maintain noting, or persist until labeling becomes more automatic and less disruptive. Dropping labels is not dropping noting; dropping intentional noting itself may indicate a transition to [[Do Nothing]] or [[Nurture Positive]], or it may indicate sloppiness ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Simple and Flexible Labels]] S9-S12).

Unit 04 adds another boundary around range choice. Shinzen says a practitioner may Turn Towards discomfort by choosing a range that includes it, or Turn Away by choosing another range; both train concentration, clarity, and equanimity if done as mindful awareness practice ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Starting Focus Ranges]] S5). That validates strategic narrowing or redirecting, but it does not by itself distinguish wise regulation from avoidance, flooding, medical neglect, or unsupported exposure.

Unit 05 adds a different boundary: practice can be correct without being maximally intense. The Four Okays protect against performance anxiety, but the "by definition" correctness criterion does not prove that a session is sufficient, balanced, safe, or behaviorally integrated. Mechanical labels, forced focus stance, relaxation-as-shutdown, and unsupported suppression worries still route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

Unit 07 adds strong-label and reassurance boundaries. Escalating to spoken or strongly spoken labels can restore feedback when attention collapses, but the unit does not say when stronger labels become strain, shame, exposure, compulsion, or clinically unwise. Its claim that lifelong modest practice has a high probability of making incessant chatter fade should remain reassurance inside Shinzen's teaching frame, not a universal prognosis ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Noting Nutshell and FAQ]] S3, S13).

The Part 1 talk adds an adjacent boundary: "Use the voice" can support equanimity during intense expression, but a calm label tone is not proof that the practitioner is safe, regulated, or free from suppression or dissociation. Strong labels, repeat-until-Gone, and pacing changes remain technique options, not complete support criteria ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S7-S12).

[[Forcing Spoken Labels]] sharpens the strong-label boundary. Discomfort from forced labels may reveal the scatter that was already controlling attention, but discomfort is not proof that the practitioner should continue intensifying. Hard work, agitation, shame, retreat pressure, and the feeling of being sent back to "basic" practice all need guidance and support criteria, not just exhortation ([[Forcing Spoken Labels]] S5-S8).

[[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] adds an effort boundary around Noting as a whole. If Noting becomes racy, goal-driven, or unable to relax into non-effort, the practice may need Do Nothing, rest, guidance, or a simpler focus rather than more technique pressure. The test of productive effort is not endless bearing down; it is increased freedom to release effort when release is appropriate ([[Bear Down or Ease Up in Meditation]] S5-S7).

The Part 2 talk adds the zooming boundary. Local-global zooming is explicitly optional and non-default; its overwhelm and loss-of-control language should not be treated as proof that the practitioner should persist, nor as proof that purification is occurring. Intense body sensation, shaking, trauma activation, medical pain, panic, dissociation-like drift, and perceived loss of control still route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]] ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, Part 2 of 2, Zooming]] S8-S10).

The full grid adds choice and therefore selection risk. Unit 02 gives better cues for pacing, spoken labels, strongly spoken labels, dropping labels, and not labeling labels, and Unit 04 gives better cues for broadening or narrowing focus range, but the sources still do not give complete clinical safety rules for intense distress, Gone-related disorientation, energy activation, medical pain, fatigue, urges, or positivity bypass. The Flow-labeling talk adds that a guide may evaluate a student's spoken-label pace and tone, which is useful feedback but not a complete consent, privacy, or support protocol. Those limits route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

[[What Is Mindfulness]] clarifies several false boundaries: Noting is not mere noticing, Noting and mental labeling are not the same thing, and repeated notes of the same ordinary object can still train deep processing when the practitioner repeatedly infuses clarity and equanimity into the object ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S12, S23).

## Implications
Noting operationalizes [[Sensory Clarity]] because it asks the practitioner to know what kind of sensory event is present. It operationalizes [[Concentration Power]] because the event receives focused attention. It supports [[Equanimity]] because the event is acknowledged as an arising, changing, resting, or vanishing sensory process rather than suppressed or identified with.

For advanced practice reasoning, the key question is not "Should labeling happen?" but "What degree of explicit support best preserves acknowledgement, focus, clarity, and noninterference right now?" Sometimes the answer is a precise label, sometimes broad Focus notation, sometimes Just Note Gone, sometimes [[Do Nothing]], and sometimes constructive [[Nurture Positive]] rather than Noting.
