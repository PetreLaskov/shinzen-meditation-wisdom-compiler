The mindfulness skill triad is Shinzen's three-part frame of concentration power, sensory clarity, and equanimity. [[What Is Mindfulness]] makes the triad the candidate definition of mindful awareness, while the Basic Mindfulness manual and book chapters turn it into practice routing and transformation theory.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Mindful awareness is not focus alone or just awareness; it combines relevance-based attention, sensory discrimination, and noninterference with sensory events over time.
- **Why this matters**: Basic Mindfulness routes every practice option through the triad, and complete experience, pain without suffering, insight, and purification require the triad rather than calm concentration by itself.
- **Key tensions**: Shinzen proposes CCE as an intuitive, explanatory, historically useful, and eventually measurable definition, but current quantification, diagnosis of imbalance, and support criteria remain incomplete.
- **Source posture**: Primary evidence for Shinzen's teaching architecture; outcome and safety claims remain source-attributed.

## Model
[[Mindfulness Definitions]] should be loaded first when the word mindfulness itself is ambiguous. Shinzen's definitional article says mindfulness can mean a word, an awareness, practices, a path, a translation, a fad, a shadow, or a possible revolution; CCE defines the narrower facet called mindful awareness ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S1-S3).

The article analyzes the common formula "present-centered, non-judgmental attention." Present-centeredness can be selective physical anchoring, tight breath focus, or all-inclusive awareness; non-judgment becomes workable only when clarified as equanimity rather than elimination of useful judgment. Shinzen's conclusion is that concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity are necessary and sufficient for that refined definition ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S13, S15, S17).

The See Hear Feel introduction restates the same broad Unified Mindfulness frame: mindful awareness is CCE working together, and any practice that raises baseline CCE over time can count as mindful awareness practice, including Nurture Positive and Do Nothing ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Source Frame and Happiness Rationale]] S2). This matters because SHF is introduced as one technique inside the larger skill engine, not as the whole system.

[[Vipassana and Mindfulness]] gives the older-term bridge into the same triad.
Shinzen interprets vipassana as seeing sensory strands separately, seeing
through them by soaking awareness into them, and seeing into true nature or
insight. He then says his mindfulness definition includes that separating and
soaking as sensory clarity, requires concentration to sustain it, and adds
equanimity because practical vipassana teaching emphasizes gentle
matter-of-fact noninterference ([[Vipassana and Mindfulness]] S1-S8).

[[Shinzen Young's Welcome to New Viewers]] gives the public-channel version of
the same definition. Shinzen tells newcomers that mindfulness is a spiritual
path modeled as skill cultivation: concentration power, sensory clarity, and
equanimity developed through exercises, time, practice, and competent
instruction ([[Shinzen Young's Welcome to New Viewers]] S1). The same talk
uses "ordinary experience becomes utterly extraordinary" as the entry-level
promise of CCE rather than adding a fourth skill or a new technique
([[Shinzen Young's Welcome to New Viewers]] S3).

[[Reality & Sensory Experience]] gives the sharper evidence-tier version of
that promise. Shinzen says he has strong confidence that enough
concentration, clarity, and equanimity can make any ordinary sensory
experience extraordinary or spiritual, while refusing to claim comparable
confidence about the objective reality behind sensory experience
([[Reality & Sensory Experience]] S1-S4).

The beginner FAQ [[Beginner FAQs - Why Are We Doing This Why Meditate]] gives the public-entry consequence of that skill engine: mindfulness practice raises baseline concentration, clarity, and equanimity, and raising baseline CCE raises baseline human happiness in broad and deep senses ([[Beginner FAQs - Why Are We Doing This Why Meditate]] S3). The clip does not add a new skill, but it makes CCE the causal bridge between beginner motivation and [[Total Happiness]].

[[Five Basic Assumptions in Mindfulness Practice]] gives the public-entry axiom frame. Shinzen says concentration, clarity, and equanimity are assumptions behind mindfulness as he teaches it: useful capacities to implement, not logical proofs or demands to maintain a special state at all times ([[Five Basic Assumptions in Mindfulness Practice]] S1-S4). The same talk adds that reactions to CCE effects should be recycled into practice, which makes [[Recycle The Reaction]] the safety-facing extension of the triad ([[Five Basic Assumptions in Mindfulness Practice]] S5).

The next See Hear Feel unit turns that support role into mechanics. Concentration is relevance-based focus, sensory clarity is momentary tracking, and equanimity is allowing experience to come and go without push and pull ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S2-S3). Labels are optional within noting, but when used their pace primarily supports concentration, wording supports sensory clarity, and voice tone supports equanimity ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S5-S7). That makes labeling an adjustable support for the triad rather than a separate fourth skill.

The Gate 1 Part 1 labeling talk adds an important calibration: practitioners can influence CCE through Noting options, but they do not have huge control because concentration, clarity, and equanimity are the capacities being trained. The practical options are labeling, spatial direction of attention during the focus phase, and pacing ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S4). This protects the triad from becoming an expectation that a beginner should already be able to summon high CCE on demand.

[[Forcing Spoken Labels]] adds a sharper feedback example. Forced labels may reveal that attention was being scattered by a previously invisible force by turning that force into observable impatience or agitation in Feel. In CCE terms, the label stream supports concentration, makes the disruption clearer as a sensory object, and exposes the equanimity problem around discomfort and resistance ([[Forcing Spoken Labels]] S1-S4).

Chapter 2 says meditation systems share concentration power, but Shinzen's preferred mindfulness explicitly develops two additional skills: sensory clarity and equanimity ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 2 - The Most Fundamental Skill]] S3). The chapter then defines complete sensory experience as experience met with extraordinary concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 2 - The Most Fundamental Skill]] S8).

The Basic Mindfulness introduction makes the same triad the manual's root definition: mindful awareness is three attentional skills working together ([[Basic Mindfulness Introduction]] S2). It gives compact practice definitions: concentration stays with what is relevant, clarity tracks actual sensory experience, and equanimity allows sensory experience to come and go without push or pull ([[Basic Mindfulness Introduction]] S3). This turns CCE from an explanatory framework into the training target for every manual focus option.

Chapter 4 makes the triad more usable:

- [[Concentration Power]] stabilizes attention and supports the calming side of practice through a concentration-calm feedback loop ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 4 - Calming and Clarifying]] S2).
- [[Sensory Clarity]] separates complex experience into sensory components, tracks their interactions, and supports insight ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 4 - Calming and Clarifying]] S5).
- [[Equanimity]] lets sensory events expand and contract without craving, aversion, or interference ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 4 - Calming and Clarifying]] S8).

The triad therefore has two roles. It differentiates Shinzen's mindfulness from concentration-only training, and it supplies the conditions under which ordinary sensory events become complete experiences.

Chapter 5 gives the triad its compact transformation formula. Shinzen states that concentration, sensory clarity, equanimity, and time catalyze [[Insight and Purification]] ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 5 - Insight and Purification]] S10). This means the triad is not merely a taxonomy of attention skills. It is the input side of a process that is supposed to change paradigm, untangle suffering, and metabolize limiting patterns.

[[Discrimination and Unification - Part 1 of 4]] adds a compact oral image:
CCE is the digestive power applied to moment-by-moment experience. To the
extent concentration, clarity, and equanimity are present, experience is
digested; at the extreme, it breaks into Flow and can assimilate present
experience or re-digest stored holdings ([[Discrimination and Unification -
Part 1 of 4]] S3-S6).

[[Discrimination and Unification - Part 4 of 4]] adds a complementary skill
warning. If unification makes a practitioner unwilling to track ordinary
sensory objects, the triad has lost clarity. Healthy oneness should alternate
with healthy discrimination so experience remains trackable, tractable, and
manageable rather than fused into overwhelm ([[Discrimination and Unification
- Part 4 of 4]] S3-S8).

[[Growth and Tastes of Concentration, Sensory Clarity and Equanimity]] adds
the clearest oral growth-and-reward model for the triad. Shinzen says all
three factors grow on average over months, years, and decades, but local
practice still shows peaks and valleys. A specific event can temporarily
receive a critical mass of concentration, clarity, and equanimity so that it
completes as it arises and never constellates into a thing. Each factor also
has a distinct taste: concentration's in-the-zone fulfillment, equanimity's
release and future-suffering reduction flavor, and clarity's unmixing,
resolution, subtle-detection, and immediate-detection flavor ([[Growth and
Tastes of Concentration, Sensory Clarity and Equanimity]] S1-S8).

Chapter 7 stress-tests the triad in the [[Intermediate Realm]]. Shinzen says CCE is the vehicle through ordinary surface experience, altered power phenomena, and the path toward Source. Nothing special needs to happen, but if frightening, blissful, visionary, or empowering phenomena arise, the mature response is the same: meet them with concentration, clarity, and equanimity rather than craving, fearing, or comparing them ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 7 - The Realm of Power]] S6-S8, S11-S12).

The manual adds a routing role. [[Basic Mindfulness Practice Architecture]] asks the practitioner to train the same triad through contrasting Ways and techniques. [[Noting]] operationalizes the triad by acknowledging a sensory event, focusing on it, and optionally labeling it ([[Basic Mindfulness Introduction]] S8-S9). The first two Ways show the triad applied to inner image-talk-emotion and outer sight-sound-body fields. Chapter 3 applies it to rest and effort regulation through [[Focus on Rest]] and [[Do Nothing]] ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 3 - The Way of Tranquility]] S1-S11). Chapter 4 applies it to impermanence through [[Flow]] and [[Expansion And Contraction]] ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 4 - The Way of Flow]] S1-S7). Chapter 5 applies concentration and clarity constructively through [[Nurture Positive]], while keeping goodness linked to behavior and service ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 5 - The Way of Human Goodness]] S1-S12).

Chapter 8 adds [[Gone]] as a concentrated test of all three skills. Detecting vanishings requires sensory clarity; staying with Goneness requires concentration; and not being captured by each new arising requires equanimity. Shinzen explicitly says those skills can turn micro-endings into macro-relief and create a positive feedback loop ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 8 - Five More Ways]] S6).

Chapter 11 makes the triad the long-term effect of maintained practice and links it to the fun cycle: practice is easier to sustain when the practitioner detects the distinct reward flavors of concentration, sensory clarity, and equanimity ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 11 - Life]] S4-S6). Chapter 12 gives the most compact final comparison: each skill has a definition, a "what it is not" boundary, surface and deep views, examples, and a reward taste ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S5-S8).

[[What Is Mindfulness]] gives the earlier version of the reward-flavor model. Concentration has the taste of focus, clarity the taste of vividness, and equanimity the taste of purification; Shinzen interprets those immediate rewards as a possible positive-feedback loop that strengthens trait-level mindfulness ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S17).

The Growth and Tastes talk makes that reward model more practice-diagnostic
than the article's compact card. Reward taste is not only motivation; it is a
way to recognize which member of the triad is present. The same source keeps
the model non-linear: brief critical-mass moments can occur before baseline
CCE is stable, while long-term development can still have a hockey-stick arc
with ordinary fluctuations.

The same article is also the clearest measurement caution. Shinzen treats mindful awareness as vector-like rather than scalar and suggests future operational measures for attention, sensory resolution, event detection, and non-stickiness, but he says current CCE quantification is not yet rigorous and that the three skills overlap rather than being fully independent ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S4, S17, S24).

## Boundaries
This page is an architecture page, not a measurement model. The sources do not say how much concentration, clarity, or equanimity is enough, how to score the skills, or how to reliably detect imbalance without teacher or behavioral feedback.

The beginner FAQ's "threshold of intensity" language should therefore stay source-attributed. It confirms that Shinzen talks about CCE as a baseline and threshold phenomenon, but it does not define a measurable cutoff, a safe intensity rule, or a method-selection protocol ([[Beginner FAQs - Why Are We Doing This Why Meditate]] S5).

The SHF claim that proper labeling provides enough CCE for effective noting should be read as a practical training claim, not as proof that any verbally labelled session is balanced mindfulness. Labels can stabilize acknowledgement and focus, and strongly spoken labels can help recover a basic feedback loop when attention is being lost ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - CCE and Labeling as Skill Support]] S9). They do not remove the need to check clarity, equanimity, behavior, and safety.

The Part 1 talk makes the same boundary explicit from the other side: options can help CCE, but limited control over CCE is normal because these are not already-mastered skills ([[How to do Labeling and Noting During Meditation, 1 of 2 Parts]] S4). Strong labels, an equanimous voice, or repeat-until-Gone are therefore supports for practice, not proof that the whole triad is balanced.

The forcing-labels talk extends that boundary. A forced label stream may make hidden scatter trackable, but agitation and hard work do not prove that CCE is balanced or that continuing is safe. Strong labels remain a support condition that needs monitoring for clarity, noninterference, shame, and strain ([[Forcing Spoken Labels]] S1-S8).

The manual also makes the triad's accessibility double-edged. It says one or two focus options may be enough if they work, but it also admits that the system's large option set can overwhelm ([[Basic Mindfulness Introduction]] S1). Chapters 3-5 add more options and a few routing cues, but future practice routing still needs selection criteria, not just definitions of the skills.

The welcome talk's "anyone can develop" formulation should stay encouraging
and source-attributed. It supports Shinzen's public accessibility frame, but
it does not supply criteria for disability, trauma, psychiatric risk, teacher
competence, dosage, or practice worsening ([[Shinzen Young's Welcome to New
Viewers]] S1).

The triad also should not be reduced to a slogan. Concentration without clarity can stagnate as pleasant calm. Clarity without equanimity can become intense analysis or agitation. Equanimity without clarity can be confused with vagueness, numbness, or indifference. Chapter 5 adds that meditation may need psychotherapy, recovery work, social feedback, and explicit ethics when the triad is not enough by itself ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 5 - Insight and Purification]] S12). Those risks route to [[Complete Experience Safety Boundary]].

The definitional article adds that mindfulness should not be equated with unrestricted focus range, just awareness, MBSR, Noting, or labeling. It also says a comprehensive system may be enough for some people and not enough for others, leaving room for therapy, counseling, recovery work, religious belief, and medication ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S20, S23).

Chapter 7 adds that CCE with altered phenomena is not a complete safety protocol. The same chapter names illness, trauma, sleep deprivation, drugs, fasting, and chance as possible conditions for intermediate-realm descent, so CCE language should not bypass clinical, medical, sleep, substance, or support assessment when phenomena become risky ([[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 7 - The Realm of Power]] S11).

Chapter 12 sharpens the same boundary in positive terms. The reward taste of a skill is not proof that the whole triad is balanced. Enjoying focus, crispness, or release should still be checked against behavior, service, and support.

## Implications
Practice routing should avoid treating deeper concentration as sufficient by itself. If a practitioner becomes focused but unclear, rigid, dissociated, or ethically indifferent, the framework implies that the triad is incomplete.

The triad also explains the chapter 4 complementarity between samatha and vipassana. Calm can support clarification, and clarification can deconstruct the blockages that prevent calm ([[Calming-Clarifying Balance]]).

For Shinzen's own terminology, vipassana and mindfulness can be treated as
practical synonyms only after the triad is present. Without concentration,
the separated strand cannot be held; without clarity, the strand is not
separated or penetrated; without equanimity, seeing-through can become
strain, aversion, or forcing ([[Vipassana and Mindfulness]] S7-S8).
