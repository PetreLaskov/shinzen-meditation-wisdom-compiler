This page is a translation, not a claim that Shinzen is doing academic moral
philosophy. If his teachings are translated into philosophy-department
ethics, the best fit is a practice-based eudaimonist virtue ethic, checked by
pragmatic consequences for suffering, behavior, love, feedback, and service.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: The system asks what kind of person and perception are
  trained by practice, then tests that training by reduced suffering,
  increased fulfillment, positive behavior, love, and service.
- **Closest academic family**: Eudaimonist virtue ethics, with Buddhist
  service and care elements. The central virtues are not only traits such as
  compassion and humility, but trainable capacities: concentration, sensory
  clarity, equanimity, responsiveness, feedback openness, and goodness.
- **Consequentialist check**: The system is not pure consequentialism, but it
  repeatedly asks whether practice actually improves suffering, conduct,
  relationships, and service.
- **Not deontology-first**: Rules and precepts appear as guidelines and
  boundaries, especially around teacher conduct, consent, and harm. They are
  not the main explanatory engine of the path.
- **Source posture**: Probable as an inference from compiled pages; not an
  explicit Shinzen-authored ethical theory.

## Philosophical Translation

The nearest normative category is **eudaimonist virtue ethics**. The good
life is not defined only by obeying rules or maximizing an external outcome;
it is defined by the transformation of the practitioner into someone less
captured by suffering, selfing, compulsion, and distorted perception, and
more capable of fulfillment, love, responsiveness, and service. [[Total
Happiness]] names that aim as surface and deep happiness for self and others,
while [[Way of Human Goodness]] makes the reconstructive side explicit:
practice should build wholesome affect, behavior, cognition, ideals, and
service.

The difference from many classical virtue-ethics presentations is that
Shinzen's virtues are not mainly character labels. They are trained
attentional and phenomenological capacities. Concentration, sensory clarity,
and equanimity change what the practitioner can perceive and tolerate;
positive practice reconstructs Feel/Image/Talk patterns; no-self work reduces
identity capture; and feedback openness keeps the whole process from becoming
private self-approval ([[Current Model]]; [[Way of Human Goodness]];
[[Spiritual Teachers' Behaviour Feedback and Ethics]] S2-S6).

The system also has a **pragmatic consequentialist test**, but not a pure
utilitarian one. Practice is judged by whether suffering decreases,
fulfillment increases, self-knowledge deepens, behavior improves, and love or
service grows in a reasonable time frame ([[Current Model]]; [[Basic
Mindfulness Introduction]] S2-S5). [[Total Happiness Behavior And Service
Test]] sharpens this: insight, Source feeling, no-self, and service intention
do not self-verify. Conduct must change, mistakes must learn from feedback,
and failed behavior change may need therapy, recovery support, friendship,
or other accountability ([[Mindfulness and Behavioural Change]] S1-S7).

It is **not primarily deontological**. Shinzen does not appear, in the
compiled model, to ground ethics in a universal law, duty procedure, divine
command, or rule hierarchy. Precepts and ethical guidelines matter, but
[[Spiritual Teachers' Behaviour Feedback and Ethics]] treats ethical
cultivation mainly as openness to feedback from other human beings, while
[[Guidance Scope and Accountability Boundary]] translates that into consent,
role clarity, referral, protection, and accountability.

It also is **not Source-based moral realism**. [[Source And Service
Boundary]] is explicit that Source contact may incline love, kinship, and
service, but does not verify conduct, consent, repair, teaching authority, or
beneficial action. [[How a Positive Human Being Emerges from the Source]]
adds the important qualifier: the impersonal Source is not itself love or
hate; the human surface should return as ordinary, approachable helpfulness.

The moral psychology is perhaps the strongest part of the implied ethics.
Bad action is often understood as tangled, incomplete, or coercive sensory
process: pain, craving, fear, image, talk, and body emotion fuse into
distorted perception and driven behavior. Ethical cultivation therefore
requires perceptual training, affective digestion, positive reconstruction,
behavioral support, and social feedback, not merely stronger intention
([[Suffering Distortion Cycle]]; [[Mindfulness and Behavioural Change]]
S2-S7; [[Deconstruction-Reconstruction Balance]]).

## Short Answer

In philosophy-department shorthand:

**Shinzen's implied ethics is a contemplative eudaimonist virtue ethic, with
pragmatist consequential checks and a bodhisattva/service orientation.**

It asks the practitioner to become the kind of person whose perception is
less distorted by selfing and suffering, whose affective life can be
completed and reconstructed, and whose realization is tested by ordinary
behavior, love, feedback, consent, competence, and service.

## Boundaries

Do not overclean the inference. Shinzen's system gives a strong moral
psychology and practice-based formation model, but it does not supply a full
academic ethics: no complete theory of justice, rights, distribution,
political obligation, punishment, institutions, or moral epistemology.

Do not treat "service" as sufficient. Service intention can be sincere and
still lack skill, consent, strategy, or ordinary expertise. [[Total Happiness
Behavior And Service Test]] and [[Guidance Scope and Accountability
Boundary]] govern before any Source, no-self, or compassion claim becomes a
reason to act.

Do not treat deep happiness as passivity. [[Total Happiness]] preserves
ordinary surface happiness and the rule that conditions should be changed
when they can feasibly be changed. Condition-independent fulfillment does
not justify ignoring medicine, safety, relationship repair, social action, or
ordinary needs.
