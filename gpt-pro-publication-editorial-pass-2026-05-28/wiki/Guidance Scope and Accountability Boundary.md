This page owns the interpersonal and institutional side of safety. It asks what must surround Shinzen-style practice so guidance, behavior-change claims, crisis routing, service language, ritual adjacency, teacher opinions, canon appeals, wording effects, and transmission do not outrun competence, consent, support, dialogue, independence, or accountability.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Practice depth and good intention do not remove the need for feedback channels, referral, behavior verification, consent, wording correction, anti-dependency, dialogue, and outside support.
- **Why this matters**: The system is built for live routing, but live routing becomes risky when teacher authority, crisis, altered phenomena, clinical material, or claimed service enters the room.
- **Key tensions**: Compiled sources explicitly value guidance and outside support, but do not yet define guide qualifications, referral thresholds, grievance structures, anti-retaliation protection, or power-service safeguards.
- **Source posture**: Strong as a Shinzen-internal accountability frontier; incomplete as governance or clinical guidance.

## Role Ladder

For a live request, identify the highest role actually warranted:

- **Ordinary support**: listening, encouragement, practical help, and
  suggesting the person contact an appropriate helper.
- **Practice reminder**: naming CCE, labels, effort, or method fit when the
  situation is green and consent is clear.
- **Shinzen-style coaching**: branch-and-loop guidance with follow-up,
  behavior checks, and willingness to change the plan.
- **Terrain-specific support**: no-self, void, bhanga, altered phenomena,
  trauma-adjacent intensity, or retreat aftershock support from someone with
  relevant lived and teaching competence.
- **Qualified care or protection**: psychotherapy, psychiatry, medical care,
  recovery support, legal/organizational protection, emergency support, or
  ritual-lineage competence when ordinary risk exceeds meditation scope.

When roles conflict, the stricter ordinary-care role governs first. A teacher
can remain helpful while explicitly declining authority outside their lane.

## Stop, Refer, Or Protect Criteria

Stop optimizing meditation and prioritize qualified support when any of these
are active: self-harm or harm risk, abuse/coercion/retaliation risk, medical
danger, medication or treatment decisions, severe dissociation or DPDR
distress, psychosis/mania-like instability, loss of functioning, unsafe sleep
deprivation, trauma flooding, teacher misconduct, sexual or financial power
pressure, legal risk, or practice language replacing needed care.

Simplify or pause practice before referral is clearly mandatory when there is
repeated worsening, unclear method fit, behavior mismatch, strong shame or
pressure, destabilizing no-self/Gone/Source language, or insufficient support.
The next move is context, consent, support, and role clarity before more map.

## Evidence Map
[[Practice Guidance Toolkit]] gives the base helping model. Guidance includes rapport, client-world analysis, strategy selection, focus-method teaching, behavior commitments, follow-up, and method/commitment revision. The client chooses where to start, and behavior commitments are part of the process ([[Basic Mindfulness Chapter 12 - The Big Picture]] S10-S13).

[[What Are Your Specialties as a Teacher]] and the session-setup sources give the live-coaching model. Shinzen's system is optimized for branch-and-loop interaction: give a technique, ask what happened, then keep, modify, redirect, offer options, or add information. This makes guidance responsive, but it also means the guide's competence and consent boundaries matter ([[What Are Your Specialties as a Teacher]] S4-S5; [[Three Ways to Set Up Your Basic Mindfulness Session]] S6-S7).

[[How Do I Know Which Kind of Meditation Is Best for Me]] adds a beginner
method-assignment norm. Shinzen says systems differ in whether they vary
objects by an algorithm or keep returning to one object, and that the teacher
should be willing and able to explain why a student is asked to practice in a
particular way. This does not validate the assignment by itself, but it makes
explainability part of accountable guidance ([[How Do I Know Which Kind of
Meditation Is Best for Me]] S6).

[[Authority, Opinions and the Buddhist Canon]] adds a dialogue-specific
authority boundary. Shinzen says his own retreat teaching usually requires
clarifying back-and-forth because he often needs to know what the student
means before answering; teachers and scripture can point, but they should not
be inflated into pronouncements beyond direct experience, logical inference,
and available dialogue ([[Authority, Opinions and the Buddhist Canon]]
S3-S8).

[[Say What You Mean]] adds the wording-impact version of the same boundary.
Shinzen says a person can have a reasonable idea internally and still fail to
say it accurately, then applies the point to himself after a
political/cultural comment sounded dismissive despite his intended
inclusivity. For guidance accountability, the relevant test is not only what
the teacher meant, but whether the spoken words can be corrected when they
land otherwise ([[Say What You Mean]] S1-S6).

[[The Use of Woo Woo Words by Enlightened Masters]] adds the incentive and
anti-cynicism version of the teacher-language boundary. Shinzen says unusual
mystical words can help or obstruct, acknowledges that teacher motivations
may include maintaining student or donation streams, and also reports that
practice later made some strange master-language intelligible to him
([[The Use of Woo Woo Words by Enlightened Masters]] S1-S5). Guidance should
therefore neither revere nor reflexively dismiss such language; it should
check practice function, incentives, consent, dependency, and feedback.

[[On Rites, Rituals, and Ceremonies]] adds a consent-and-authority boundary
for retreat form itself. Shinzen says retreatants will not have to bow to him
or participate in rites, rituals, or ceremonies, and he prefers being treated
as ordinary enough to challenge or disagree with ([[On Rites, Rituals, and
Ceremonies]] S1-S3). When he later allows sweat lodge as an optional ancient
ritual, the practice rationale is CCE development, not institutional
obedience or required devotion ([[On Rites, Rituals, and Ceremonies]] S7-S9).

[[Five Basic Assumptions in Mindfulness Practice]] gives a simple fallback: if a practitioner forgets concentration, clarity, equanimity, or reaction recycling, they should contact a competent guide who can remind them of the first four axioms. The source strengthens the support boundary while leaving competence undefined ([[Five Basic Assumptions in Mindfulness Practice]] S6).

[[What Is Mindfulness]] and [[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 5 - Insight and Purification]] explicitly limit sufficiency. Mindfulness can complement therapy, and some cases may require mental health professionals, recovery programs, social feedback, explicit ethical guidelines, religious belief, counseling, or psychotropic medication ([[What Is Mindfulness]] S20, S23; [[Science of Enlightenment Chapter 5 - Insight and Purification]] S12).

[[Mindfulness & Psychotherapy]] adds the scale-based collaboration frame.
Shinzen says psychotherapy and mindfulness share ideals such as awareness,
release from inappropriate holding, non-conflict, and insight, but work at
different resolution levels. Therapy gives the gross anatomy or
building-level structure of personality and behavior, while mindfulness applies
those ideals at fine-grained second-by-second sensory scale and intensity
([[Mindfulness & Psychotherapy]] S4-S8). This is a complementarity claim, not
a clinical protocol or permission for meditation teachers to replace therapy.

[[Strengthening a Weak Ego Structure Through Mindfulness]] adds a more
specific clinical-adjacent scope boundary. Shinzen says weak ego structure
does not automatically rule out meditation, but the practice type and
orientation matter: when another person's sight, sound, or touch triggers
dysfunctional loss of self-contact, the guide may use Feel/Image/Talk to
strengthen ego boundaries rather than dissolve selfing
([[Strengthening a Weak Ego Structure Through Mindfulness]] S1-S5). Because
the source names diagnosis-like territory without assessment criteria, a
guide should keep therapy, diagnosis, trauma, dissociation, impairment, and
relational-safety issues outside meditation-teacher authority.

[[How to Do Healthy Merging]] adds the relational Source-contact boundary.
Shinzen says healthy merging should not make the other person feel invaded
or weirded out, should be understood and controlled, and should leave
empowerment or purification rather than disempowerment or identification
with harmful dynamics ([[How to Do Healthy Merging]] S1-S7). For guidance,
this means "merging" is not self-validating: ordinary impact, feedback,
relational repair, and clinical-adjacent support remain governing checks.

[[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 1 of 3]] adds the medical version of scope
clarity. In health crisis, Shinzen distinguishes meditation intended to
influence the objective course of healing from meditation intended to reduce
suffering; he says both paradigms are valid, but his go-to teaching is
suffering reduction ([[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 1 of 3]] S3-S8). A
guide should therefore not convert meditation guidance into oncology advice,
cure promises, or claims that the patient's emotions caused the disease.
[[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 2 of 3]] adds a further role boundary:
the guide may help the practitioner parse the body-mind sensory challenge
and use non-suppressive focus-away practice, but the same source still leaves
medical treatment, symptom escalation, prognosis, and cancer-causation claims
outside meditation-teacher authority ([[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 2 of
3]] S1-S9). [[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 3 of 3]] adds that healthy
focus-on practice requires actual training: the guide can explain the theory
of separating physical body, emotional body, visual thought, and auditory
thought and reducing resistance through CCE, but should not present that map
as a quick substitute for guided exercises, medical care, or clinical support
([[Mindfulness, Cancer & Healing - 3 of 3]] S1-S9).

[[How to Guide Someone through the Death Process using Mindfulness]] adds the
end-of-life guidance edge. Shinzen's first move is not a technique; it is to
ask whether the person is still oriented toward survival or is ready to orient
toward dying, and to introduce the topic through a life-threatening situation
rather than by declaring the person already dying. His second move is advance
consent for possible one-way guidance when weakness, medication, or the dying
process removes normal feedback ([[How to Guide Someone through the Death
Process using Mindfulness]] S4-S5). Rest, Flow, and Gone are then queried as
available doors, not imposed as beliefs or replacements for medical,
palliative, family, legal, or religious care ([[How to Guide Someone through
the Death Process using Mindfulness]] S6-S9).

[[A Mindful Birth and Zen Parenting, Part 1 of 2]] adds a childbirth version
of the same role boundary. Shinzen gives one coaching strategy for an already-
trained practitioner: use contraction peaks as Touch expansion while Image,
Talk, and emotional selfing contract into Rest, then relax during valleys
([[A Mindful Birth and Zen Parenting, Part 1 of 2]] S1-S9). A guide can use
that as sensory practice language, but should not convert it into obstetric
advice, pressure against pain relief, or authority over birth-plan,
complication, fetal-monitoring, clinician, or trauma-support decisions.

[[A Mindful Birth and Zen Parenting, Part 2 of 2]] adds the parenting version.
Shinzen's baby-as-Roshi metaphor can help a parent treat touch, sight, sound,
and infant distress as practice material, and the student's raw-fear report
can increase compassion for crying or upset states ([[A Mindful Birth and Zen
Parenting, Part 2 of 2]] S4-S9). A guide should not convert that metaphor
into pediatric, attachment, postpartum mental-health, sleep, family-system,
or developmental authority.

[[Sleep Interruption & A Good Night's Rest]] adds the sleep version of the
same role boundary. Shinzen gives a practice instruction for disturbed sleep:
lie still so the body rests, keep a continuous technique going, and reframe
the goal as a good night's rest rather than demanded unconscious sleep
([[Sleep Interruption & A Good Night's Rest]] S5-S8). A guide can use that as
practice support, but should not convert it into sleep-medicine, psychiatry,
neurology, medication, substance, pain, trauma, or chronic-insomnia authority.

[[Tea, Coffee and Meditation]] adds the caffeine/substance version. Shinzen
says a stimulant is not automatically incompatible with meditation when it
does not make the practitioner jangled, because meditation also has a
clarity-alertness component ([[Tea, Coffee and Meditation]] S2-S4). A guide
can use that as a practice-fit question, but should not convert it into
medical, medication, sleep, anxiety, mania, cardiovascular, pregnancy,
addiction/recovery, or dosage advice.

[[Do You Think Sex is Dirty]] adds the sexuality version of the same role
boundary. Shinzen gives a practice interpretation of sexual pleasure:
craving and unconsciousness obscure the experience, while established CCE
can let lovemaking become pleasure meditation and a window into
Feel/Image/Talk self-dissolution ([[Do You Think Sex is Dirty]] S1-S9). A
guide can cite that as source-level practice framing, but should not convert
it into relationship advice, sex therapy, sexual-health advice, tantric
authorization, teacher-led sexual practice, or permission to bypass consent,
ordinary ethics, power dynamics, trauma care, compulsion treatment, or
partner communication.

[[Mindfulness and Behavioural Change]] adds a behavior-specific version of
the same support boundary. Shinzen first teaches sensory deconstruction of
urges through Touch, Feel, Image, and Talk, but then says mindfulness alone
is not guaranteed to achieve the desired behavior change. If the first
principle is insufficient, he says to add an external accountability
structure such as counseling, therapy, a twelve-step program, a sponsor, a
friend, or another outside support giving manageable assignments
([[Mindfulness and Behavioural Change]] S2-S7).

[[The Native American Sweat Lodge Ceremony - Part 1 of 2]] adds a ritual
adjacency competence boundary. Shinzen says sweat lodge participation around
his retreats is optional and led by named Native leaders, then explicitly
distinguishes traditional sweats run by competently trained Native people
from incompetent non-Native-led sweats ([[The Native American Sweat Lodge
Ceremony - Part 1 of 2]] S1-S6). This is not a general meditation-teacher
qualification claim; it shows that when Shinzen brings practitioners near a
non-Buddhist ritual, the governing competence may belong to the ritual's own
trained lineage holders rather than to the meditation teacher.

[[The Native American Sweat Lodge - Part 2 of 2]] adds a containment boundary
inside that ritual adjacency. Shinzen says the ceremony can involve lowered
defenses, speaking, crying, group affirmation, altered states, strong
physical and emotional sensations, and communal support ([[The Native
American Sweat Lodge - Part 2 of 2]] S2-S8). Guidance questions therefore
include not only who can lead the ceremony traditionally, but how consent,
privacy, emotional vulnerability, heat risk, social pressure, and debriefing
are handled.

[[Towards a Balanced Enlightenment]] provides the clearest teacher-accountability source. Shinzen says enlightenment is a vector, not a scalar: development in one component does not prove balanced judgment, behavior, relationship, or organizational competence. Precepts, deconstructing negative urges, Nurture Positive, feedback from everyone, psychotherapy, 12-step programs, peer correction, and senior-teacher dialogue may be needed when Buddhist practice is not enough ([[Towards a Balanced Enlightenment]] S4-S11).

The same talk supplies a concrete caution from Shinzen's own behavior: feedback from students and peers over months was needed to shake him out of a high-handed or manipulative pattern. This makes behavior feedback a system requirement, not an optional nicety ([[Towards a Balanced Enlightenment]] S9).

[[Classical Enlightenment Healing the World and Screw-ups]] adds the
shortest misconduct boundary. Shinzen says people who have seen no-self can
still have major public screw-ups, including stream-entry-level people he
knows personally. He frames many teacher failures as ordinary human
patterns, but that framing only strengthens the need for ordinary
accountability structures; it does not define repair or protection criteria
([[Classical Enlightenment Healing the World and Screw-ups]] S7-S8).

[[Meditation Teacher's Qualifications and Liberation Experiences]] adds a
qualification boundary. Shinzen says liberation experience and teaching
qualification are not equivalent, body language is uncertain and can mislead,
ordinary behavior requires long close observation, and student results are
the main practical evidence for teaching ability ([[Meditation Teacher's
Qualifications and Liberation Experiences]] S1-S5).

[[How Do I Find a Good Meditation Teacher]] adds the positive selection side
of that boundary. Shinzen recommends seeking a teacher who can handle
classical enlightenment and occasional associated pathology-adjacent issues
when such a teacher is available and relatable; if not, the seeker should
step down through professional, paraprofessional, descriptive, or subtle
teaching while keeping the lower role clear ([[How Do I Find a Good
Meditation Teacher]] S4-S5). This helps the boundary avoid two errors:
requiring rare master-level support for every situation, or treating subtle
presence and coherent description as if they were explicit monitored
guidance.

[[Pros and Cons of Dharma Maps]] adds a map-independent teacher-test
boundary. Shinzen says he would rather a practitioner study with a teacher
whose perspective differs from his but who has a track record in leading
people to significant transformation than with a map-agreeing teacher who
lacks such a record ([[Pros and Cons of Dharma Maps]] S14). That strengthens
student-result evidence, but it does not define how to count harms,
dependency, selection effects, misconduct, adverse outcomes, or suppressed
feedback; those remain accountability questions.

[[Spiritual Teachers' Behaviour Feedback and Ethics]] adds a feedback-channel
boundary. Shinzen says teacher blind spots are likely when feedback is
blocked by pedestal structures, when teachers refuse continued senior
oversight, or when family and non-meditator feedback is dismissed. His
ethics posture is not mainly legalistic: basic precepts are guidelines, but
ethical cultivation depends on openness to human feedback and on consensus
patterns that reveal blind spots over time ([[Spiritual Teachers' Behaviour
Feedback and Ethics]] S2-S6).

[[Shinzen Young Day-Long Retreat at the Monastic Academy - July 22, 2017]]
adds a quality-C retreat-stream confirmation in response to a question about
meditation-community authority problems. Shinzen distinguishes practice
supports from structures: deconstructing negative urges and nurturing
positive ones are practice components, while explicit ethical guidelines,
open feedback channels, behaviorally oriented accountability/support
structures, and realistic role models are separate safeguards. Because the
transcript is quality `C`, this should be used as broad routing support, not
as an exact policy quote ([[Shinzen Young Day-Long Retreat at the Monastic
Academy - July 22, 2017]] S14).

[[Teacher's or Student's Issues Impeding Spiritual Progress]] adds a
dependency boundary. Shinzen says dependence on the teacher is the main
teacher-student issue to watch, whether teacher-induced or student-generated.
The intended result of liberation teaching is independent practitioners,
colleagues, and peers who can take the principles and skill set onward and
apply any transmission under real conditions ([[Teacher's or Student's Issues
Impeding Spiritual Progress]] S1-S6).

[[Practice Cycles]] and [[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness Retreat]] add ongoing support. SHF recommends yearly competent coach check-ins and retreat or equivalent; post-retreat aftershock should route to guide contact when activation blocks recall or application, while daily-life behavior remains the gold standard ([[See Hear Feel Introduction - Practice Organization and System Transition]] S1-S3; [[What to Expect and Do After a Mindfulness Retreat]] S1-S8).

[[Maximizing Psycho-Spiritual Growth with an Algorithmic Approach (Windows & Walls)]] gives the crisis frontier. Severe life events can be treated as monastery-like intensity when guided well, but the current source does not define emergency, clinical, legal, consent, confidentiality, facilitator-competence, or ordinary-repair criteria ([[Maximizing Psycho-Spiritual Growth with an Algorithmic Approach (Windows & Walls)]] S3-S6).

[[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 2 of 3]] adds a
dissolution-specific competence claim. Shinzen says that for a severe process
like the student's, the most important support is someone who has personally
been through it and come out the other end. This strengthens the need for
terrain-specific guidance, while leaving unresolved how lived experience,
clinical training, referral judgment, supervision, and accountability should
combine ([[Experiences of the Dissolution (Bhanga) Process - 2 of 3]] S3-S4).

[[Enlightenment DPDR and Falling Into the Pit of the Void]] adds a void-side
competence boundary. Shinzen says meditation-linked movement toward DPDR-like
distress is very rare under competent guidance and reports usually being
able to re-route meditators through the right direction. That claim depends
on senior competence and does not extend to unsupported self-treatment or to
spontaneous DPDR in the general population ([[Enlightenment DPDR and Falling
Into the Pit of the Void]] S4-S6).

[[Strengthening a Weak Ego Structure Through Mindfulness]] adds that
competence may also require recognizing when the apparent contemplative
movement toward self-loss is actually weak self-contact or boundary collapse.
The guide's task is then not more dissolution, but self-strand contact,
function, support, and referral humility
([[Strengthening a Weak Ego Structure Through Mindfulness]] S2-S5).

[[Classic Dark Night or Clinical Issues]] adds the clearest referral boundary
so far. Shinzen says he may ask questions and make an informal guess about
whether apparent dark-night distress is fully practice-based, but he usually
suspects ordinary depression, anxiety, or clinical biochemical factors may be
involved. He explicitly refuses to advise people to take medication because
he is not qualified, while strongly encouraging competent mental-health
evaluation and openness to standard treatment when clinical factors may be
present ([[Classic Dark Night or Clinical Issues]] S1-S7).

## Boundary Question Clusters
- **Role and competence**: what makes a guide competent for beginner practice, Do Nothing, pain, trauma, retreat aftershock, no-self distress, bhanga, altered phenomena, or crisis-as-monastery framing; when should the guide simplify, switch, stop, ground, refer, or protect ordinary action?
- **Ritual and retreat form**: who is competent for an optional ritual, what authorization they hold, and how bowing, ceremony, heat, disclosure, medical screening, privacy, social pressure, debriefing, and follow-up stay genuinely optional and contained.
- **Teacher authority and outcomes**: when an answer is tested through direct experience, inference, and dialogue rather than received as authority; what counts as a real student result; how adverse outcomes, harmed students, dependency, and power imbalance weigh against apparent success.
- **Mystical language and incentives**: when strange or special vocabulary
  helps practice and when it obscures accountability, centralizes the
  teacher, sustains a payment/loyalty stream, or pressures students to defer.
- **Feedback and misconduct**: what channels let students, senior teachers, peers, family, children, non-practitioners, and community members correct a teacher without fear; how communities distinguish wording corrections, projection-heavy criticism, consensus patterns, and misconduct without relying on realization claims.
- **Clinical and combined-care scope**: when DPDR-like void distress, depression, anxiety, paralysis, burnout, weak self-contact, unhealthy merging, trauma flooding, medication questions, or loss of functioning shifts the governing need from meditation instruction to therapy, psychiatry, medical care, emergency support, or another qualified-care branch.
- **Applied-life domains**: in serious illness, dying, childbirth, parenting, sleep disruption, sexuality, stimulants, or recovery-adjacent behavior, who owns medical decisions, consent, treatment coordination, ordinary support, symptom escalation, relationship ethics, legality, and clinical care?
- **Death and life-threatening care**: how death-process guidance should coordinate with survival-oriented treatment, hospice or palliative care, consent capacity, family systems, religion, grief, privacy, medication, delirium, and one-way guidance.
- **Behavior and accountability**: how behavior commitments are chosen, tracked, revised, and protected from coercion or shame; when outside accountability is enough and when therapy, recovery support, medical care, legal support, or protection must govern.
- **Power, Source, and service**: what consent, verification, supervision, referral, behavior-verification, and grievance norms are required before claimed power, Source-based care, healthy merging, or service language is used to help others.

## Evidence Needed
- **Competence by domain**: criteria for Noting, Do Nothing, pain, emotion, trauma, retreat aftershock, no-self fear, Gone, bhanga, Source, altered phenomena, and crisis practice.
- **Referral and combined care**: thresholds for DPDR, derealization, nihilistic void distress, loss of functioning, panic, suicidality, psychosis risk, trauma shutdown, depression, medication conversations, therapy, recovery, medical care, psychiatry, legal protection, organizational governance, and emergency support.
- **Weak ego and boundary loss**: criteria distinguishing healthy no-self, healthy merging, ordinary intimacy, dysfunctional self-loss, dissociation, trauma activation, personality-structure concerns, and Feel/Image/Talk self-contact that is supportive but insufficient.
- **Applied medical and life domains**: criteria for illness, objective-healing intentions, suffering reduction, focus-away, trained focus-on, dying, childbirth, parenting, sleep, sexuality, stimulants, treatment adherence, symptom escalation, partner autonomy, and avoidance of cure promises, patient blame, or role confusion.
- **Death-process guidance**: criteria for survival-versus-dying orientation, consent capacity, one-way guidance, palliative coordination, spiritual/religious preference, family involvement, privacy, medication, delirium, and grief support.
- **Ritual and retreat consent**: criteria for authorization, medical and heat-risk screening, emergency planning, cultural protocol, debriefing, optional bowing/chanting/ceremony, implicit pressure, and non-transfer of permission to imitate.
- **Behavior change and verification**: standards for behavior commitments, addiction-adjacent escalation, Total Happiness, purification, service, teacher conduct, and "practice is working" claims.
- **Teacher accountability**: standards for genuine student results versus charisma, selection effects, survivorship bias, community pressure, or suppressed harm; peer review, grievance process, supervision, anti-retaliation, family/non-practitioner input, dependency signs, public correction of misstatements, and misconduct response independent of realization claims.
- **Teacher track record**: how to weigh significant student transformation
  against adverse outcomes, dependency, power imbalance, selection effects,
  map agreement, charisma, and misconduct history.
- **Program validity**: criteria for distinguishing real mindfulness systems from shallow offerings: definition quality, trait-training aim, instructor practice, safety disclosure, referral capacity, and behavior-verification norms.
