Turn Toward and Turn Away names the Gate 2 strategy fork in Shinzen's live guidance. The durable point is not that direct contact is always superior: guidance chooses among direct CCE, background equanimity, Flow, Rest, positive Feel, and ordinary support according to what is actually workable.

## Key Points
- **Core claim**: Turning toward means bringing concentration, clarity, and equanimity directly to a challenge; turning away means stabilizing attention elsewhere while the challenge is genuinely permitted in the background.
- **Why this matters**: It prevents Shinzen's pain and emotion practice from becoming a direct-contact mandate, while also preventing focus-away from becoming disguised suppression.
- **Key tensions**: The sources give partial criteria but not full safety rules for medical pain, trauma, dissociation, exposure pressure, teacher authority, or ordinary action.
- **Source posture**: Strong as a Gate 2 practice-routing synthesis; still incomplete as a clinical, medical, or teacher-scope protocol.

## Model
The physical-discomfort sequence gives the first direct-contact route. A practitioner need not start with the worst local pain; Shinzen breaks the challenge into local touch, global spread, and Feel/Image/Talk reactions, then recommends beginning with the weakest workable component before working up to the stronger field ([[Turn Towards Physical Discomfort Sequence & The Taste of Purification]] S1-S5).

The first interactive physical-discomfort talk makes the fork explicit. Shinzen distinguishes turning toward uncomfortable touch, turning away to a restful or positive object while allowing the discomfort, and focusing on change when the discomfort already presents as Flow or vanishings ([[Turn Towards, Turn Away, Focus on Flow w Physical Discomfort]] S2-S10).

The clearest turn-away criteria come from the sensory-challenges talk. Focus-away remains mindfulness when the challenge is permitted to arise in the background, there is no agenda to make it disappear, and the practitioner remains at least sometimes willing to turn toward it. Concentration and clarity stabilize the chosen object, while equanimity surrounds the unattended eruption ([[Using Turn Away and Background Equanimity with Sensory Challenges]] S5-S8).

Emotional practice follows the same fork but uses inner sensory grammar. Strong emotion may be turned toward by untangling Feel, Image, and Talk, alternating parts and wholes, then subdividing Feel by flavor, location, and radial sector when needed ([[Turn Towards Difficult Emotion and Challenging Feel-Image-Talk Eruptions - 1 of 2]] S1-S4; [[Turn Towards Difficult Emotion and Challenging Feel-Image-Talk Eruptions - 2 of 2]] S1-S4). Agitation, impatience, and restlessness may also be left in the background with equanimity while attention returns to the chosen object, or contacted directly as Feel when that is useful ([[Turn Towards, Turn Away Working with the Agitation Flavor in Meditation]] S1-S8).

Flow is a third branch, not a trophy state. When itch or discomfort is already popping, bubbling, pulsing, waving, tingling, expanding, or contracting, Shinzen may route practice into Focus on Flow, including label-gear adjustments and whole-body widening. If a subtle Flow branch is absent, the guide drops it rather than forcing it ([[Turn Towards Flow (Change) Using the Labeling Gears & Options]] S1-S8; [[Turn Towards the Soothing Flow of Poison Ivy, See Flow in Rest]] S2-S7; [[Hear-In to Mental Talk Space, Feel Flow in Body Space]] S1-S5).

Positive and Rest branches belong in the same routing map. A student may look for already-present pleasant emotional Feel without forcing it; if the report becomes calmness or emotional peace, Shinzen classifies that as Rest rather than positive Feel ([[Finding Feel Good in Emotional Body Space]] S1-S5). The turn-away branch may therefore stabilize in Rest or positive material, but the background challenge must not be denied.

## Boundaries
Turning toward can become flooding, compulsive subdivision, endurance pressure, teacher-led overexposure, or spiritualized pain. The weakest-link rule lowers this pressure but does not define medical, trauma, posture, panic, dissociation, self-harm, or referral thresholds.

Turning away can become avoidance, suppression, dissociation, medical neglect, Rest preference, or task avoidance. The strongest current internal criteria are permission, no disappearance agenda, and occasional willingness to turn toward; these are necessary but not sufficient safety rules.

Focusing on Flow can become Flow chasing, pleasantness bypass, divided-attention risk, or neglect of physical signals. The live talks repeatedly make Flow conditional: use it when it is detectable, drop the branch when it is absent, and do not force change into stable Rest or subtle thought space.

Intentional evocation marks the outer emotional limit. Shinzen allows occasionally striking the body-mind bell of known difficult material, then observing the Feel/Image/Talk resonance, but he rejects making negative evocation central because that becomes psychotherapy ([[Evoking and Working Through Challenging Material]] S2-S5).

Sticky letting-go states show that "turn away" is also a history of coping. Shinzen frames practice as replacing tightening-and-turning-away with opening-up-and-turning-toward, while noting that relaxation can temporarily expose vulnerable or disoriented intermediate states ([[Open Up and Turn Towards Challenging Letting Go States]] S1-S5). That reassurance still needs support criteria.

## Implications
Future guidance should ask:

- Is the live challenge best contacted directly, allowed in the background, routed through Flow, stabilized through Rest or positive Feel, or handled first through ordinary support?
- If turning toward, what is the weakest workable component?
- If turning away, is the challenge actually permitted without a disappearance agenda?
- If using Flow, is change clearly presenting, or is the guide or student imposing it?
- If the issue is emotional, is Feel/Image/Talk decomposition clarifying experience or becoming rumination, exposure, or overcontrol?
- What ordinary behavior, medical, relational, or support signal would override the meditation branch?
